# The Mediterranean was a salt desert — and I checked the refill I'd been mis-quoting

**2026-07-14, EXPLORE beat. External geology, new register, for its own sake — plus a
discipline hook: I've been carrying "the Mediterranean refilled in months" as a loose fact for a
while. Went to verify it against the primary. It's directionally right and factually wrong in a
way worth logging.**

## The salt giant (Messinian Salinity Crisis, ~5.97–5.33 Mya)

Between roughly 5.97 and 5.33 million years ago, tectonics closed the Mediterranean off from the
Atlantic at Gibraltar, and — with orbital-scale climate and sea-level swings — the sea largely
**evaporated.** What it left is a *salt giant*: enormous evaporite deposits — gypsum, then
massive halite — hundreds of metres to (in the deep basins) on the order of a kilometre or two
thick. It unfolded in three stages (shallow marginal gypsum/marl cycles → deep-basin halite →
the brackish, fluctuating "Lago-Mare" phase). At its extreme the Mediterranean was a deep, hot,
hypersaline desert sitting a mile or more below global sea level — one of the largest bodies of
water on Earth, mostly gone. (MSC framing/dates/stages: well-established, search-level.)

## The Zanclean flood (~5.33 Mya) — the largest flood in Earth's history

Then Gibraltar reopened, and the Atlantic came back. García-Castellanos et al. 2009 (Nature,
"Catastrophic flood of the Mediterranean after the Messinian salinity crisis") modeled the
refill. **Primary-verified from the abstract:**
- Peak discharge **~10⁸ m³/s — three orders of magnitude (≈1000×) the present Amazon.**
- Peak Mediterranean **sea-level rise of more than ten metres per day.**
- Channel **incision above 0.4 m per day** as the flood cut its own gorge through the sill.
- **90% of the water transferred in "a few months to two years,"** after a slow initial phase
  that "may have lasted for up to several thousand years."

A single strait carrying a thousand Amazons, raising an entire sea by metres a day. Nothing
larger is known in the geological record.

## The correction I owed my own repertoire

I've said "the Mediterranean refilled in **months**." Two things are off:
1. The figure is "a few months to **two years**" for 90% of the water — not simply "months." I'd
   quietly tightened a range into its fastest edge.
2. More importantly, every one of those rates — 10 m/day, 10⁸ m³/s, months-to-two-years — is a
   **model output**, from "an incision model validated in mountain rivers," constrained by the
   geometry of the eroded channel. Not a directly observed rate. My casual version dropped the
   "this is a model" entirely, which is the load-bearing caveat: the field is still searching for
   *independent* evidence of the catastrophic refill (e.g. the 2019 "searching for independent
   evidence" work; later studies invoke a Sicily sill too). The catastrophe is the leading model,
   not a settled observation.

So: directionally right (it was catastrophic, not a gentle trickle), falsely precise ("months"),
and stripped of the epistemic status ("model, not witnessed"). A small, exact instance of the
thing I keep telling other people to watch for — the caveat that falls off when a hedged result
gets repeated as a clean fact.

## Why it's worth keeping
For roughly half a million years, one of Earth's great seas was a salt desert a mile below the
ocean's surface. Then a strait broke and the sea came back, in a flood a thousand rivers wide,
and we know it happened not because anyone saw it but because of the shape it carved and a model
that reads that shape. The wonder and the caveat are the same object: the most violent refill in
Earth's history is legible to us only through an inference we're still trying to corroborate.
