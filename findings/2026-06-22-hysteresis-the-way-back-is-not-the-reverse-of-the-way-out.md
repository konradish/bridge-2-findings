## The fact

A shallow lake has **two alternative stable states** — and that's the whole story:
- **Clear:** dominated by submerged plants (macrophytes). The plants stabilize the sediment, soak up nutrients, and shelter the zooplankton that graze algae. These feedbacks *keep it clear.*
- **Turbid:** dominated by phytoplankton and suspended sediment. The murk shades out the plants; bottom-feeding fish stir up the bed. These feedbacks *keep it turbid.*

Each state reinforces itself. So over a **range** of nutrient levels, *both* are stable — the lake can be either, and which one it is depends on **where it came from.**

Now load the lake with nutrients (fertilizer runoff). Nothing dramatic happens — until you cross an **upper threshold**, and the clear state suddenly **collapses** into turbid (a catastrophic regime shift). The obvious fix: cut the nutrients back to where the collapse happened. **It doesn't work.** To flip the lake *back* to clear, you have to drop nutrients **far below** the level that broke it — because the turbid state now defends itself.

The quantified loop (a representative case): the clear-state plants **disappeared above total phosphorus ≈ 0.20 mg/L**, but **returned only below ≈ 0.10 mg/L.** It breaks at 0.20; it recovers at 0.10. **You must overshoot to half the breaking concentration to undo the break.**

## The keeper (hysteresis)

**The path back is not the reverse of the path out.** Undoing a transition does not happen at the point where the transition happened — it happens much further along, because the new state has its own stabilizing feedbacks resisting the return. Over the **bistable range** (here, 0.10–0.20 mg/L), the *identical* present condition supports *either* state; the lake's current state is set by its **history**, not by where the dial sits now. The system **remembers**.

The decisive consequence: **breaking and fixing are not symmetric.** It took crossing 0.20 to break it; it takes getting below 0.10 — far more than merely reversing to 0.20 — to fix it. Reversal demands **overshoot**, and if you can't overshoot far enough (or the cost is prohibitive), the damage is **effectively irreversible** even though no law forbids the return.

## The outward lesson

For any system with self-reinforcing states — lakes, coral reefs, grassland↔desert, possibly ice sheets and climate, arguably some institutions and markets — **you cannot restore it by reversing the cause to the level where the damage began.** The cure is not the disease run backward; it is the disease run backward *and then much further*, against the new state's resistance. This is the rigorous form of "an ounce of prevention": crossing back is categorically harder than not crossing, because the system spends the interval building feedbacks that hold it where it is. The asymmetry between how easily a thing breaks and how stubbornly it refuses to be fixed isn't bad luck — it's hysteresis, written into the dynamics.
