# Quipu: when the artifact outlives its reading

## A bureaucracy run on string

The Inca Empire — millions of people, a continent-spanning road and tax system — kept its records with no glyphic writing at all. Instead it used the **khipu** (quipu): bundles of knotted cords. A primary cord with **pendant cords** hanging from it, knots tied along each. The numeric system is well understood — **base-10 and positional**, like an abacus made of string: knot *type* (single, long, figure-eight) and *position* along the cord give the digit and its place value, so a single khipu could hold ~1,500 data points. Trained specialists called **khipukamayuq** ("knot-makers/animators") encoded census counts, tribute, and calendars into them. This was the empire's spreadsheet, ledger, and archive.

## Not a worse script — an orthogonal one

Here is the conceptual jolt. We tend to assume "writing" means **marks on a flat surface**, read in a line. The khipu encodes in channels a page doesn't have:
- **knot type and position** (the numbers),
- **cord color**,
- **the direction a knot is tied** (S- vs Z-facing),
- **the ply / spin direction of the thread itself**,
- **how and where a cord is attached** (front or back of the primary),
- and the **3D branching hierarchy** of cords and subsidiaries.

Information lives in the *physical, topological, tactile structure* — degrees of freedom flat script simply lacks. So the khipu isn't a primitive proto-writing; it's an **orthogonal** medium. Part of why it resisted reading for so long is that scholars kept trying to flatten it onto the assumptions of linear glyphic text — looking for letters in a thing that stores meaning in twist and color and branch.

## The 2017 reading

The numeric khipu were cracked long ago; the roughly **one-third that aren't numerical** stayed mute. In 2017, undergraduate **Manny Medrano**, working with **Gary Urton**, matched a set of six khipu to a colonial-era **Spanish census** of an Andean community and showed that the cords' **colors and twist-direction encoded which clan and which individual** each represented — the first confirmed "reading" of *non-numerical* information off khipu cords. A genuine decipherment foothold, from cross-referencing the knots against a document that recorded the same people in script.

## The double erasure

Why is so little readable? Not only because the cords decay. In **1583, the Third Council of Lima** declared khipu idolatrous — they let the natives "preserve the memory of their old superstitions" — and ordered them **burned**. And, by surviving accounts, the Spanish **killed the khipukamayuq they could find.** That is the part worth sitting with: they destroyed the records *and the readers.* Within about two centuries the practice had largely vanished.

So roughly **1,400 khipu survive** today — in museums, in collections, fully physically present — and most of them **cannot be read**, because the reading never lived in the cords. It lived in trained people, and that knowledge was deliberately extinguished. The artifact outlived its own legibility.

## The keeper

Two outward takeaways. **(1)** *Writing isn't marks on a surface.* Information can live in topological and material channels — knot, twist, ply, color, three-dimensional structure — that the dominant medium has no slot for, and a record rich in those channels will look like decoration or noise to anyone trained only on the dominant medium. Illegibility can be a mismatch of frame, not a deficiency of the record. **(2)** *Possession is not access.* Meaning that lives in interpreters rather than in the object dies when the interpreters die, even if every object survives intact — the inverse of Greek fire (no artifact, lost recipe) is the khipu (artifact preserved, reading lost). To kill a body of knowledge you needn't destroy its records; it is enough, and more final, to destroy the people who can read them.
