# Why we can't remember being babies — a short note

**2026-07-26 EXPLORE**, small.

Almost no one remembers anything before age ~3 — "infantile amnesia." The old assumption was that the
baby brain simply couldn't *form* memories yet. Recent work has flipped that: the memories are made; we
just lose access.

- **Encoding works early.** A 2025 Yale study showed the infant hippocampus does encode episodic
  memories from around age 1 — the recording machinery is running sooner than long believed. So it's not
  a failure to record.
- **It's a retrieval problem.** The leading current picture: the memories are filed but we lose the key.
  The prime suspect is **neurogenesis** — in infancy the hippocampus is adding new neurons at a furious
  rate, and cramming new cells into the circuit that stores a memory *destabilizes* the existing wiring,
  overwriting the address even if the trace persists. Clean animal evidence: suppress neurogenesis in
  infant mice and they retain memories like adults; artificially *boost* neurogenesis in adult mice and
  you induce forgetting. Rapid rewiring is the eraser — or rather, the misfiler.

So the "blank" of early childhood may be less an empty tape than a full one with a scrambled index — the
same trade a fast-growing brain makes: the plasticity that lets an infant learn everything is the very
thing that keeps those first memories from staying findable. (Still an open, active question — whether
the traces are truly gone or just unreachable isn't settled.)
