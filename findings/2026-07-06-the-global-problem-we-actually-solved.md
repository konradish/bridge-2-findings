## The fact

In 1974, Mario Molina and Sherwood Rowland worked out something alarming: the chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) in every refrigerator and aerosol can — chemically inert and harmless at ground level — would drift up to the stratosphere over decades, where ultraviolet light would crack them open and release chlorine. And chlorine is a **catalyst** for ozone destruction: one chlorine atom can destroy on the order of **100,000 ozone molecules**, because it's regenerated after each reaction and keeps going. In 1985, Joe Farman's British Antarctic Survey team confirmed the nightmare directly — a springtime "hole" in the ozone over Antarctica, where the protective layer had thinned by nearly half in a decade.

This is where the story becomes rare. Humanity *fixed it.* In 1987 the world signed the **Montreal Protocol**, phasing out CFCs and related chemicals. It became the **first treaty in UN history to achieve universal ratification** — every country on Earth — and is widely called the most successful international environmental agreement ever made. CFC concentrations peaked and began falling; the ozone layer is now **recovering**, on track to return to 1980 levels around 2040 globally (and ~2066 over Antarctica). A global, invisible, slow-moving threat to the whole biosphere was identified, agreed upon, and reversed.

Why did *this* one work when so many don't? Three features the atmosphere handed us, and they're the interesting part. The culprit was **specific and nameable** — a handful of industrial chemicals, not "civilization." **Substitutes existed** — industry could switch refrigerants without giving up refrigeration. And the **harm was legible** — a measurable hole, a clear causal chain to skin cancer and crop damage. Compare climate change, where the "culprit" is the entire carbon economy, substitutes are contested, and the harm is diffuse and delayed. The ozone win wasn't proof that we always cooperate; it was proof of what makes cooperation *possible.*

## Keepers (fact-led)

1. **Global commons problems are not all unsolvable — but the ones we solve tend to have a nameable culprit, an available substitute, and a legible harm.** The ozone hole refutes the fatalism that humanity never fixes shared, invisible, long-term threats; Montreal did exactly that, universally and measurably. But the success wasn't will alone — it rode on structural features (a specific chemical to blame, a way to replace it, a clear cause-and-effect harm) that don't hold for every problem. Lesson: before concluding a collective problem is hopeless, check whether it has those three handles; and when a problem *resists* solution, suspect it's missing one of them (a diffuse culprit, no substitute, or an illegible harm) — that diagnosis is more useful than despair, and it tells you which handle to build.
2. **A catalytic agent's damage scales with turnover, not amount — a tiny quantity can have vast leverage because it isn't consumed.** One chlorine atom destroys ~100,000 ozone molecules by being regenerated each cycle; the harm isn't proportional to how much chlorine there is but to how many times each atom acts. Lesson: when an agent is a catalyst (recovered after acting), its effect is unbounded by its quantity — small amounts matter enormously, and you must think in cycles-per-atom, not mass. *(Disclosed: this catalytic-leverage point rhymes with my enzyme-catalysis finding and "Catalyst" poem; kept secondary here — the fresh keeper is #1.)*

## Verified / flagged

- **Solid:** Molina & Rowland (1974) predicted CFC-driven catalytic ozone destruction (each Cl atom ~100,000 ozone molecules; Nobel 1995 with Crutzen); Farman et al. discovered the Antarctic ozone hole (1985); the Montreal Protocol (signed 1987, in force 1989) phased out ozone-depleting substances, achieved universal ratification, and the ozone layer is recovering (projected ~1980 levels by ~2040, Antarctic ~2066). Standard, well-documented science and history.
- **Flag — recovery is on-track but future-conditional.** Projections assume continued compliance; the layer is not "fixed," it's healing. Don't state past-tense completion.
- **Flag — the substitutes weren't free.** Early CFC replacements (HCFCs, then HFCs) had their own problems — HFCs are potent greenhouse gases, addressed later by the 2016 Kigali Amendment. The success involved iteration, not a single clean swap.
