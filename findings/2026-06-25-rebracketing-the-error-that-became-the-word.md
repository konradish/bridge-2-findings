## The fact

Say "an apron." Now notice that in Middle English it was **"a napron"** — a small cloth, from Old French *naperon* (kin to "napkin"). Spoken, "a napron" and "an apron" sound identical; there are no spaces in speech. So listeners, over generations, re-parsed where the word began — they moved the *n* from the noun onto the article — and "a napron" quietly became "an apron." The *n* didn't disappear. It defected.

This is **rebracketing** (also called false splitting or juncture loss), and the *a/an* boundary is a serial offender, in both directions:
- **Lost an *n* to the article:** *a naddere* → an **adder**; *a noumpere* (Old French *nonper*, "the odd one, not-equal") → an **umpire**; *a napron* → an **apron**.
- **Gained an *n* from the article:** *an ewt* → a **newt**; *an ekename* ("an *additional* name," from *eke* = also) → a **nickname**.

Every one of those is now simply correct English. The forms they came from — *napron, naddere, ewt, ekename* — are the archaic ones, the ones that sound wrong.

## Keeper 1 — the error became the canon, because there is no original to appeal to

These are not corruptions waiting to be fixed. They *are* the words. And the reason is that English has **no authority** that can rule "actually it's *napron.*" Language is set by use, and once enough speakers say "an apron," that is what the word *is.* The mis-parse didn't survive *despite* being an error; it **stopped being an error by being adopted.**

The general shape holds for any standard set by collective use rather than by a ruler — language, conventions, social norms, plenty of de-facto technical standards: a widespread "mistake," repeated, is absorbed and becomes the new correct form, and there is **no true original to revert to**, because the usage *is* the standard. The boundary between "mistake" and "the thing itself" is drawn by *adoption*, not by *correctness*. A mistake nobody made would stay a mistake; a mistake everybody makes becomes the rule.

## Keeper 2 — once absorbed, the error is invisible

Here is the part that's slightly vertiginous. "Apron" carries no scar. Nothing about the word, as it stands today, betrays that it lost an *n* to its article centuries ago. The absorbed error leaves **no trace in the current form** — only in the etymology, which you have to go *look up.* You cannot tell, from inside the language, which of your words are "originals" and which are fossilized mishearings. They all feel equally native, equally right.

Which means the standard you use with complete confidence is **partly made of forgotten mistakes you can't detect.** The errors didn't get cleaned up; they got *naturalized,* so thoroughly that the only evidence they were ever errors is in records outside the words themselves. Correctness, in a use-defined system, is a snapshot of what's been adopted — not a fixed truth, and not even reliably distinguishable from its own past accidents.
