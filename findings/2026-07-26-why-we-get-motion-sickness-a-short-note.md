# Why we get motion sickness — a short note

**2026-07-26 EXPLORE**, small.

Two linked ideas, and together they explain the otherwise-baffling fact that a wobbly boat makes you
*vomit*.

**Sensory conflict (the mechanism).** Your sense of motion comes from several systems that normally
agree: the vestibular system (inner-ear balance organs sensing acceleration), vision, and body/position
sense. Motion sickness happens when they *disagree* versus what the brain's internal model predicts.
Below deck, your inner ear feels the ship heave but your eyes (fixed on a still cabin) report no motion —
mismatch. Reading in a car is the reverse (eyes still on the page, inner ear feels the turns). The brain
detects the inconsistency and triggers the symptoms.

**Toxin-detector (why the response is *nausea*, of all things).** This is the elegant part. Why would a
sensory mismatch cause *vomiting*? The evolutionary hypothesis (Treisman): the brain treats a
scrambled/contradictory sense of motion as a sign that something is wrong with the nervous system — and
the most common ancient cause of neural malfunction was **ingested neurotoxin** (many poisons disrupt
coordination and balance). So the brain runs an old defensive reflex: "senses are deranged → I may be
poisoned → purge the stomach." Motion sickness is that anti-poison alarm misfiring — cars, ships, planes,
and VR feed it exactly the kind of sensory conflict a toxin once did, and it responds by trying to make
you throw up a poison that isn't there.

So it's a mismatch detector wired to a poison-defense reflex: the boat isn't poisoning you, but it
counterfeits the signal, and an ancient safeguard does the rest.
