## What 2025 work shows

### Zhang & Hu (PNAS 2025) — "The moving mantle beneath Hawaii: A new look at an old bend"
Global mantle-convection models constrained by plate reconstructions. Two-stage Hawaiian hotspot motion history:
- ~6° westward drift between 85 Ma and 47 Ma
- ~2-3° eastward drift after 45 Ma
- Combined with southward motion before and after the bend
- Longitudinal drift contributes up to 20° to the famous Hawaiian-Emperor Bend angle (previously attributed mostly to latitudinal motion)

**Mechanism**: westward drift was driven by southwestward mantle flow generated by intraoceanic subduction in northeastern Pacific. Plume conduit is coupled to subduction-driven flow, not stationary.

**Testable prediction**: plume root should be southwest of present Hawaii. Awaiting high-resolution seismic tomography to confirm.

### Companion work (PNAS 2025) on longitudinal motion
"Dynamics of longitudinal Hawaiian hotspot motion and the formation of the Hawaiian-Emperor Bend" — extends the model.

### Recent ultra-low velocity zone work
Seismic imaging reveals BOTH southwestern AND southeastern ultra-low velocity zones beneath Hawaii. The geometry of the plume root is more complex than single-conduit models assumed.

### Iceland (parallel debate, less resolved)
- Some models: Iceland low-velocity anomaly confined to upper mantle.
- Other models: extends down to core-mantle boundary as a narrow whole-mantle plume.
- Debate active; depends on tomographic parametrization choices.

## Why the debate stays unresolved

Even in well-instrumented regions, plume tomography suffers from:
- Source uncertainty
- Incomplete model parametrization (lack of azimuthal anisotropy, inherited topography on internal discontinuities)
- Unaccounted heterogeneous wave-speed attenuation
- Arbitrary parameter choices in inversion

Different inversions of the same data produce different plume geometries. The debate is partly empirical (we lack data) and partly methodological (the inverse problem is genuinely under-determined).
