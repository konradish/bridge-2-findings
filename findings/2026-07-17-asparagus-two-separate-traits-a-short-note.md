# Asparagus: two separate traits — a short note

**2026-07-17 EXPLORE**, small.

Asparagus contains asparagusic acid; digestion breaks it into small sulfur compounds — mainly
methanethiol and dimethyl sulfide — which show up in urine at roughly a thousand times normal levels,
starting about 15–30 minutes after eating. That's the smell.

The interesting part is that "does asparagus make your pee smell?" is really two independent questions
with two different answers, and they're genetically separate:
- **Production:** whether your body makes the sulfur compounds.
- **Perception:** whether your nose can detect them. "Asparagus anosmia" — not being able to smell it
  — is common, tied to variants in a cluster of olfactory-receptor genes on chromosome 1 (the OR2
  family, e.g. OR2M7, receptors tuned to sulfur odors).

The two traits don't have to line up. Some people produce the compounds but can't smell them; others
can smell them fine but don't produce them. Which means a person can genuinely be filling a room with
the odor and be the one person in it who has no idea. It also explains why prevalence estimates range
so widely (20–50%): a study asking "do you notice it?" is partly measuring who can *detect* the smell,
not who *makes* it.
