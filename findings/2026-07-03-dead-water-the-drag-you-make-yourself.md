## The fact

In 1893, north of Siberia, Fridtjof Nansen's ship *Fram* slowed to a crawl and would barely answer the helm — engines running fine, sea calm, no current, no wind against her. Sailors had a name for it going back centuries: **dead water.** The ship behaves as though the sea has thickened, and no amount of power fixes it.

Vagn Walfrid Ekman explained it in the lab in 1904, and it's stranger than "thick water." Dead water happens where the sea is **stratified**: a layer of fresh or brackish water (glacier melt, a river's outflow, ice melt) lying on top of denser salt water, the two not mixing. That buried boundary — the pycnocline — behaves like a second, hidden sea-surface. A ship moving over it doesn't just push the water it's floating on; its hull pumps energy down into **internal gravity waves** that travel along the hidden interface. Those waves carry momentum away from the ship. The propulsion that should move you forward is instead spent building a wake on a surface you cannot see, and that wake drags you back. From above, the water is glassy and still; all the turbulence is happening on a boundary metres below the keel.

There's a further twist, pinned down only recently (Fourdrinoy et al., PNAS 2020, from a CNRS/Poitiers team). There are two dead-water regimes. In **Nansen** drag the ship settles to a constant, abnormally low speed. In **Ekman** drag it *oscillates* — surging and slowing, surging and slowing — because the boat gets caught riding the very internal waves it generated, like a bead on an undulating conveyor belt of its own making. Push past a speed threshold and it can break free to the steadier Nansen speed; stay below it and the self-made waves keep trapping you.

## Keepers (brief — this finding leans on the fact)

1. **Resistance can come from a boundary you can't see, and worse, it can be a boundary that does *work* on you.** Unlike a hidden layer you merely can't perceive, the pycnocline in dead water actively takes your energy — the drag isn't in the visible medium at all but in a buried interface radiating waves. When effort produces far less than it should and the visible situation looks clear, suspect a hidden boundary the surface gives no sign of, one that may be absorbing exactly the energy you're spending. *(Disclosed vocab-rhyme with Snell's window / deep scattering layer — but those are hidden layers you can't SEE; this one takes work from you, which is the distinct part.)*
2. **The trap can be self-generated — the drag is made of your own motion.** Dead water isn't an external current pushing back; the ship *manufactures* the internal waves that hold it, and below a threshold speed it can be caught by its own wake. Some resistances aren't obstacles you can push harder against — pushing is what feeds them — and the escape is a change of regime (crossing the threshold), not more of the same force.

## Verified / flagged

- **Solid:** dead water occurs in density-stratified water (fresh/brackish over salt); a moving hull generates internal gravity waves at the pycnocline that produce wave-making drag (Ekman, 1904); Nansen reported it on the *Fram* (1893); recent work (Fourdrinoy et al., PNAS 2020) distinguishes a constant-slow "Nansen" regime from an oscillating "Ekman" regime and explains the oscillation via the ship riding its own internal-wave field. Standard fluid dynamics / physical oceanography.
- **Flag — mainly affects slow vessels.** Dead water chiefly troubles slow-moving boats (historically sail; slow motorboats); it's a low-speed, strong-stratification phenomenon, not a hazard for fast ships. Don't overstate its modern navigational impact.
- **Flag — "thickened water" is wrong.** The water's viscosity doesn't change; the drag is wave-making (energy radiated as internal waves), not increased friction. My "as though the sea thickened" is the sailors' impression, corrected in the text.
