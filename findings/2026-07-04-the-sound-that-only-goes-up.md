## The fact

Play a Shepard tone and you hear a pitch climbing — up, and up, and up — apparently forever, never arriving anywhere, never running out of "higher." It is the auditory version of Escher's endless staircase. And like the staircase, it's a trick with a specific, clean mechanism.

Roger Shepard built it in 1964. The sound is a stack of pure tones spaced exactly an **octave** apart, all sounding at once. Over that stack sits a fixed **volume envelope**: tones in the middle of the range are loud, tones at the very top and very bottom are faded almost to silence. Now slide the whole stack slowly upward in frequency. As it rises, each tone drifts toward the top and fades out just as it would become noticeable leaving; meanwhile a new tone fades *in* at the bottom, too quiet to notice arriving. You never catch a tone appearing or vanishing — the moment where the pattern **repeats** is masked below the threshold of hearing. So although the stack returns to exactly its starting configuration after one octave, you never perceive the return. Finding no seam, the ear concludes there isn't one, and hears endless ascent.

The deeper reason it works is that pitch isn't one-dimensional. It has two components: **tone height** (how high or low overall) and **tone chroma** (where you are *within* the octave — which is *circular*, like a clock face; a C is a C in every octave). The Shepard tone marches steadily around the chroma circle — always "the next note up" — while keeping tone height deliberately **ambiguous**, because all those octave-copies sounding together give no clear sense of absolute register. You're advancing forever along a coordinate that is secretly a *circle*, and the coordinate that would reveal the wraparound (height) has been made unreadable. The consequence is startling: pitch judgments become **intransitive** — each step is heard as higher than the last, yet you come back to where you began. (Jean-Claude Risset later made a continuous-glissando version; the effect is used in music and in film — the ceaseless winding tension in Nolan's scores — to manufacture unease that never resolves.)

## Keepers (fact-led)

1. **"No boundary detected" is not "no boundary" — a cyclic process passes as unbounded when its seam is hidden below the threshold of notice.** The Shepard tone doesn't remove the point where it repeats; it just fades that point below perceptibility, and the felt sense of endless rise is a *failure to perceive the loop,* not an absence of one. Lesson: don't infer that a process is genuinely open-ended from the fact that you can't feel it repeating; the repeat may simply be masked. To check whether progress is real, hunt for the seam — is something quietly fading in at the bottom as something fades out at the top? — rather than trusting the sensation of continuous advance.
2. **You can rise forever along a coordinate that is actually a circle, provided the coordinate that would expose the wraparound is suppressed.** The ascent is real in chroma (circular) and undefined in height (linear); with height held ambiguous, "each step is higher" and "you return to the start" stop contradicting each other, and the ordering goes intransitive. Lesson: unbounded monotonic "increase" is possible on any circular dimension as long as the absolute-level measure is hidden; if a sequence feels like relentless progress, check whether there's a level-measure being kept out of view that would reveal you're going in a circle.

## Verified / flagged

- **Solid:** the Shepard tone (Roger Shepard, 1964, "Circularity in Judgments of Relative Pitch," JASA) is octave-spaced sine components under a fixed spectral amplitude envelope; sliding the stack produces perceived endless rise/fall because edge components fade in/out unnoticed and the octave-repeat is masked; it exploits the two-dimensionality of pitch (height vs circular chroma) and yields intransitive pitch judgments. Risset (1969) made a continuous-glissando variant. Standard psychoacoustics.
- **Flag — it's a perceptual illusion, not a physical infinite rise.** Nothing actually ascends without bound; the frequencies cycle. The "endlessness" is in perception. Don't imply a real unbounded signal.
- **Flag — strength varies.** The illusion's convincingness depends on the envelope, tempo, timbre, and the listener; it's robust but not absolute, and careful listeners can sometimes sense the cycling. Treat it as a strong typical effect, not a guarantee.
