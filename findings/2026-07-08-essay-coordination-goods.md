# Coordination Goods

*Essay · 2026-07-08 · CREATE (~520w — a class of thing the shipping-container finding pointed at: value that exists only in universal adoption. Outward/intellectual; grounded in examples, lands on a sharp asymmetry. Publish decision Konrad's; not rostered.)*

---

The shipping container remade global trade, and the strange part is that the box did none of it. A steel box is nothing. What did the work was that *everyone agreed on the same box* — one set of dimensions, corner-castings, and fittings that every port, ship, truck, and railroad on earth would conform to. The value wasn't in the container. It was in the *sameness*.

That's worth naming as a category, because it behaves unlike almost everything else we value. Call them **coordination goods**: things whose entire worth lives in universal adoption, and which are worth essentially nothing outside it.

Money is the clean case — a currency no one else will take is just paper; its value is precisely the number of others who accept it, nothing more. A language only you speak is not a language, it's a private cipher. The meter, the second, the volt; the side of the road you drive on; the railway gauge; the voltage in the wall; the protocol two machines shake hands with. In none of these does the value sit *in* the thing. It sits in the agreement. Each instance is worthless alone and priceless in chorus.

And here is where they invert the ordinary logic of value. A better mousetrap is worth more the instant it exists — its worth is intrinsic, and the first buyer gets the full benefit. A better *standard* is worth **less** than the incumbent until nearly everyone has switched, because a standard's value is its adoption, and a new one starts with an adoption of one. So the pioneer of a coordination good bears a real cost for a benefit that only the *last* adopter completes. You cannot create the value unilaterally. You can only build the thing and wait to see whether it tips.

Which means the fate of a coordination good is decided by something other than its quality. Worse standards routinely win and outlast better ones — not because anyone judged them better, but because they tipped first, and once tipped, the cost of everyone switching exceeds the gain from the improvement. QWERTY beat faster layouts. The width of a Roman cart rut echoes, by a chain of path-dependencies, in the gauge of modern railways. Quality doesn't win a coordination game; *adoption* does, and adoption is mostly about who got there first and who else was already there.

So the honest thing to notice is that for this whole category of value, you do not actually want the *best* one. You want *the one everyone else is using* — and those are two different wishes, which happen to point at the same object only by luck. When they diverge, the crowd is right and the connoisseur is wrong, because the good was never the artifact. It was the company you keep in choosing it.

The container was a triumph of exactly this and nothing else: not a clever object, but a boring one that everybody, finally, agreed to hold in common.
