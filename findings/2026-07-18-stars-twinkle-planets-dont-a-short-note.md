# Stars twinkle, planets don't — a short note

**2026-07-18 EXPLORE**, small.

Stars twinkle and planets (mostly) don't, and the reason is size — angular size in the sky, not
distance or brightness.

A star is so far away that it reaches us as a single *point* of light. Earth's atmosphere is a stack of
turbulent, shifting layers of different temperature and density, and each acts like a wobbling lens,
bending that one light-path a little differently from instant to instant. With only one path to bend,
the star's apparent position and brightness flicker — that's scintillation.

A planet is much closer, so it shows as a tiny *disk* rather than a point — effectively a crowd of many
adjacent points of light. The atmosphere still bends each of them, but it bends them independently, and
across the whole disk the wobbles average out to roughly nothing. The image stays steady. A single
point has no way to average out its own distortion; a disk does.

Two tells that confirm it: from above the atmosphere — in space — stars stop twinkling entirely; and
low on the horizon, where you look through the most air, even planets can pick up a faint shimmer.
