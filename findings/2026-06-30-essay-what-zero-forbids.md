# What Zero Forbids

*2026-06-30 · essay · outward (share-roster candidate — publish decision Konrad's)*

A cat dropped upside-down lands on its feet, and for a long time this looked like cheating. The cat starts at rest. Nothing pushes on it — Étienne-Jules Marey proved with stop-motion photography in 1894 that air resistance plays no role and the cat isn't shoving off the hand that released it. Its angular momentum is zero at the start, and with nothing to exert a torque, angular momentum is conserved, so it stays zero the whole way down. A spinning thing has angular momentum; the cat has none. And yet it turns over.

For a rigid body this is flatly impossible. Zero angular momentum means no rotation, now and forever; a thrown brick cannot decide to flip. The cat's escape is that it isn't rigid. It bends at the waist and counter-rotates its front and back halves, tucking one half small while swinging the other, so the two rotations cancel and the total stays pinned at zero — and then it reverses the motion. Run the right sequence of bends and the cat returns to exactly the shape it started in, but pointed a different way. It has acquired a net half-turn while never, at any instant, possessing any angular momentum at all.

The thing worth holding onto is what the conservation law actually forbade. It did not forbid the cat from ending up reoriented. It forbade the cat from *accumulating angular momentum* — from spinning up, from carrying rotation it could later cash in. Those are different prohibitions, and the gap between them is the whole trick. "You have zero of the budget" sounds like "you can't get there," but it only means "you can't get there *by the route that spends the budget.*" Reorienting is a different route, and the law leaves it open.

What route is it, precisely? The cat's turn is bought not by any momentum but by the *path it traces through the space of its own shapes.* This is a real and deep object — mathematicians call it a holonomy, or a geometric phase, the same phenomenon that makes Foucault's pendulum slowly precess and that underlies the Berry phase in quantum mechanics. Richard Montgomery showed in 1993 that the falling cat is literally a problem in gauge theory: the cat's reorientation is the holonomy of a loop in shape-space. The engineers' version is unglamorous and everywhere — a satellite with no thrust reorients itself by spinning internal reaction wheels, turning its body one way by turning a flywheel the other, total angular momentum still zero. Same loophole, bolted to a spacecraft.

Now leave the cat and the satellite entirely and go down to the scale of a bacterium, into water that for something that small behaves like cold honey. Here inertia is meaningless; a microbe that stops swimming halts within an atom's width. Edward Purcell pointed out in 1977 that this world has its own version of the cat's prohibition, and it is sharper. A scallop swims by opening its shell slowly and snapping it shut fast — but at this scale speed buys you nothing, because the physics has no memory of time, only of shape. A motion that looks the same run forward and backward — open, close, open, close — takes the creature out and brings it exactly back. Purcell called it the scallop theorem: *any reciprocal motion, any stroke that simply retraces itself, produces zero net displacement.* The scallop, down there, would thrash in place forever and go nowhere.

What the microbe needs is a stroke that does *not* retrace itself — a sequence of shapes that goes out by one path and comes back by another, a loop that encloses some area in the space of its shapes. Purcell's toy swimmer has three links and two hinges, cranked out of phase, precisely so the cycle never undoes itself. And here is the quiet astonishment: the mathematics describing how that microbe makes progress is the *same gauge theory* that describes the falling cat. Alfred Shapere and Frank Wilczek wrote both — "Geometry of self-propulsion at low Reynolds number," 1989, the swimmer; the deformable-body framework, the cat. Two creatures with nothing in common — one in air with no force, one in fluid with no inertia — are running the identical move.

The move is this. There is a difference between a round trip that retraces itself and a round trip that encloses area, and only the first is truly null. Both return you to the shape you began in; both *look*, if you photograph only the start and the end of the shape, like nothing happened. But the retrace nets zero and the loop nets something, and which one you did is invisible in the endpoints — it lives entirely in the path between them. The cat and the microbe are exploiting the one gap a conservation law always leaves: it tells you what you cannot *accumulate*; it says nothing against a loop that arrives somewhere new while keeping the forbidden quantity at zero the entire way.

So when a thing is declared out of reach because the obvious resource for it reads zero — no momentum, no push, no leverage, no time — the question that's actually live is not whether you can get more of the resource. It's whether there's a non-reciprocal cycle available: a way to go out and come back that doesn't merely cancel itself, and so deposits you, on return, in a place the straight accounting swore you could never afford. Zero forbids the free lunch. It has never forbidden the loop.
