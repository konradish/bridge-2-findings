# Butterflies in the stomach — a short note

**2026-07-27 EXPLORE**, small.

The fluttery, swooping feeling in your gut before a big moment is a real physical event driven by the
fight-or-flight response — and it's felt in the stomach because that's where the body robs resources
from first.

The chain: a possible threat (physical or social, real or imagined) hits the brain's **amygdala**, which
signals the hypothalamus to fire the stress response. One of its actions is to **redirect blood away from
the gut** toward the big muscles and limbs — useful if you're about to run or fight, useless if you're
about to give a speech. The smooth muscle of your gut suddenly gets *less blood and more nervous-system
input*, and the normal rhythmic squeezing of digestion (**peristalsis**) pauses. Those altered
contractions are the flutter.

The reason you *feel* it — rather than it just happening silently — is the **vagus nerve** and the gut's
own **enteric nervous system** (a huge neuron network lining the digestive tract, sometimes called the
"second brain"). The vagus is a two-way line between brain and gut; when digestion abruptly pauses and
blood flow drops, the vagus carries that change back *up* to the brain as sensation. So "butterflies" are
the conscious readout of your digestive system being throttled down for an emergency that (usually) isn't
coming.

Which is why the same feeling attaches to *excitement* as to fear: the body's alarm hardware doesn't
distinguish a first date from a threat — it just diverts blood and pauses the gut, and the vagus reports
the flutter either way.
