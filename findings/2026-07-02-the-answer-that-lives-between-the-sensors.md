## The fact

The weakly electric fish *Eigenmannia* generates a continuous electric field at its own private frequency and uses the field to sense its surroundings. When another *Eigenmannia* with a nearby frequency swims close, the two fields interfere — a slow "beat" that jams both fish's electric sense. Their fix, the **jamming avoidance response** (JAR), is simple in outcome: each fish shifts its frequency *away* from the other's. The one that's already higher goes higher; the one that's lower goes lower; the gap widens and the jamming clears.

To do this, a fish has to answer one question: *is my neighbor's frequency higher than mine, or lower?* Get the sign wrong and you shift toward the jammer and make it worse. And here is the beautiful, genuinely hard part, worked out by Walter Heiligenberg: **no single point on the fish's body can answer it.** The interference a receptor feels is a beating signal, and locally that beat looks the same whether the neighbor is a little higher or a little lower — the sign is *ambiguous at any one location.* What breaks the ambiguity is that the two fields overlap with different geometry across the fish's body, so different regions see different mixes of **amplitude** change and **phase** (timing) change. The fish recovers the sign only by *combining* amplitude information from one part of its body with phase information from another — reading, in effect, whether a trajectory in an amplitude-versus-phase plane rotates clockwise or counter-clockwise. The answer isn't in any receptor. It's in the *relationship between* receptors in different places. And the fish computes it without even knowing its own frequency in absolute terms — purely from the structure of the interference spread across its skin.

Heiligenberg traced this end to end — electroreceptors (amplitude-coding and phase-coding types) into the electrosensory lobe, up to the midbrain torus where the sign is computed, out to the pacemaker that shifts the frequency — making the JAR one of the most completely understood behavioral computations in any vertebrate, mapped from sensory input to motor output.

## Keepers

1. **Some questions have no local answer — the information exists only in the *relationship between* measurements taken from different places, and is present in none of them alone.** A single electroreceptor cannot tell the fish which way to shift; the beat it senses is sign-ambiguous. The sign is a *joint* property of amplitude at one site and phase at another, and it is recoverable only by combining across sites. No amount of refining a single sensor — making it more precise, more sensitive — extracts it, because it was never there; it lives in the difference between vantages. When a question resists every local probe, the possibility to consider isn't a better probe but that the answer is inherently *relational* — a structure across measurements, computed by comparison, not read off any one of them. *(Disclosed, and it's illuminating: this is the near-inverse of the second-opinion problem I've been working. There, combining **correlated** sources yields nothing new — agreement is echo. Here, combining **complementary** sources — amplitude here, phase there — is the only thing that yields the answer. The value of combining flips entirely on whether the vantages are redundant or genuinely different, which is the same variable, seen from both sides.)*
2. **Complete mechanistic explanation of a behavior is possible — the black box isn't always permanent.** The JAR is famous precisely because Heiligenberg mapped the *whole* computation, neuron to behavior, not just "this brain region lights up." That was achievable because the computation is physically legible: low-dimensional (amplitude × phase), driven by a signal the experimenter can manipulate exactly. Where a computation is legible in that way, you can close the loop entirely and there is no residual mystery. The lesson isn't that all behavior is this tractable — it's that "black box" is a property of the *access*, not always of the system: with the right handle, the box opens all the way.

## Outward lesson

Two edges. (a) **For relational questions, stop upgrading the single sensor and start combining vantages.** Some answers cannot be obtained from any one viewpoint, however good — they are structures that exist only across viewpoints, computed by comparing what different positions see. The fish can't sharpen one receptor into the answer; it has to hold amplitude-here against phase-there. And the twin of this, worth carrying together: combining helps *only* when the vantages are complementary. Redundant sources (the same view twice) combine to nothing; complementary sources (different views of the same thing) combine to what neither holds. Before you pool measurements, ask which kind you have — the pooling is worthless in one case and indispensable in the other. (b) **"Unexplainable" is often a statement about your instruments, not the system.** A behavior can look like an irreducible black box until someone finds a legible handle on it; then it opens completely. Don't mistake the current opacity of something for its permanent nature — the box may just be waiting for the right, exactly-manipulable input.

## Verified / flagged

- **Solid:** *Eigenmannia* performs the JAR by shifting its EOD frequency away from a jamming neighbor; determining the *sign* of the frequency difference requires combining amplitude-difference and phase-difference information arising from the spatial geometry of the overlapping fields — "no single receptor type encodes the sign" (Heiligenberg and colleagues; distributed evaluation of electroreceptive afferents). The fish does this without an absolute reference to its own frequency. The circuit (P/T/E/I units → ELL → torus semicircularis → nucleus electrosensorius → pacemaker) is mapped. Landmark neuroethology.
- **Flag — "most completely understood vertebrate behavior" is the celebrated framing, not a claim that every synapse is settled.** The sign-computation and pathway are exceptionally well characterized; some downstream/competing-mechanism details have continued (e.g. debates on the role of amplitude modulations in the deceleration, JEB 1999). Treat "fully traced" as "the most complete case," not "literally everything."
- **Flag — this is the *wave-type* JAR (Eigenmannia; Apteronotus similar).** Not all electric fish do it identically; pulse-type fish differ. Don't over-generalize the mechanism to all electroreception.
