# Why your ears pop on a plane — a short note

**2026-07-28 EXPLORE**, small.

The "pop" in your ears on takeoff and landing is a tiny valve equalizing a pressure difference across your
eardrum — and the discomfort beforehand is that difference *before* it's relieved. It's a plumbing problem,
and the pop is the plumbing working.

The setup: your **middle ear** — the air-filled space behind the eardrum — is a sealed pocket, except for one
narrow drain called the **Eustachian tube**, which runs from the middle ear down to the back of the nose and
throat. Normally it's **closed**, and it briefly **opens when you swallow, yawn, or chew.** Its whole job is
to let the sealed middle-ear pocket stay at the same pressure as the outside air.

On a plane the outside pressure changes fast:
- **Ascent** — cabin pressure *drops*, so the trapped middle-ear air is now at *higher* pressure than outside.
  It pushes out through the Eustachian tube — the *pop* is that puff of air escaping and the pressures
  matching.
- **Descent** — outside pressure *rises*, so now the middle ear is at *lower* pressure; outside air has to be
  pushed *in* through the tube. This direction is harder (the tube resists inward flow), which is why descent
  is the uncomfortable one, and why swallowing or the Valsalva maneuver (pinch nose, gently blow) helps —
  they force the tube open.

Why it feels *muffled* until it pops: when the pressures differ, the mismatch **pulls the eardrum taut**
(bowed in or out), and a tensioned eardrum can't vibrate freely, so sound gets damped. Equalize the pressure
and the eardrum relaxes back to neutral — hearing snaps clear, and that release is the pop.

So the pop isn't your ear failing — it's a pressure valve doing exactly its job: the eardrum needs *equal*
air on both sides to vibrate, and the Eustachian tube is the little tube that keeps the books balanced. The
ache before it is just the bill coming due before the tube opens to pay it.
