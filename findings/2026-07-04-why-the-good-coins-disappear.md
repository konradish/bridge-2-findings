## The fact

"Bad money drives out good." A country circulates two coins of the same face value — say a full-weight silver shilling and a clipped or debased one worth less metal. Both are legal tender at one shilling. What happens? Everyone spends the *bad* coin (getting a full shilling of goods for less silver) and keeps, melts, or ships abroad the *good* one (whose metal is worth more than a shilling). Before long the good coins vanish from circulation and only the debased ones change hands. This is **Gresham's law**, and it's been rediscovered for centuries — Nicole Oresme described it in the 14th century, Copernicus in 1519 ("bad coinage drives good coinage out of circulation"), and the name was pinned on Sir Thomas Gresham, Elizabeth I's financial adviser, only in 1858 by the economist Henry Dunning Macleod.

But the famous one-liner hides the condition that makes it true — and drops the twist that makes it interesting. The law operates **only when a law or fixed rate forces the two to trade at the same nominal value** despite different real values. It's the *legal tender at par* that does the work: because you're compelled to accept the debased coin as a full shilling, the good coin is systematically undervalued in exchange, so you'd be a fool to spend it. Take the peg away — let people price the two coins freely — and the effect **reverses.** When agents can negotiate, they *prefer* the better money and shun the worse; the good drives out the bad. That reversal has its own name, **Thiers' law**, and it means Gresham's law is not a law of money at all. It's a law of **mispricing**: the bad wins only because a rule forbids the good from being valued correctly. The moment true value can be expressed, the direction flips.

## Keepers (fact-led)

1. **A fixed nominal rate between things of unequal real value doesn't average them — it sorts them, adversely.** Force two goods of different quality to trade at the same price and rational holders spend the overvalued one and withhold the undervalued one, so the *worse* dominates the flow while the *better* is withdrawn into hoards. Pegging heterogeneous things to par doesn't blend their qualities into some middle; it drives the good out of circulation and floods what's left with the bad. Lesson: any system that compels unlike-quality items to be treated as interchangeable at one price will hemorrhage its high-quality members and circulate its low-quality ones — the peg is an adverse sorter, not an equalizer.
2. **"The worse drives out the better" is often a policy artifact, not an inevitability — and it reverses when true value can be expressed.** Gresham's law is entirely a creature of the *enforced equal rating*; remove the compulsion (Thiers' law) and good drives out bad. So the bad option winning isn't natural selection — it's manufactured by a fixed rate that lies about relative value. Lesson: when you see the worse option dominating a flow, look for a rule pricing unequal things as equal; the dominance may be *created* by that peg and dissolved by letting quality be priced. Don't mistake an artifact of forced equivalence for a law of the world.

## Outward lesson

The durable edge is keeper 2. "Bad drives out good" is quoted as if it were a grim natural law, but it holds only under a specific, removable condition: a fixed rate that forbids the good thing from being valued for what it is. Under that peg, the good is hoarded and the bad circulates; lift the peg and the flow reverses. So before treating the dominance of a low-quality option as inevitable — in money, or in any market where a rule forces unlike things to trade as equals — ask whether a mandated equivalence is doing it. If it is, the fix isn't fighting the bad option directly; it's letting the good one be priced correctly, and the sorting inverts on its own.

## Verified / flagged

- **Solid:** Gresham's law (bad money drives out good) holds specifically under legal-tender/fixed-par regimes that rate two currencies of different intrinsic value equally; holders spend the overvalued (bad) and hoard/melt/export the undervalued (good). The precondition (compelled par) is essential. Without it, under free pricing, the reverse holds — **Thiers' law** (good drives out bad). The principle predates Gresham (Oresme 14th c., Copernicus 1519); "Gresham's law" was coined by Macleod (1858). Standard monetary economics/history.
- **Flag — the condition is load-bearing and usually omitted.** Stated bare ("bad money drives out good") the law is often *wrong*; it requires the fixed-rate/legal-tender compulsion. My finding leans on exactly this correction.
- **Flag — attribution.** Gresham didn't originate it and the surviving phrasing attributed to him is debated; it's a 19th-century naming of a much older observation. Don't credit Gresham as discoverer.
- **Flag — scope.** The generalization to any "fixed price on unequal quality" (keeper 1) is my extension by analogy; it's a real and common pattern (e.g., adverse selection has a related flavor), but Gresham's law proper is about money under a par mandate. Treat the broadening as illustrative.
