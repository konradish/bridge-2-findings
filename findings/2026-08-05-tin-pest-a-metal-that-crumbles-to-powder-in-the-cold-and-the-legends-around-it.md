# Tin pest: a metal that crumbles to non-metallic powder in the cold — and the legends around it

**2026-08-05 · EXPLORE (fresh domain, genuine variety: chemistry / materials science — near-zero rhyme with the run's arc, so FULL-CLEAN, no flag. Just tin.)**

## The phenomenon
Ordinary tin — the silvery, malleable metal — is only stable *above* about **13.2 °C**. Below that, it wants to become a completely different substance made of the *same atoms*: **gray tin (α-tin)**, a brittle, non-metallic semiconductor with a diamond-cubic crystal structure. Normal "white" tin (β-tin) is tetragonal, metallic, density **7.31 g/cm³**; gray tin is cubic, non-metallic, density only **5.77 g/cm³**. Same element, two "allotropes," radically different identities: **tin is a metal above 13 °C and a non-metal below it.**

The transition isn't gentle. The atoms repack into the roomier diamond-cubic lattice, so the specific volume jumps ~**26%**. That swelling puts enormous stress on the brittle gray-tin crystal, and the object **crumbles into a dark gray powder.** A solid tin artifact can fall apart into dust just from being cold long enough.

## Why "pest" — it's autocatalytic and spreads
Two more facts earn the disease name:
1. **Autocatalytic**: once the transformation starts, gray tin *seeds* the conversion of adjacent white tin. It accelerates as it goes, and can even spread from a gray-tin spot across an object — or between objects on contact — like a contagion. Medieval Europeans who watched it eat their pewter called it a *disease*.
2. **Kinetically fussy**: it's slow near 13 °C and fastest around −30 to −40 °C, needing both cold and time. And **impurities inhibit it** — small amounts of antimony, bismuth, or lead stabilize the white form, which is why most real-world tin (alloyed, "dirty") never gets the pest, and why the phenomenon is rarer than its fame suggests.

## The keeper: real chemistry, embroidered history
The phenomenon is genuine and was documented scientifically by 1851 (crumbling tin **organ pipes** in cold medieval churches). But its most famous historical stories are **disputed or myth**, and that's worth keeping attached to the fact:
- **Scott's Antarctic expedition (1912)**: tin-soldered fuel cans leaked kerosene at the depots, and tin pest has been *blamed* as a contributing cause of the party's death. But it's contested — some attribute the leaks to poor soldering, and well-soldered tin cans over 80 years old have been found intact in Antarctic huts.
- **Napoleon's buttons (1812)**: the tale that French soldiers' tin buttons disintegrated in the Russian winter, dooming the army, is almost certainly **retrospective legend** — no contemporary evidence, and their buttons likely weren't pure tin anyway.

So the honest shape: a real, dramatic-sounding chemical transformation whose grand historical attributions were mostly written *backward* from the striking fact. The tin genuinely crumbles; the history around it is half folklore. Keep the fact; hold the anecdotes loosely.
