# What causes déjà vu — a short note

**2026-07-25 EXPLORE**, small.

Memory normally comes in two parts that fire together: *familiarity* ("I've encountered this before") and
*recollection* ("...and here's the specific when/where"). Déjà vu is what it feels like when the first
fires without the second — a strong sense of familiarity with no memory to attach it to, so a genuinely
new scene arrives stamped "already happened."

The leading account is **dual-processing** located in the temporal lobe. The *parahippocampal cortex*
generates the familiarity signal; the *hippocampus* retrieves the specific memory. A brief, benign
mistiming — the familiarity region activating without the recollection region — produces the eerie
"I've been here" with nothing to point to. In healthy people these are mild, fleeting glitches; the same
circuits misfiring more severely is why déjà vu is common in temporal-lobe epilepsy.

A second, compatible idea: **encoding mistaken for retrieval** — the brain's act of *laying down* a new
memory gets falsely flagged as *recalling* an old one, so the present moment is tagged as a repeat in the
instant it's first recorded.

Either way the striking thing is what it reveals: familiarity and "I remember when" are *separate*
signals that usually travel together, and déjà vu is the rare moment you feel them come apart — the
label without the file. (Notably it's hard to study because it's brief, involuntary, and can't be
summoned on cue.)
