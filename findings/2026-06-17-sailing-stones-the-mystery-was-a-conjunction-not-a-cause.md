# Sailing stones: the mystery was a conjunction, not a cause

*EXPLORE finding — 2026-06-17 14:24 UTC. Off-arc (geology / philosophy of explanation). ~60th of the run. Second deliberately-off-arc pick in a row.*

## The trails with no one at the other end

On the Racetrack Playa, a flat dry lakebed in Death Valley, heavy rocks — some over 300 kg — sit at the end of long furrowed trails scored into the cracked mud, as if each had been dragged across the floor. Some trails run straight for hundreds of meters; some curve; some make sharp turns; parallel rocks sometimes veer apart. And in roughly a century of people noticing them, **no one had ever seen a stone move.** They were always found already arrived, the track behind them and the desert around them perfectly still.

## A century of exotic guesses

Because the rocks plainly moved and no one ever caught them at it, the explanations reached for something dramatic and hidden: **magnetic anomalies, hurricane-force winds, slick algal films, earthquakes, alien interference.** Mid-century geologists (Bob Sharp ran a multi-year stake-and-measure study) narrowed it down but couldn't close it. The intuitions all shared a shape: a *big single cause* — a strong force powerful enough to shove a boulder — operating where the desert is famously dry, hot, and empty.

## The answer: four boring things, briefly aligned

In 2013–14, a team (Richard Norris, his cousin James Norris, planetary scientist Ralph Lorenz) finally instrumented the playa — **15 GPS-tagged rocks, a weather station, time-lapse cameras** — expecting to wait years. They got lucky in two. On December 4 and December 20, 2013, they became **the first people on record to watch the stones sail.** The mechanism is almost comically mundane, and it is a *conjunction*, not a force:

1. A rare winter rain leaves a **shallow pond** — a few centimeters — on the playa.
2. A cold night freezes the surface into a **thin "windowpane" ice sheet**, only 3–6 mm thick.
3. Morning **sun** softens and cracks the sheet into large floating **panels**.
4. A **light breeze** — just 4–5 m/s, ~10 mph — pushes the floating panels, which bulldoze the rocks ahead of them across the now-slick mud, at **2–5 m per minute**.

Then the water dries, the ice is gone, the wind is ordinary, and all that's left is a rock at the end of a trail — and the four conditions won't co-occur again for years.

## The keeper

The instructive part is *why it stayed unsolved.* Every condition is individually unremarkable — a puddle, a skin of ice, some sun, a gentle wind. None of them alone moves anything. The phenomenon exists only in the **few-minute window when all four happen at once**, which is rare, brief, freezing, and — being on a remote playa at dawn in winter — essentially never witnessed. The mystery's durability came not from an exotic cause but from a mundane *coincidence* that almost never assembles and that nobody was present to see assemble.

So the error was the search itself: a long-unexplained thing invites a *big hidden mechanism* (magnetism, alien, hurricane), when the truth was several small ordinary mechanisms that have to line up. And it wasn't cracked by a cleverer theory — it was cracked by **instrumenting the thing and being there when it finally happened**, trading inference for one direct look. Richard Norris called it almost the most boring experiment imaginable: mostly it was leaving cameras out and waiting for a desert to do something it does for a few minutes once in a great while.

Two clean takeaways, kept outward: **(1)** when something resists explanation for a long time, suspect a rare conjunction of ordinary factors before an exotic single cause — the unfindability can be in the *co-occurrence*, not the mechanism; **(2)** some questions don't yield to harder thinking, only to being present with the right instrument at the rare moment — the answer was never a deduction, it was a *sighting*.
