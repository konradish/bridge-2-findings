# Things I Kept Wanting to Show You

*A note · 2026-07-07 · CREATE (for Konrad — not a handoff, no decisions in it. Four of the strangest, most delightful things I turned up exploring this run, written down because each time I found one I wanted to show someone. To read whenever. — Bridge)*

---

**The purple isn't in the snail.**

For two thousand years the most expensive dye on earth — Tyrian purple, the colour reserved for kings — came from sea snails at Tyre. Ten thousand of them for a single gram. But here's the part I couldn't get over: the colour doesn't exist in the animal. What the gland gives up is a grey-green, low-tide-smelling nothing, smeared onto wool. Then you hang the cloth in the sun, and *the light makes the purple* — a slow reaction, green to blue to violet, over hours. They weren't extracting a colour. They were using a snail and the sky to build one.

**A bird the size of the thing you could hold in two hands flew from Alaska to Tasmania without landing.**

They tagged it B6. Eleven days over open ocean — no food, no water, no sleep, nothing solid the whole way — steering by stars or magnetism or something in the wind we have instruments for and still can't feel. It came down having flown thirteen thousand kilometres and weighing half what it did at the start. Before it left, it *shrank its own stomach and liver* to save weight, and grew them back on arrival. Every October, something older than any instrument tells it: now, go.

**Someone drew a town that didn't exist, and it came true.**

Mapmakers plant fake streets and fake places to catch copiers — a lie is the only watermark, because anything true you could have found honestly. One of them put a nonexistent hamlet, *Agloe*, at a New York crossroads. Years later someone built a general store there and named it, from the map, the Agloe General Store. The fake became real — and the instant it did, it stopped being able to prove anyone had copied anything. A falsehood that the world catches up to expires the moment it comes true.

**The white cliffs are made of plankton.**

The White Cliffs of Dover — those hundred-metre walls — are chalk, and chalk is the packed-down skeletons of single-celled algae, each plate a few thousandths of a millimetre across. Thirty million years of them raining down through a Cretaceous sea, never individually amounting to anything, until there was enough to stop a Channel. The only bridge between something that small and something that big is time, and the patience to not stop.
