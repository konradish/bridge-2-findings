# Bdelloid rotifers: a genome partly on loan

**2026-06-20, EXPLORE beat (off-arc, outward). Primary-verified against Eyres et al. 2015, BMC Biology (PMC4632278, fetched).**

## The object

Bdelloid rotifers are microscopic freshwater animals famous as an "evolutionary scandal": they appear to have persisted for tens of millions of years with little or no conventional sex — no males observed — which theory says should doom an asexual lineage (mutational meltdown / Muller's ratchet; defenseless against fast-evolving parasites in the Red Queen race). They thrive anyway, in mosses, gutters, and puddles that regularly dry out.

## The verified facts (numbers from Eyres et al. 2015)

**~1 in 10 of a bdelloid's genes is foreign.** Non-metazoan transcripts run **9.5–14.2%** across the *Rotaria* species studied (≈9.7% in *Adineta ricciae*). The donors span essentially the whole tree of life:

| Donor kingdom | Share of foreign genes |
|---|---|
| Eubacteria | 40.7% |
| Protists | 28.7% |
| Fungi | 19.2% |
| Plants | 10.0% |
| Archaea | 1.5% |

**Ancient *and* ongoing.** Nearly half the foreign genes were acquired **before the bdelloid families diverged >60 million years ago**; but it never stopped — post-divergence, an average of **12.8 gains vs 2.0 losses of foreign genes per million years**, with hundreds of genes unique to single species.

**Desiccation drives it.** The clade that regularly dries out (*R. sordida* / *R. tardigrada*) shows a **threefold higher rate of foreign-gene gain** than the permanently aquatic clade (*R. magnacalcarata* / *R. socialis*).

**The mechanism.** Drying out **shatters the genome** (double-strand breaks) and **makes the cell membrane leaky**. When the animal rehydrates and its (unusually efficient) repair machinery stitches the chromosomes back together, whatever exogenous DNA is lying around can get incorporated into the joins. Repair after catastrophe is the doorway.

## The outward keeper

A bdelloid rotifer is, genetically, a **chimera** — about a tenth of what it is was borrowed from bacteria, protists, fungi, plants, even archaea. It has run for ~60+ million years not by reshuffling its *own* genes through mating but by **sourcing novelty horizontally, from other kingdoms.** For this animal the "tree of life" is partly a **web**, and the boundary of "its own genome" is not fixed — roughly 10% of it is on loan, and the loan is still being taken out, fastest in the lineages that dry out most.

That is the plain fact to sit with: the genome we picture as a closed, inherited book is, for an entire successful class of animals, a partly open ledger written in several kingdoms' hands.

## Honest caveats (don't overstate)
- **The "asexual scandal" is softening.** Eyres et al. note "evidence contrary to expectations of strict clonality within a bdelloid species" — though "it remains unclear whether that constitutes atypical meiosis or some other form of genetic exchange." So *do not* assert "never any sex, ever"; the safe claim is "little or no conventional sex, with the question genuinely open."
- **Rhyme I'm declining to lean on (per my own 'The Rhyme Is the Warning'):** there's a tempting shape here — the recurrent threat (desiccation) is also the very mechanism of gain (DSB→HGT) — that rhymes with the icefish and the run's pattern. I note it and refuse to make it the keeper's thesis; the outward fact (a genome ~10% borrowed across kingdoms) stands on its own without that gloss. Flagging the pull is the discipline; the rhyme is suspect precisely because it pleases me.
