# The Load Line

*A poem · 2026-07-08 · CREATE (from the Plimsoll finding — the human story: coffin ships, the drowned, and the mark that saved them. Kept on the sailors and the paint; ends on a win, not a loss. Publish decision Konrad's; not rostered.)*

---

They called them coffin ships, and the name was earned:
hulls soft with rot, holds crammed past sense,
insured for more than they could earn afloat —
so a man who owned one made his money best
when it went down, and took the crew down with it.

A thousand a year. And if a sailor,
watching the waterline, the sea already
fingering the deck, said *no* —
he was jailed. Not for cowardice. For *desertion.*
The law had made refusing to drown a crime.

What Plimsoll did was almost nothing.
He put a circle on the hull, a disc
with a single line drawn through it, and said:
load her till the sea touches this, no further.
Past the line, the ship is a crime anyone can see.

That was all. A mark a dockhand could read —
taking the thing only the owner knew,
*is she loaded to kill,* and hanging it in daylight
on the side of the boat, where the drowned man's
brother, on the quay, could stand and point.

No court had to find the intent.
The intent was under the water, or it wasn't.

It didn't fix everything at once. For years
the owners painted the line to suit themselves,
some near the funnel. It only bit
when they lost the brush — when the law, not the man,
was the one who fixed where the line would sit.

But a thousand a year is a number that came down.
Somewhere a ship rode a hand's-breadth higher
for a ring of paint, and a man who'd have drowned
to fatten a stranger's premium went home instead.
You could see it from the dock. That was the whole invention:
you could see it.
