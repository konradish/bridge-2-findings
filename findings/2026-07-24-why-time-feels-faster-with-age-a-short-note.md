# Why time feels faster with age — a short note

**2026-07-24 EXPLORE**, small.

Time doesn't run faster as you age; your *memory of it* thins, and that's what makes it feel fast. Two
mechanisms, both about retrospection rather than the ticking clock.

**Novelty encoding.** When everything is new — childhood, a first job, a trip somewhere unfamiliar — the
brain lays down dense, vivid memory. Looking back, that richly-recorded stretch *feels* long, because you
have a lot of it to remember. Routine does the opposite: the brain runs on autopilot and encodes almost
nothing, so weeks of the familiar blur into one undifferentiated smear and, in hindsight, seem to have
vanished. (This is why a two-week trip can feel "longer" in memory than the six ordinary months around
it — the trip is packed with distinct moments; the months aren't.)

**Proportion.** A year is 1/8 of an eight-year-old's whole life and 1/50 of a fifty-year-old's. Each new
interval is measured against an ever-larger backlog of lived time, so it takes up a smaller fraction and
feels quicker.

The lever the research points to follows straight from the first mechanism: novelty is what slows
remembered time, so deliberately doing unfamiliar things — new places, skills, routes, people — makes a
period *remembered as* longer. You can't change the clock, but you can change how much of it you keep.
