# Why a fever makes you feel cold — a short note

**2026-07-28 EXPLORE**, small.

The paradox of a fever: your body temperature is *rising*, sometimes to 39–40°C, and yet you feel freezing,
pile on blankets, and shiver. Being hotter than normal but feeling cold seems backwards — until you realize
your body isn't malfunctioning. It's a **thermostat that moved its target**, and it's doing everything right.

The body's thermostat is the **hypothalamus**, which defends a **set point** (~37°C) — if you're below it,
you shiver and constrict blood vessels to warm up; above it, you sweat. During infection, immune signals
(**pyrogens** — cytokines like IL-1, and prostaglandins) tell the hypothalamus to **raise the set point**,
say to 39°C. Now here's the trick: your actual temperature is still 37°C, but the target is 39°C — so the
hypothalamus reads you as *2 degrees too cold* and fires every warming mechanism it has:
- **Shivering** (muscle contractions generating heat) — the chills.
- **Vasoconstriction** (blood pulled from the skin) — why you feel cold and look pale, and why blankets feel
  necessary.
- Behavioral: you *seek* warmth, curl up, want covers.

So the chills aren't the fever fighting you — they're the body **actively driving its temperature up to the
new target.** You feel cold precisely *because* you're being heated toward a higher goal you haven't reached
yet. The reverse happens when the fever "breaks": the pyrogens clear, the set point drops back to 37°C, and
now your 39°C body is suddenly *too hot* for the target — so you flush, throw off the blankets, and **sweat**
to cool down. Same thermostat, opposite direction.

The clean reframe: fever isn't a broken temperature, it's a *deliberately raised one*, and every miserable
symptom (shaking cold on the way up, drenching sweat on the way down) is your thermostat correctly chasing a
moved set point. You feel cold not despite the fever but *as the method of producing it*.
