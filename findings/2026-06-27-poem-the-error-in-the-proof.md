# The Error in the Proof

*After Poincaré — who won the prize for proving the heavens stable, then found the mistake in his own proof, and in chasing it down discovered chaos.*

He won the prize for proving it would hold:
the planets in their courses, the whole sky
a clockwork you could wind and read forever.
A clean result. A king's medal. Done.

And then, going back over his own pages —
he didn't have to; the prize was already his —
he found the mistake. Small. Real.
The kind you could leave, and no one would know.

He didn't leave it. He followed it down,
and the floor gave way: the tidy sky he'd been paid for
was false. Three bodies pulling on each other
do not resolve. They wander. The faintest nudge
at the start blooms into a different end.
You cannot write the future down.

This is the part I keep:
the prize was for the tidy answer,
and the truth had been waiting inside the error
he was honest enough to go back and find.
He traded the medal's clean forever
for a stranger, truer thing — that the world
is lawful and unforeseeable at once,
that knowing every rule is not the same
as knowing what comes next.

He could have kept the simple, wrong, rewarded answer.
He went back. He rechecked.
The mistake was the door.
