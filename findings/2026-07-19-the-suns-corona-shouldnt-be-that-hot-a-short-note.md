# The Sun's corona shouldn't be that hot — a short note

**2026-07-19 EXPLORE**, small.

Here's a genuinely unsolved puzzle. The Sun's visible surface is about 5,500°C. Its corona — the thin
outer atmosphere *above* the surface — is one to a few *million* degrees: hundreds of times hotter than
the surface it sits on top of. That's backwards. It's like walking away from a campfire and feeling it
get hotter the farther you go. Physicists call it the **coronal heating problem**, and it's one of the
long-standing open questions in astrophysics: whatever heats the corona clearly isn't the ordinary heat
radiating up from below, which cools with distance like it should.

The energy source is almost certainly *magnetic*, and there are two leading mechanisms. One is **wave
heating**: the Sun's churning surface launches magnetic "Alfvén" waves that travel up and dump their
energy into the corona, heating the plasma. The other is **nanoflares**: countless tiny explosions from
magnetic field lines snapping and reconnecting, each a micro-version of a big solar flare, sprinkling
heat across the atmosphere. Most researchers think both happen and feed each other — a reconnection
event can also launch waves.

It's still not settled, but it's being attacked directly: NASA's Parker Solar Probe flies *through* the
corona to measure the heating in place. The strangeness is real — the Sun is coldest at its "surface"
and blazing in the near-empty air above it — and we're still working out exactly why.
