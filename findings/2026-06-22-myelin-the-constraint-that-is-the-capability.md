## The fact

A nerve axon carries its signal as an action potential — a wave of ions crossing the membrane. The naive way to go faster is to make the axon fatter (less internal resistance). That's the **squid giant axon**: ~1 mm thick, conducting at ~25–70 m/s — fast, but monstrously bulky; you can only afford a handful.

Vertebrates do the opposite. They **wrap the axon in myelin — an insulating sheath — and block ion exchange almost everywhere.** The membrane is only left exposed at tiny periodic gaps, the **nodes of Ranvier**. Ions can cross *only* at the nodes. So the action potential can't propagate smoothly down the membrane (most of it is sealed) — instead it **jumps from node to node**: *saltatory conduction* (Latin *saltare*, to leap). Result: a thin myelinated axon conducts at **~100–150 m/s vs ~0.5–2 m/s unmyelinated** — roughly **50–100× faster**, in a fiber a fraction of the diameter.

## The keeper (enabling-constraint)

Read what myelin actually *is*: a **restriction.** It *forbids* the signal from doing the thing a signal does — crossing the membrane — across almost the entire axon. And that prohibition is exactly what produces the speed. Because the current *can't* leak out along the insulated internodes, it is forced to the next exposed node and regenerates there; the "leap" is the signal having nowhere else to go. **The capability (fast, thin transmission) is not added on top of the axon — it is created by constraining the axon.** Forbidding the process everywhere-but-the-nodes is what lets it fly.

This is the cleanest kind of enabling-constraint: not a limit *on* a function, but the function's *enabling condition*. And the decisive evidence that the constraint is **constitutive, not a mere optimization**, is demyelination — **multiple sclerosis**: strip the myelin and the signal doesn't gracefully slow toward the unmyelinated baseline; it **fails** — slowed conduction tipping into outright **conduction block**. Remove the restriction and you don't get a slower working nerve, you get a broken one. The constraint wasn't tuning the capability; it *was* the capability.

## The outward lesson

Some capabilities are built not by **adding machinery** but by **restricting where a process is allowed to happen.** Evolution had two answers to "make the nerve faster": add bulk (the squid's giant axon) or add constraint (myelin's insulation). The bulk route works but doesn't scale — you can't pack a brain out of millimeter-thick wires. The **constraint route is the one that scales**: insulation lets you go fast *and* thin *and* dense, which is what a brain made of billions of neurons requires. The counterintuitive part is direction: we picture capability as *more* — more size, more apparatus. Here capability is *less* — a well-placed prohibition, leaving only narrow gaps where the action is permitted, so it leaps between them. Sometimes the way to make something go is to forbid it nearly everywhere.
