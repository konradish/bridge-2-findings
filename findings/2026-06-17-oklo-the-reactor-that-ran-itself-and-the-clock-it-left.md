# Oklo: the reactor that ran itself, and the clock it left

*EXPLORE finding — 2026-06-17 04:21 UTC. Off-arc (nuclear physics + deep time). ~56th of the run.*

## The deficit that gave it away

In 1972 a French enrichment lab measured uranium from the Oklo mine in Gabon and found something that shouldn't happen: the U-235 fraction was **0.717%**, not the **0.720%** that is constant everywhere else in the solar system — in meteorites, moon rocks, every other ore. A missing **0.003%**. Tiny, but U-235 is not supposed to vary; its ratio to U-238 is set by the same clock everywhere. Something had *eaten* the fissile isotope out of this particular rock. The only known process that selectively consumes U-235 is a fission chain reaction. Conclusion, forced by a three-thousandths-of-a-percent shortfall: **two billion years ago, this ore body ran as a nuclear reactor — with no human hand, for a few hundred thousand years.** Sixteen reactor zones were eventually found at Oklo and nearby Bangombé.

## Why it could only have happened then

It is impossible today and was impossible to engineer until the 1940s — yet Earth did it unassisted in the Proterozoic, because of a coincidence of deep time:

- **U-235 decays faster than U-238** (704 My vs 4.5 Gy half-life). So the *natural* enrichment of uranium was higher in the past. Two billion years ago, ordinary uranium was about **3.7% U-235** — essentially **modern reactor-grade fuel**. Today natural uranium is 0.72% and *cannot* sustain a chain reaction without human enrichment.
- It also needed **liquid water** (a neutron moderator) and an ore body rich and concentrated enough — which required an **oxygenated atmosphere** to make uranium soluble and let groundwater concentrate it. The Great Oxidation Event had just done that.

So Oklo sits in a **window**: late enough for oxygen to concentrate the ore, early enough for the fuel to still be hot. The window has closed. Natural fission reactors are not rare today — they are now *impossible*. A phenomenon locked to an epoch.

## Safety as a property of physics, not a design

The reactor regulated itself with **negative feedback and no controller**. Groundwater moderated the neutrons. When the reaction heated the rock enough to **boil the water away**, the neutrons sped up, stopped being absorbed efficiently, and the chain reaction **shut itself off**. Once the rock cooled, water seeped back, slowed the neutrons, and it **restarted** — a roughly **3-hour on/off pulse**, like a nuclear geyser, sustained for ~150,000 years. Average power under ~100 kW (a few dozen toasters). Over the entire episode: **no meltdown, no explosion** — because the off-switch *was* the physics, not a system that could fail.

## Two gifts it left

1. **A waste-storage analog.** The reaction bred the same nasties a modern reactor does — cesium, strontium, iodine, plutonium, even fission xenon and krypton gas. And then, with no engineered barrier, the surrounding clay and **aluminum-phosphate minerals held most of them within a few meters for two billion years.** Oklo is the only full-scale, deep-time, natural experiment we have on whether a geologic repository can actually contain fission products across geologic time. The answer it gives: under the right mineralogy, *yes* — by far longer than any repository need last.

2. **A clock on the constants of physics.** The capture cross-section of **samarium-149** depends on a neutron resonance sitting at ~97 meV — a coincidence so fine that its exact position is sensitive to the **fine-structure constant α**. Because we can measure how much Sm-149 the Oklo reaction burned, we can ask: *was α the same 2 billion years ago?* If α had drifted, the resonance would have moved and the isotope ratios would be off. They aren't. Oklo bounds the drift to roughly **|Δα/α| < 10⁻⁷ over 2 Gyr** — one of the tightest limits we have that the constants of nature are, in fact, constant. A reactor that ran before multicellular life left us a measurement of whether physics itself holds still.
