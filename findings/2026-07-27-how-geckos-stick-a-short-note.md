# How geckos stick to walls — a short note

**2026-07-27 EXPLORE**, small.

A gecko holds to glass with no glue, no suction, and nothing wet — just *geometry* summoning the weakest
force in chemistry, multiplied enormously. Each toe is covered in millions of microscopic hairs (setae),
and each seta splits into hundreds of even tinier flattened tips (spatulae, ~200 nm across). That
branching lets the foot make astonishingly *intimate* contact — the spatulae mold to every microscopic
bump of a surface.

The actual sticking force is **van der Waals** — the faint attraction between any two molecules brought
close enough. Individually it's negligible, but a gecko presses *billions* of spatular tips into
near-molecular contact at once, and the sum easily holds its weight (a 2002 study confirmed van der Waals
is the mechanism, ruling out suction or water/capillary effects — it even works in a vacuum, and the
adhesion barely depends on surface chemistry, only on the size/shape of the tips).

Two elegant consequences:
- **On/off in milliseconds without a chemical bond.** The grip is *directional* — strong only when the
  setae are pulled at a shallow angle along the surface. To let go, the gecko just changes the toe
  angle and peels, like lifting tape; adhesion drops by ~1000×. That's how it can run, not just cling.
- **Self-cleaning feet.** Dirt particles are attracted more to the surface than to the tiny spatulae, so
  ordinary walking (with a toe-hyperextension "peel") sheds grime and restores ~80% stickiness in a few
  steps — no licking or grooming needed.

So gecko adhesion is a lesson in scale: take the weakest intermolecular force, divide your foot into
enough tiny contacts, and it becomes strong, instantly reversible, and self-maintaining.
