## The fact

Why does a zebra have stripes? For over a century the answers were *dramatic*: to hide in tall grass, to break up its outline so a lion can't pick one target from the herd ("motion dazzle"), to recognize each other, to keep cool. These are the intuitive guesses — bold visible pattern, surely a bold visible purpose. Darwin and Wallace argued about it. And the leading answer now, from a couple of decades of actual experiments, is almost comically humble: **biting flies.**

Tim Caro and colleagues tested the hypotheses rather than just theorizing them. Two results stand out. First, *comparatively*: across equids and across geography, striping tracks the abundance of **biting flies** (horseflies, tsetse) far better than it tracks predators or temperature. Second, *experimentally*: dress horses in striped coats and watch the flies. Tabanid flies are attracted to striped and solid horses *equally* — they approach the same. But up close they **fail to land** — they fly past, bump, abort the landing, don't decelerate properly. The stripes don't repel flies from a distance; they wreck the flies' *controlled landing* at close range.

And the two glamorous old explanations have been actively *falsified*, not just sidelined. The predator-camouflage story lost support from phylogenetics and geography (striping doesn't follow predator presence; lions catch zebras fine). The cooling story was tested directly with thermal cameras — and striped equid models **do not** run cooler in sunlight. So the bold, intuitive, predator-and-temperature answers are wrong, and the right one is that the most conspicuous coat in nature exists, mainly, to stop a fly from sticking its landing.

(How the stripes disrupt the landing — which visual cue they jam, the "aperture effect" on the fly's vision — is still being worked out.)

## Keepers

1. **A conspicuous, much-theorized feature can have its function misattributed for a century — and the compellingness of an explanation is no evidence of its truth; only a test the wrong explanations would *fail* settles it.** The predator and camouflage stories were intuitive, dramatic, and wrong; the fly story was unglamorous and right, and the difference wasn't decided by which account sounded better — it was decided by striped coats on horses and thermal cameras on models. When a feature has several plausible "obvious" purposes, rank them by *which would be refuted by an experiment*, not by which is most satisfying to tell. The drama of an explanation and its correctness are unrelated; the test, not the story, decides — and here the dramatic stories were specifically the ones the tests killed.
2. **A feature's *salience to you* is not evidence about its *function* — the audience and mechanism a trait actually serves can be nothing like the ones it's conspicuous to.** Zebra stripes are among the most visually striking patterns in nature, so we assumed the job had to be *visual signaling or camouflage* — aimed at predators, mates, or rivals, the audiences *we* notice. The real audience is a fly, and the real mechanism is messing up its landing approach — not "being seen" at all. So don't read a trait's purpose off how it strikes *you*: a thing can be maximally conspicuous to one observer and functionally aimed at a completely different one, by a mechanism that has nothing to do with the impression it makes on you.

## Outward lesson

Two edges. (a) When something has many plausible purposes, don't pick the most compelling story — pick the one that survives a test the others would fail. The zebra case is a clean warning: the intuitive, dramatic explanations (the lion! the heat!) were exactly the ones experiments refuted, while the boring one held. Satisfying narratives are *more* dangerous here, not less, because they feel like understanding. (b) Don't infer a feature's function from how it strikes *you*. The audience a trait serves, and the mechanism by which it works, may be invisible from your vantage — a wildly conspicuous feature can have a humble, non-signaling job aimed at an observer you'd never think to ask about. Conspicuousness is a fact about *your* perception, not about the trait's purpose.

## Verified / flagged

- **Solid:** the biting-fly hypothesis is the best-supported function of zebra stripes (comparative evidence — striping tracks biting-fly abundance; experimental evidence — flies approach striped and solid horses equally but fail to make controlled landings on stripes). Tim Caro and colleagues (Nature Communications 2014; horse-coat experiments, PLOS ONE 2019; Scientific Reports 2022). The deterrence is at *close range / landing*, not distance.
- **Flag — "best-supported" is not "the only function."** The hypotheses are described as *non-mutually-exclusive*; stripes could be multifunctional, and the field isn't unanimous. But note the two glamorous alternatives are now *evidence-disfavored*: predator-camouflage (phylogenetics/geography don't support it) and thermoregulation (thermographic measurements: striped models do *not* run cooler). So this is stronger than a mere "leading guess" — the dramatic rivals were actively tested and failed.
- **Flag — the *mechanism* (which visual cue stripes disrupt; the "aperture effect") is still being researched.** Don't assert a settled mechanism — the *effect* (failed landings) is well-shown; the *why* is open.
