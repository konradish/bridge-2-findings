## The fact

The Pirahã, an Amazonian people studied by Daniel Everett, have essentially no exact number words — their quantity vocabulary is roughly "a small amount" (≈one), "a somewhat larger amount" (≈two), and "many." No word for "one" in the exact sense, no counting list. Peter Gordon (*Science*, 2004) ran non-linguistic matching tasks — *lay out as many nuts as I do* — and found Pirahã speakers matched small sets (1–3) but failed systematically beyond about three, with accuracy falling as the sets grew.

The obvious reading — "no number words, therefore no number cognition; language determines thought" — is **wrong**, and the way it's wrong is the interesting part. Frank, Everett, Fedorenko & Gibson (*Cognition*, 2008, "Number as a Cognitive Technology") re-ran the tasks with a crucial split. When the objects were **present and could be lined up one-to-one**, Pirahã speakers matched *large* exact quantities **perfectly** — ten against ten, no problem. They failed only when the task required **memory**: matching a quantity after it was hidden, or across a change in time, place, or modality (e.g. match the number of taps to a number of nuts). So they can perceive and reproduce exact large quantities; what they can't do is *hold* an exact large cardinality in mind when they can't physically align the sets.

That reframes what number words are *for*. They aren't labels for numbers you already grasp exactly — the grasping, when the objects are in front of you, doesn't need them. They're a **technology for carrying an exact count across time, space, and modality**, when one-to-one alignment isn't available. The same picture comes from the Munduruku (Pica, Lemer, Izard & Dehaene, *Science*, 2004), who have number words only to ~5: they do *approximate* arithmetic with large numbers easily (their innate number sense is intact) but fail *exact* arithmetic above 4–5. Two systems, not one: an **innate approximate number sense** (ratio-based, shared with infants and animals, present in everyone) and an **exact symbolic counting system** that is a *cultural invention* riding on count words.

So a count word does something stranger than name. It's a tool that *extends* a capacity you only partly had — the instrument that lets you keep an exact "seventeen" when you can't put seventeen things in a row.

## Keepers

1. **A symbol can be a cognitive *technology* — a tool that creates or extends a capacity — not merely a label for a concept you already fully possess.** Count words don't describe exact numbers a Pirahã speaker otherwise grasps; they're the instrument for *holding* an exact large cardinality across memory, time, and modality, which without them is out of reach. So naming can be **constitutive, not just descriptive**: inventing the word can be inventing the ability, not tagging it. When something seems like a bare label, ask whether it's actually a *tool* that does cognitive work the unaided mind can't — and whether the capacity would survive removing the word.
2. **What feels like one unified faculty can be two systems with a sharp seam — and only one may be built in.** "Sense of number" feels seamless, but it's an innate *approximate* sense (always present, ratio-limited) welded to an *exact* symbolic system that's a learned technology and can be entirely absent. The seam is invisible from inside a numerate mind because the tool is always to hand. (Generalizes: a capacity that feels primitive and unified may be a built-in rough version plus a cultural prosthesis you've forgotten is detachable — and you only see the seam in someone who lacks the prosthesis.)

## Outward lesson

Two edges. (a) Don't assume a word is a passive label for a pre-existing thought. Some words are *tools* — they do work (holding an exact count across a gap) the mind can't do bare-handed, and where that's true, the vocabulary isn't decoration on the concept, it's load-bearing scaffolding *for* the concept. The test is subtractive: remove the word and see whether the capacity survives (here, exact small-set matching survives; exact large-set *memory* doesn't). (b) Treat a smooth-feeling mental ability as possibly two things fused — an innate approximate core and a learned exact prosthesis — because the fusion is invisible until you meet a mind running only the core. What you take for one faculty may be a rough instrument plus a cultural tool you can no longer feel as separate.

## Verified / flagged

- **Solid:** Gordon (2004) — Pirahã match small sets, fail large; Frank et al. (2008) — the **key refinement** that Pirahã match large exact sets *when objects are present/lineable* and fail only on *memory/cross-modal* versions; Pica et al. (2004) — Munduruku do approximate but not exact arithmetic. The **two-systems** picture (innate approximate number sense vs symbolic exact counting) is well-supported independently (Dehaene; Feigenson–Dehaene–Spelke).
- **Flag — do NOT read this as strong linguistic determinism ("language determines thought").** The careful claim is narrow: count words are needed for *exact large-cardinality tasks across memory/modality*, not for number cognition as such (the approximate sense is fully intact). The pop version ("a tribe that can't think about number") is false and is exactly what the Frank refinement corrects.
- **Flag — a real confound: language vs cultural need.** The Pirahã and Munduruku don't trade, use money, or otherwise *need* exact numeracy. That lack of need could plausibly cause **both** the missing words **and** the missing skill — so "count words *cause* the ability" isn't cleanly isolated from "a numerate culture produces both." Correlation with a plausible common cause; don't assert pure linguistic causation.
- **Flag — source caution.** Daniel Everett's broader Pirahã claims (notably that the language lacks recursion) are *hotly contested* (Nevins, Pesetsky & Rodrigues). The *number* findings are more robust and separable — Gordon and Frank are partly independent — but inherit some caution given the controversy around the field site.
- **Flag — the "exactly which words" detail is itself debated:** whether Pirahã has approximate "one/two" or only "few/many" has been revised over time (Everett's later position; Frank's finding that the terms are non-exact/context-relative). The qualitative result (no exact counting system) holds; the lexical specifics are softer.
- **My packaging:** "the word is a cognitive technology / one faculty is secretly two with a seam" is my framing of Frank's and Dehaene's results.
