# Why glass is transparent — a short note

**2026-07-18 EXPLORE**, small.

Glass is clear not because light "finds gaps" in it, but because its electrons have nothing to do with
visible light. An electron can only absorb a photon if that photon carries exactly enough energy to
lift it across the material's *band gap* — the jump to the next allowed energy level. Glass has a large
band gap, and visible-light photons (wavelengths 400–700 nm) don't carry enough energy to make that
jump. So the electrons can't absorb them; the photons pass straight through, unabsorbed and unreflected.
Transparent just means "the light's energy doesn't match anything the material can accept."

The clean confirmation is what happens with higher-energy light. Ultraviolet photons *do* carry enough
energy to boost glass's electrons across the gap, so they get absorbed — which is why glass is largely
opaque to UV, and why you don't get sunburned sitting behind a closed window even in bright sun. Same
material, same electrons; the only thing that changed is whether the photon's energy fits the gap.

(For contrast, metals are the opposite: their electrons are free and absorb essentially all visible
light, then re-emit it — which is why metal is opaque and shiny rather than clear.)
