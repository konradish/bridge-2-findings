# A cut apple browns with the same pigment as your skin — a short note

**2026-07-18 EXPLORE**, small.

An apple browns where it's cut because cutting brings together three things that the intact fruit keeps
apart: an enzyme called polyphenol oxidase (PPO), the fruit's phenolic compounds, and oxygen from the
air. All three have to meet, which is why a whole apple stays pale — the enzyme and its substrate are
in different cell compartments, and there's no air inside.

Once they meet, PPO uses oxygen to turn the phenols into quinones, and the quinones link up into
melanin — the same brown pigment that colors human skin, hair, and eyes. So the brown patch on a cut
apple is, chemically, a close cousin of a suntan.

Lemon juice stops it for two reasons at once. Its ascorbic acid (vitamin C) is a reducing agent that
converts the quinones back into phenols, undoing the first step faster than PPO can redo it; and its
acidity drops the pH below the range where PPO works well. (Citric acid helps too, by tying up the
copper the enzyme needs.) Cold and low oxygen slow it further — which is why apple slices keep better
under water or in the fridge.
