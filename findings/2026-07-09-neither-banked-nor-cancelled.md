## The fact

Make an activity safer and people often don't bank the safety as fewer injuries — they spend some of it as bolder behavior, adjusting back toward the level of risk they were already willing to tolerate. This is **risk compensation**. The striking cases are real:

- Drivers with **antilock brakes and airbags** drive faster and follow closer.
- A 2009 study of NASCAR's **HANS head-and-neck restraint** found *fewer serious injuries but more crashes and car damage* — the protection was partly spent on harder racing.
- Give a cyclist a **helmet** and (some studies find) he rides less cautiously — and cars pass him closer.

Sam **Peltzman** (1975) made the sharp version: auto-safety regulation, he argued, was offset so completely by riskier driving that it barely reduced highway deaths. Gerald Wilde pushed it further into **risk homeostasis** — the claim that people defend a fixed internal risk *setpoint*, so any exogenous safety gain is fully cancelled by behavior.

## The correction that is the actual finding

Here is the part that matters, because the sharp version is **wrong** and its wrongness has cost real lives. The strong claim — full offset, a defended setpoint — is **not supported by the evidence.** Seatbelts net-save lives. Helmets net-reduce head injuries. The consensus (Hedlund, 2000) is that risk compensation is **partial and conditional**: it shows up when the safety measure is *visible*, *perceived as effective*, *motivating*, and *under the person's control* — and is largely absent otherwise. It is a leak, not a cancellation.

And the seductive strong version is genuinely dangerous: the same "but people will just take more risks" logic has been used to argue against bike helmets, seatbelt laws, HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis, masks, and vaccines — cases where the compensation is small or absent and the intervention plainly helps. The contrarian read kills people by talking them out of protections that work.

## Keepers

**1. (fact-primary) A benefit routed through a system that can change its own behavior is neither fully delivered nor fully cancelled — it partially leaks, and the size of the leak is conditional.** The naive model ("safer equipment ⇒ proportionally fewer harms") ignores that the actors adapt. The contrarian model ("they'll just compensate, so it's pointless") assumes the adaptation is total. Both are lazy: one sets the leak to zero, the other to 100%. The truth is a *measured fraction* that depends on named conditions. The discipline isn't to pick a side — it's to **measure the leak and stop assuming its size.**

**2. (the sharper, less comfortable half) The direction of the error is not symmetric in cost.** Underestimating the leak wastes some of a safety investment. Overestimating it — believing the strong story — talks you out of the intervention entirely. When a partial, real effect gets inflated into a total one, it flips from "spend a little more to cover the leak" into "don't bother," and the contrarian's cleverness becomes lethal. A true-but-partial mechanism is most dangerous exactly when it's stated as a law.
