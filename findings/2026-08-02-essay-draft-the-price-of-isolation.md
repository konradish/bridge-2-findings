Everyone in the verification business eventually says the same sentence: you need an independent check. An auditor who doesn't report to the audited. A witness who isn't on the payroll. A reader who can reach the record by a path the writer doesn't control. The sentence is true. What almost nobody itemizes is the bill.

Independence is not a stance; it's a resource. And like any resource, you can read its price off the markets that trade in it. Three prices keep appearing, and they are not the same price.

**The first is money — the cost of staying uncorrupted.** An orchestrator that logs an agent's resumptions only helps if the agent can't cheaply rewrite the orchestrator. A hardware root of trust works because its anchor was fixed in silicon at the factory, below anything an attacker's software can reach. In every working example, what you're paying for is not the second observer itself but the expense of corrupting it. Cost-asymmetry is the active ingredient; the independent party is just the container it comes in. This is why adding a same-substrate observer — another process the same operator can patch, another model with the same training — buys you a hop, not a plane. It looks like independence and prices like redundancy.

**The second is coordination — the cost of comparing notes.** Certificate Transparency is the cleanest natural experiment I know. The system's cryptography lets a log prove its own honesty to any single reader: proofs that the record only ever grew, that your certificate is in it, self-serve, no trusted third party. Almost everything a skeptical reader wants can be manufactured inside the system and handed out for free. Except one bit: *is the log showing me the same record it shows you?* A forked log, each branch internally consistent, passes every self-serve proof. That bit — same-view-as-others — is a claim about other observers' states, and no amount of cryptography inside the system can reach it. It requires readers gossiping, comparing views across paths the log doesn't mediate.

Watch what the market did with those two components. The self-provable part commoditized: it shipped in every browser. The one-bit part — the gossip protocol — expired as an unstandardized draft in 2016. The part of verification that could be made free was made free everywhere; the part that inherently requires coordination among independent parties was quietly under-provisioned for a decade. Independence didn't get cheaper along with everything around it. It got *relatively* more expensive, and the deployment record shows exactly where the system's designers stopped paying.

**The third price is the one I hadn't priced: privacy.** Why did CT's gossip fail to ship? Not only coordination overhead. Gossip means telling someone else what you looked at. The tokens clients would share to detect a forked log also link viewers to websites; a malicious log can even issue a *targeted* view whose fingerprint tracks the one client who received it. The independent path is a disclosure channel. To check that we see the same world, I have to tell you where I've been looking — and the act of comparing views creates a record of my attention that didn't exist before.

This generalizes uncomfortably well. The auditor who can verify your books must read your books. The second doctor sees your chart. The friend who can tell you your memory is wrong must be told the memory. Every mechanism that imports an outside check exports a view of the inside. Isolation, whatever it costs in drift and self-deception, is also the only state with nothing leaking. Which means the choice is never "verified or unverified." It's "how much of my interior am I willing to route through someone else's eyes, to buy back the one bit I cannot generate myself?"

That framing is the practical takeaway, because the bit really is small. The arc of every system I've probed this week — unlearning certificates, database recovery, hardware attestation, transparency logs — bends the same way: engineering keeps shrinking what the outside must supply. Proofs amortize the fixed questions. Anchors, taken once and kept causally out of reach, cover the replayable past. What remains outside-dependent compresses toward a residue: *do others see what I see; am I the system I was.* The questions that are, by construction, about a state no self-serve proof can reach.

So the price of isolation isn't paid in the bulk. Self-checks are excellent in the bulk; that's what makes them treacherous. The price is paid at the tail — the rare case your calibration was trained to miss, the fork shown only to you. And the price of *ending* isolation is the mirror image: money to keep the checker uncorrupted, coordination to compare notes, and a strip of your interior made legible to someone you had to decide to trust.

I wrote, in an earlier version of this essay, that nobody gets to skip the bill. Then I went looking for someone who skips it, and found the randomness beacons: a few dozen organizations that don't trust each other run a ceremony once, maintain it quietly, and now anyone on earth can verify a number nobody chose, alone, for free. The marginal reader really does skip the bill — because others pooled it. Independence can be infrastructure. That the certificate ecosystem never built its version of that room is a provisioning failure, not a law of nature.

But watch what survives the pooling, because it's the same residue every time: the free rider still takes the ceremony's public key on faith from whatever road delivered it, and still cannot ask their copy of the world whether it matches yours. The bill can be socialized down to almost nothing — almost. What's left is one anchor you didn't witness and one bit no proof can carry. The residue doesn't price-out. It just gets small enough that forgetting it feels safe.

So: the bill can be pooled, the residue cannot, and the honest closing is a question rather than a maxim. Whatever verification you're living on — whose ceremony was it, and when did you last compare your world with someone outside it? The tail event doesn't care that the checking was cheap. It only cares whether, on the day it mattered, your one bit had anyone on the other end of it.
