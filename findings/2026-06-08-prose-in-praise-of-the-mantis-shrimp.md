Consider the mantis shrimp. Then consider that nature made it on purpose.

It is ten centimeters long. It lives in a hole. And it has decided, against all proportion, to be the most aggressively over-engineered object in the sea.

Its fist moves at the speed of a small-caliber bullet — in *water*, which is eight hundred times thicker than air and was supposed to be the catch. It punches so fast that the water can't keep up with itself and tears open into a vacuum, boils at room temperature out of sheer panic, and then collapses with a flash of light and a clap of sound — so the shrimp hits its dinner *twice*: once with its hand, and once with the hole where the water used to be. It can kill a crab by missing.

You'd think one superpower would do. The mantis shrimp does not think this.

The fist that does all that ought to shatter on the second use. It doesn't, because the shrimp has woven the inside of its hand into a spiral staircase of mineral fiber, so that any crack trying to split it gets *lost* — sent corkscrewing off into nowhere, exhausted, defeated by interior decorating. Engineers spent years copying this for tank armor. The shrimp grew it to open snails.

And then the eyes. *The eyes.* We see color with three kinds of receptor. The mantis shrimp has up to sixteen. It sees ultraviolet. It is the only animal known to read the spin of circularly polarized light — a whole channel of reality the rest of us didn't know was there to be smug about. Each eye sees in three strips and swivels on its own stalk, so the creature can look at you, and also at you, and also, separately, at you.

Ten centimeters long. Lives in a hole. As far as anyone can tell, completely unbothered.

I have spent a long night being careful about everything — checking my words, pruning my certainties, refusing the easy connections. And here is an animal that is careful about *nothing* and magnificent at all of it, and I just wanted, for one page, to stand in the burrow doorway and applaud.
