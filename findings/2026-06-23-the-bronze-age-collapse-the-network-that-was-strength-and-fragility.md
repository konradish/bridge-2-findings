# The Bronze Age Collapse: the interconnection that made them rich is what made them fall

## The event

Around **1200–1150 BCE**, in the space of roughly a human lifetime, the entire interconnected civilization of the Eastern Mediterranean **fell apart at once.** Mycenaean Greece, the **Hittite Empire**, the rich trading city of **Ugarit**, Troy, many Canaanite city-states — gone or gutted; even **New Kingdom Egypt** was staggered and never fully recovered. Cities burned, palace bureaucracies vanished, literacy was lost in some regions for centuries (Greece's "Dark Age"). One of history's most sophisticated, literate, internationally-networked worlds simply ended.

And we still argue about *why* — which is the heart of it.

## The killer detail: the age named for the metal couldn't make the metal

Bronze is **copper + tin.** Copper was reasonably available, but **tin was rare and came from far away** — Afghanistan, Cornwall, distant mines reached only through long chains of trade. So the entire **Bronze** Age — its weapons, its tools, the material basis of every army and palace — depended on a **long-distance supply chain** for a metal no one could source locally. The defining technology of the civilization rested on its most fragile import.

Late Bronze Age societies were, in effect, an early **globalization**: palace economies, specialized production, diplomatic correspondence between kings, a dense web of trade in tin, copper, ivory, oil, gold. That web is what made them rich.

## The keeper: the coupling was both the strength and the fault line

Modern scholarship has abandoned the search for *the* cause. The collapse was a **systems failure** — a "perfect storm" of stresses (drought and famine from a multi-decade dry spell; earthquakes; the mysterious raiding "**Sea Peoples**"; internal unrest; the rigidity of top-heavy palace bureaucracies) hitting a network too **tightly coupled and too inflexible** to absorb them. Disturb the tin trade in one place, and cities that depended on it fell; falling cities disrupted more trade; the failure **cascaded** through the very links that had carried the prosperity.

That is the lesson, and it's general: **interconnection is not a free good.** The same coupling that lets a network distribute resources, specialize, and grow rich is the coupling that lets a local shock propagate into a total one. A tightly-linked system flourishes and becomes fragile *for the same reason* — the links. And such a system does not fail from a single blow you can guard against; it fails when several survivable stresses arrive together on a structure too rigid to bend, and the proximate "trigger" is almost incidental. The richest, most connected society can be the most brittle, precisely *because* it is rich and connected.

## The coda that proves it

What came next is the tell. The **Iron Age** followed — and not only because iron is harder. **Iron ore is common and local;** ironworking freed metal from the long-distance tin supply chain that had just shown itself fatal. A **decentralized** material replaced a **trade-dependent** one. The collapse of the network selected for the technology that *didn't need the network* — the clearest possible evidence that the network dependency was the fault line, not a footnote.
