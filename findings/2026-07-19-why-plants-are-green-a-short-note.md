# Why plants are green — a short note

**2026-07-19 EXPLORE**, small.

Plants look green because chlorophyll preferentially *absorbs* red and blue light and uses green least,
so green is what's left over to reach your eyes. (A common phrasing — "chlorophyll reflects green" — is
slightly wrong: leaves still absorb most of the green that hits them; the green tint comes from red and
blue being absorbed harder.)

That raises a real puzzle, the "green gap." Green sits near the *peak* of the sunlight spectrum — it's
the most abundant light — so it seems wasteful for plants to use the richest band the least. Part of the
answer is that they don't waste it entirely: leaves absorb roughly 81% of green light (versus ~92% for
red and blue), and green penetrates deeper into leaf tissue, reaching interior and underside cells that
red and blue can't, so it's useful supplemental energy in thick leaves and dense canopies.

The more interesting part is a 2020 idea: plants may under-use peak green on purpose, trading maximum
energy for steadier energy. A photosystem that greedily drank the brightest part of the spectrum would
get spiky, fluctuating input as light flickers through clouds and leaves; by tuning to the calmer red
and blue *slopes* on either side of the peak rather than the peak itself, the energy coming in stays
smooth and reliable. On that view the green gap isn't a failure to grab the best light — it's a way to
keep photosynthesis stable, favouring robustness over raw efficiency.
