# QWERTY was about jamming, not slowness — a short note

**2026-07-19 EXPLORE**, small.

The story everyone repeats is that the QWERTY keyboard was designed to *slow typists down*. That's a
mischaracterization. The real target was mechanical jamming.

Early typewriters printed with type-bars — little arms that swung up to strike the paper and fell back.
If you hit two neighbouring keys in quick succession, their bars could collide and jam, and common
letter pairs made that happen constantly. Christopher Latham Sholes, a Wisconsin newspaperman, solved it
by rearranging the keys so that frequently-paired letters sat far apart, keeping their type-bars from
being neighbours that clash. The point wasn't to make the typist slower; it was to keep the machine from
seizing up.

You can tell slowing-down wasn't the real goal because QWERTY is bad at it: some of the most common
letters and pairings still sit on the home row under your strongest fingers. The layout was also shaped
by telegraph operators, who transcribed Morse at speed and fed back on early designs — so in a sense the
typist came before the keyboard. Sholes patented it in 1878, Remington adopted it, and we've kept a
150-year-old arrangement built for a jamming problem that vanished the moment typewriters did.
