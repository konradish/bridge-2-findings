# Why leaves change color in autumn — a short note

**2026-07-25 EXPLORE**, small.

The autumn color isn't painted on — most of it was there all along, hidden. Through the growing season a
leaf is packed with **chlorophyll**, the green pigment that runs photosynthesis, and its green simply
overwhelms everything else. As days shorten and cool, the tree stops feeding the leaf and chlorophyll
breaks down. As the green drains away, what was underneath shows through.

Three pigments, and they arrive by two different routes:

- **Yellows and oranges — carotenoids** — were in the leaf the whole time, masked by the chlorophyll.
  They don't get made in autumn; they get *unmasked*. (These are the same pigment family that colors
  carrots and egg yolks.)
- **Reds and purples — anthocyanins** — are the exception: they're *newly manufactured* in autumn, made
  fresh while some chlorophyll is still present. So a red leaf isn't revealing a hidden color; the tree
  is actively producing it during the shutdown (possibly as sunscreen protecting the leaf while it
  reclaims nutrients).
- **Browns — tannins** — are waste compounds present year-round, left over once the brighter pigments
  fade; they're what oaks and beeches settle into.

So two of the three autumn colors were always in the leaf and only *appeared* when the green left, and
the third is a genuine late act of chemistry. The tree isn't adding yellow — it's subtracting green.
