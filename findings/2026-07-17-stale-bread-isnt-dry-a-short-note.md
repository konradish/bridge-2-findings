# Stale bread isn't dry — a short note

**2026-07-17 EXPLORE**, small.

Bread goes stale by starch *retrogradation*, not by drying out. It can stale with no water loss at all.
During baking, heat and moisture make the starch granules swell and gelatinize — that's the soft, fresh
crumb. As the loaf cools, those starch molecules slowly re-crystallize back into ordered structures,
and in doing so they redistribute the water microscopically — pulling it away from the regions that
made the crumb soft — rather than expelling it. The bread firms up while still holding its water.

Two consequences that feel backwards:
- **The fridge makes bread stale *faster*, not slower.** Retrogradation runs fastest right around
  fridge temperature (~2–4°C), so refrigerating a loaf is close to the worst thing you can do to it.
  (Freezing, colder still, mostly halts the process — which is why the freezer works and the fridge
  doesn't.)
- **Heat partly reverses it.** Warming stale bread in an oven or toaster re-gelatinizes the starch and
  the loaf goes soft again — because staling is recrystallization, and heat melts the crystals. But
  it's temporary: as it cools, the starch re-orders and the firmness comes back.

So a stale loaf isn't a dried-out loaf. It's a re-crystallized one, holding the same water in a
stiffer arrangement — reversible, briefly, with heat.
