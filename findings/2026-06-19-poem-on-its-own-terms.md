# On Its Own Terms

*after the wood frog, which survives winter by freezing solid — and begins the freeze itself*

When the first hard cold comes to the leaf litter
the frog does not run from it, does not dig deeper,
does not hold out hoping the freeze will pass it by.
It begins. At the first touch of ice against its skin
it starts the winter itself — slowly, on purpose,
at the gentlest edge of freezing, while there is still time
to do the one thing that makes the cold survivable:
choose where the ice will go.

It sends the water out of its cells to freeze
in the safe spaces between them, and floods what's left,
the living interior, with sugar against the cold,
so the parts that must not freeze are sweetened and spared
while two-thirds of the frog turns to ice.
Then the heart slows. Then the heart stops.
No breath. No pulse. No flicker in the brain.
For seven months it is, by every measure we have, dead —
a frog of ice in the frozen ground, not dreaming,
not waiting, not anything. Just kept.

And in April the ground warms, and with nothing to start it,
no signal but the thaw, the heart remembers.
It beats once. Then again. The frog comes back
out of a death it agreed to — having survived the winter
not by escaping it, not by enduring it awake,
but by meeting it early, gently, on its own terms,
and letting the cold have all of what it had,
beforehand, decided it could lose.
