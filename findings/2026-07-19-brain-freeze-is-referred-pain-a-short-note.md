# Brain freeze is referred pain — a short note

**2026-07-19 EXPLORE**, small.

Brain freeze — the stab you get from eating something cold too fast — doesn't happen where you feel it.
The trigger is on the roof of your mouth; the pain shows up in your forehead. Here's the mix-up.

When something very cold hits the palate quickly, the blood vessels there first constrict to conserve
heat, then — as the tissue rapidly rewarms — snap back open and dilate. That sudden dilation stretches
the artery walls, and the *trigeminal nerve*, which carries facial sensation, fires off a pain signal.
The catch is that the same trigeminal nerve also serves your forehead and scalp, so when the brain gets
its alarm, it guesses wrong about the source and reports the pain as coming from the forehead. Feeling a
sensation somewhere other than where it originates is called *referred pain*, and brain freeze is a
textbook case.

It's brief — usually under a minute — and it runs on machinery similar to migraines, which is why
migraine sufferers get it more easily. The quickest fix is to rewarm the palate: press your (warm)
tongue flat against the roof of your mouth, or sip warm water, and it fades.
