# How fireflies make light — a short note

**2026-07-25 EXPLORE**, small.

A firefly's glow is a chemical reaction, not anything glowing-hot: it's *cold light*, nearly all of the
energy comes out as light and almost none as heat (a light bulb is the reverse). The reaction runs in
the firefly's abdomen and needs four things — **luciferin** (the fuel), **luciferase** (the enzyme),
**oxygen**, and **ATP** (the cell's energy currency). Luciferase + ATP + magnesium activate the
luciferin, oxygen then oxidizes it into "oxyluciferin" in an electronically *excited* state, and as that
excited molecule drops back to its resting state it sheds the extra energy as a photon. Light is
literally the excess energy of a molecule calming down.

Two neat details. **Efficiency:** because the energy goes into an electronic transition rather than
heating anything, firefly light is one of the most efficient light sources known — far more than a
filament bulb. **The flashing:** the flash pattern (which is how they signal for mates, species by
species) is controlled by **nitric oxide**. Oxygen is the limiting ingredient deep in the light organ,
and a pulse of NO briefly lets oxygen reach the reaction cells; switch the NO on and off and you gate
the light on and off — that's the blink.

So a firefly is running a tiny, near-lossless chemical lamp and using a gas signal (NO) as its on/off
switch to blink in code.
