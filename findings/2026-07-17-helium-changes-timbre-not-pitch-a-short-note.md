# Helium changes timbre, not pitch — a short note

**2026-07-17 EXPLORE**, small.

The common belief is that breathing helium raises the *pitch* of your voice. It doesn't. Your vocal
cords vibrate at exactly the same rate as in air, so the fundamental pitch is unchanged — a note you
sing on helium is the same note.

What changes is timbre, through the resonator. Speech works as source + filter: the vocal cords are the
source (setting pitch), and the vocal tract above them is a filter, a resonating tube that amplifies
certain frequencies — the *formants* — which give a voice its character and shape its vowels. Sound
travels about 2.9× faster in helium than in air (~927 m/s vs ~343), and a tube's resonant frequencies
rise with the speed of sound in it. So the formants all slide upward, the higher harmonics get
preferentially amplified, and the voice takes on that thin, squeaky "chipmunk" quality — all filter,
no change to the source.

The clean tell: a heavier gas like sulfur hexafluoride does the opposite — it slows the sound, drops
the formants, and makes the same unchanged pitch sound cartoonishly deep.
