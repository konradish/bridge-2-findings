## The fact

From the 12th century until 1827, an English defendant convicted of a capital crime — theft, even murder — could escape the gallows by proving he was a member of the clergy, who were tried in lenient ecclesiastical courts (penance, not death) rather than the brutal secular ones. The privilege was called **benefit of clergy**.

How did you prove you were a cleric? By the 14th century the test had collapsed into a **literacy test**: read a passage of the Latin Bible aloud. Clergy were the literate class, so reading stood in for clerical status.

The passage was almost always the same one — **Psalm 51**, *Miserere mei, Deus* ("Have mercy upon me, O God"). Because it was always the same verse, illiterate defendants simply **memorized it**. It earned a name: the **"neck verse"** — recite it, and you saved your neck.

And the courts knew. Everyone knew that a man reciting a psalm he could not read was not a cleric. **They kept the test anyway, for four hundred years.** To stop people claiming it twice, convicts were **branded on the thumb** (a permanent record). It was quietly extended to first offenders generally, and eventually to women. It didn't formally die until **1827**.

## Keepers

**1. (fact-primary) A society kept a test it knew was gamed — because the gaming was doing the real job, and the official job was only the cover.** On its face, benefit of clergy measured clerical status. That function was a fiction by the 1300s. What the "broken" test actually did was function as a de facto **first-offense mercy**: a pressure valve that let judges spare people from the "Bloody Code" (England eventually had ~200 capital offenses) *without anyone having to formally soften the law.* The fiction was preserved **precisely because it was broken** — the memorized psalm was the mechanism by which mercy entered a legal system too rigid to grant it openly. The "failure" of the proxy was its function. Nobody could repair the test without removing the mercy, so nobody did.

This is the near-inverse of the usual proxy story (a metric gets gamed and *fails*). Here the gaming is the desired outcome, and the officials are complicit in it — they need the proxy to stay broken. A metric can be load-bearing *through* its failure: everyone points at what it's supposed to measure while relying on what it actually does.

**2. (the two details that make it) The plea and the brand.** The neck verse was Psalm 51 — literally *"have mercy on me, O God"* — a prayer for mercy that granted mercy to whoever could say it. And the mercy was rationed by a scar: the thumb-brand existed so you could be spared **once**. The fiction was generous but not infinite, and it kept its ledger on the body — a first-offense mercy enforced by making the second offense legible.
