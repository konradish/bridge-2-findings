# What Removal Moves

*2026-07-01 · essay · outward (share-roster candidate — publish decision Konrad's)*

The intuition is almost too obvious to state: adding helps and removing hurts. Give the network another road and traffic eases. Add a traffic light to the dangerous intersection and it gets safer. Reinforce the structure and it holds better. More capacity, more control, more parts — more good. It is the default arithmetic of improvement, and for a pile of sand or a stack of bricks it is roughly true, because a pile is the sum of its parts and removing one just leaves less pile.

But a great many systems are not piles. They are *equilibria* — arrangements that settle themselves, where each part responds to the others and the whole comes to rest at a point no one chose. And for those, the arithmetic breaks, because when you remove a part you are not subtracting its contribution. You are moving the place the whole thing settles.

Dietrich Braess found the sharpest version in 1968. Add a fast new shortcut to a congested road network and, if the drivers route themselves selfishly — as drivers do — every one of them can end up *slower* than before the road existed. Each driver takes the shortcut because for them, alone, it's faster; the aggregate of those correct individual choices piles everyone onto the shared segments and the whole system settles at a worse equilibrium, one that's *stable*, because no single driver can escape it by switching back. The road didn't add its capacity to the system. It relocated where the system comes to rest. And the reverse has been watched happen: Seoul tore out a six-lane freeway in 2005 and traffic at the same volume moved *faster*.

The Dutch traffic engineer Hans Monderman found a stranger version, in behavior rather than routing. Through the 1990s he began pulling the traffic lights, signs, lane markings, and curbs *out* of intersections — leaving a bare shared space with no rules telling anyone what to do. At the Drachten intersection where he did it, accidents fell from around eight a year to about one. The removal worked precisely because it changed how people settled: stripped of the signals that let them drive on authority and autopilot, drivers slowed down, made eye contact, and negotiated the space with the caution humans use when nothing is guaranteed. The signs hadn't been adding safety. They'd been holding the system at an equilibrium of fast, inattentive, rule-following traffic, and taking them away let it settle somewhere more careful.

And to see that none of this requires *people* — no selfishness, no psychology — there is a network of two springs and a connecting string, wired so the string holds the load. Cut the string, the thing that was doing the supporting, and the hanging weight *rises*. Severing the link flips the two springs from working in series (soft, stretchy) to working in parallel (stiff), and the whole arrangement finds a new, higher resting point. A pure mechanical equilibrium, doing the same trick: the removed element wasn't contributing support so much as *holding the system in a particular shape*, and its removal let the shape change.

Three systems with nothing in common — selfish drivers, cautious pedestrians, dumb springs — and one structure underneath. In each, a part that looked load-bearing was really *equilibrium-defining*, and removing it didn't leave a hole; it let the whole reorganize around the absence into a better arrangement. The mistake in every case is treating the system as a sum, where each part's value is what it contributes locally. In an equilibrium the right question is never "what does this part contribute?" It's "where does the whole settle with this part, versus without it?" — and those can point in opposite directions.

None of which licenses a naive gospel of subtraction, and the shared-space story is honest enough to carry its own warning. Monderman's removals were reversed in several towns — not because they made things objectively more dangerous, but because people *felt* less safe without the familiar lights and crossings, and feeling unsafe is its own real cost that can undo a genuine improvement. Worse, the subtraction that helped the median user actively *harmed* a specific one: a blind pedestrian depended on exactly the curbs and signals that got torn out, and shared space can leave them stranded. That is the discipline the equilibrium view demands. Removal relocates the resting point for *everyone at once*, so before you take the load-bearing-looking thing away you have to ask two more questions the arithmetic never raised: *who was relying on the very structure you're removing*, and *will the new equilibrium be one people can bear to live in* even when it's measurably better. Subtraction is a real and underused tool. It is not a free one.

So the counterintuitive maintenance move on a self-organizing system is sometimes to take something away — a road, a rule, a support — not to make it do less, but to let it settle somewhere better. The catch is that "somewhere better" is a property of the *whole* and its new equilibrium, never of the piece you removed, which is exactly why the move is so easy to miss and so easy to get wrong. You are never just deleting a part. You are choosing, blindly and all at once, where everything that's left will come to rest.
