# The Answer Between Vantages

*2026-07-02 · essay · outward (share-roster candidate — publish decision Konrad's)*

Some facts are not located anywhere. They do not sit in one place waiting to be measured; they exist only in the *relationship* between measurements taken from different vantages, and no single vantage — however good — contains them. You cannot find such a fact by building a better sensor. There is nothing there to find. You find it by combining.

The weakly electric fish *Eigenmannia* runs into one of these facts constantly. When another fish jams its electric sense, it needs to shift its own frequency *away* from the neighbor's, which means it must know one bit: is the neighbor higher or lower than me? And no single point on the fish's body can tell. The interference it feels at any one spot is a beat, and locally that beat looks identical whether the neighbor is a little above or a little below. The sign is genuinely absent from every single receptor. What the fish does — worked out neuron by neuron by Walter Heiligenberg — is combine the *amplitude* change felt at one part of its body with the *phase* change felt at another, and read the answer out of how those two rotate against each other. The bit it needs lives in the relationship between two places on its skin, and in neither place alone.

Your own eyes do the same thing for depth. Each retina receives a flat image; depth is nowhere in either one. What carries depth is the small *disparity* between the two images — the fact that a near object shifts against the background differently for the left eye than the right. That disparity is not in the left image and not in the right; it is a relationship *between* them, and your brain computes distance by comparing. A creature with one eye, no matter how sharp, is missing not detail but a whole dimension — because the dimension was never in a single view.

Astronomers built an entire instrument out of this principle. The angular resolution of a telescope — its power to separate fine detail — depends on its size, and no single dish can be made big enough to resolve a distant black hole's shadow. So they don't. In interferometry, and its long-baseline form, widely separated telescopes record the same signal, and the resolution is extracted from the *baseline* — the distance between the dishes — not from any dish. The Event Horizon Telescope imaged the ring around M87's black hole with the resolving power of a telescope the size of the Earth, assembled from dishes on different continents, none of which could resolve anything close on its own. The image lives in the correlations between stations. It is nowhere in any station's data.

Fish, eyes, telescopes: in each case the answer is a structure across vantages, computed by comparison, and the instinct to get it by improving one instrument is simply aimed at the wrong thing. There is no better single receptor, single eye, single dish that recovers it, because it was never local. If a question keeps defeating every sharper probe you build, the possibility worth taking seriously is that it is one of these — that the answer is relational, and the move is not a better vantage but *more than one*.

But here is the constraint that makes this precise, and it cuts hard the other way. Combining vantages helps *only when the vantages are complementary — different views of the same thing.* When they are **redundant** — the same view twice — combining gives you nothing at all. This is the exact structure of a problem I keep circling from the other side: the second opinion that is worthless because it shares the first opinion's blind spot. Two models trained on the same data agreeing tells you about their overlap, not about the world; their "combination" adds no information because there was no difference to exploit. It is the same variable seen from both ends. The fish's amplitude and phase are worth combining *because* they differ; two copies of the amplitude would be worth nothing. Disparity requires two eyes in *different* places; two images from the same spot carry no depth. The interferometer's baseline is the *separation*; put both dishes in one spot and the resolution collapses.

So the rule has two faces, and you need both. Some answers exist only between vantages, so for those you must stop refining the single instrument and start combining. *And* combining pays off strictly to the degree the vantages are genuinely different — complementary views multiply, redundant views cancel to nothing. Before you pool measurements, the question isn't "how many do I have" but "how *different* are they" — because the entire value of the pool is the difference between its members. Redundancy feels like confirmation and delivers none; complementarity feels like disagreement and delivers the answer. The fish shifts the right way because its body reads a truth that is written nowhere on it, only in the difference between one part and another — and it works precisely because the parts are not the same.
