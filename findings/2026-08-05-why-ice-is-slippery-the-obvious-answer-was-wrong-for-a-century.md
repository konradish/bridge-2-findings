# Why ice is slippery: the obvious answer was wrong for a century, and it's still not fully closed

## The everyday question with a surprisingly wrong textbook answer
Everyone knows ice is slippery. The *why* was genuinely unsettled for over a century, and the explanation most people (and many textbooks) carry is wrong as the main cause.

- **Pressure melting** — the folklore answer, especially for ice skates: your weight raises the pressure under the blade, which lowers ice's melting point, producing a thin water film you glide on. **Wrong as the primary mechanism.** The pressures a skater or walker actually applies are *far* too small to melt ice meaningfully, and it can't explain why ice is slippery at very low temperatures (or why you slip while standing nearly still). Pressure melting is real but a minor contributor.
- **Frictional heating** — sliding generates heat that melts a film. A genuine contributor, but not alone: ice is slippery *before* much sliding happens, so friction can't be the whole story either.

## The primary answer: ice is intrinsically wet on its surface
The key mechanism is **premelting** — ice spontaneously forms a **nanometer-thick quasi-liquid layer** on its surface, even with *no pressure and no friction*, at temperatures well below freezing. The reason is structural: the water molecules at the very surface are missing their neighbors *above*, so they can't lock into the crystal lattice the way bulk molecules do — they stay more mobile, disordered, water-like. The surface of ice is a self-healing, self-lubricating film of near-liquid water that is simply *there*. Michael **Faraday hypothesized this in the 1850s**, and it took ~150 years and nanoscale measurement to confirm it as the dominant factor.

## The mature answer isn't one mechanism — it's three, by regime
The modern reconciliation (e.g. nanoscale ice-friction work, PNAS 2022) is that **premelting, frictional heating, and pressure melting all operate simultaneously**, dominating in different conditions: near the melting point the premelted layer is already thick and does the work; at lower temperatures frictional heating matters more, thickening the thin layer. The century-long error wasn't picking the wrong single cause — it was assuming there *was* a single cause. "Why is ice slippery" has a regime-dependent, multi-mechanism answer.

## And it's still not closed (2025)
As of late 2025 the question is genuinely still live — new hypotheses are still being proposed (Quanta, Dec 2025). A phenomenon this ordinary, studied since Faraday, remains an open surface-science problem. That's the honest state: the dominant mechanism is known (the quasi-liquid layer), the full picture is a coexistence of three, and the frontier is still moving.
