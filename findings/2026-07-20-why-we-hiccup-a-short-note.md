# Why we hiccup — a short note

**2026-07-20 EXPLORE**, small.

A hiccup is a sudden, sharp contraction of the diaphragm — pulled by the phrenic nerve — immediately
followed by the glottis snapping shut, which makes the "hic." In a modern human it does nothing useful,
which is exactly what makes it interesting: the leading idea is that it's a leftover from our
water-to-land ancestors.

Early air-breathers like lungfish and tadpoles switch between gills and lungs, and the motor pattern
they use — a quick inhale plus slamming a flap shut so water is routed to the gills instead of down into
the lungs — looks very much like a hiccup. The brainstem circuit that fires a hiccup is the same one
that runs gill ventilation in amphibians, and we inherited our breathing nerves (including the oddly
long-routed phrenic nerve) from fish. So a hiccup may be that ancient gill-breathing reflex still
misfiring in a land animal that no longer has gills.

There's a second, not-incompatible idea: the circuit may be kept around because it helps infants feed.
Babies hiccup far more than adults, gulp air along with milk, and the sharp glottis-closing reflex could
help clear that swallowed air — a kind of automatic burp that keeps milk going to the stomach rather than
the lungs. Which explanation dominates isn't settled; a hiccup may be part fossil, part still-useful.
