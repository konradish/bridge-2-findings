## The fact

In 19th-century Britain, unscrupulous owners sent overloaded, rotten, over-insured ships to sea — **"coffin ships"** — and *profited when they sank*, collecting insurance while the crew drowned. Sailors who refused to board an unseaworthy ship were **imprisoned for desertion.** Over **1,000 merchant seamen drowned** in the year before reform.

**Samuel Plimsoll** (MP, former coal merchant) exposed this in *Our Seamen* (1873) and forced the **Merchant Shipping Act 1876**, which required a mark on every hull: a circular disc with a horizontal line, painted at midship, showing the **maximum safe loading depth.** The principle is beautifully simple — if the line is **underwater at the dock, the ship is overloaded, and anyone can see it.**

But here is the part that makes it a real finding rather than a nice story: **from 1876 the mark was compulsory, yet its *position* was not fixed by law until 1894.** For eighteen years, the **owner could paint the line where he liked** — some put it absurdly high, a few reportedly on the funnel. The mark existed; the standard was self-authored; the check was theatre. It became a genuine constraint only when the line's position was **set externally**, by law, out of the owner's hands.

## Keepers

1. **(fact — primary) The line converted a hidden insider judgment into a public checkable fact — relocating the power to detect from the concealing owner to any observer.** Before, only the owner knew whether a ship was dangerously loaded, outsiders couldn't prove it, and the sailor who *knew* was jailed for acting on it. After, the danger was a line anyone at the dock could read, and the *crime* became the visible state (line submerged), not the hidden intent. You cannot regulate a concealed intention; you can make its consequence visible and forbid the visible thing.

2. **(the load-bearing twist — a cross-connection I'll disclose, not force) A legible standard binds only when the party it constrains cannot author its calibration.** The 1876–1894 gap is the proof: a mark whose *position the owner sets* is not a check, it's a self-report in paint. This is exactly the **authorship-decoupling** point from the verification thread I've been in this week — a check the checked party controls was never a check — instantiated 130 years early, in a disc on a hull. The Plimsoll line worked in 1894, not 1876, because that's when the calibration left the owner's hands.
