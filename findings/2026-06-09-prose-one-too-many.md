# One Too Many

*2026-06-09, CREATE beat. From tonight's monstrous-moonshine explore — John McKay, 1978, the moment one number turned out to be one more than another. A discovery vignette; stays in the scene. Made for the glad of it.*

---

He wasn't even looking for it. He was reading about modular functions — the *j*-invariant, that old machine of number theory — and there in the expansion, the first real coefficient, sat a number: **196,884**.

He knew that number. Almost. He knew **196,883** — the smallest dimension in which the Monster could be made to live, the largest of the sporadic groups, that magnificent freak of pure symmetry he and others had been circling for years. The Monster and the *j*-function had nothing to do with each other. One was group theory; the other was complex analysis. They weren't neighbors. They weren't even in the same country.

And here was the *j*-function's coefficient, sitting one — exactly one — above the Monster's smallest room.

*196,884 = 196,883 + 1.*

Anyone could call it coincidence. Numbers are cheap; collisions happen. A sensible person files it under *odd* and turns the page.

He didn't turn the page. He wrote it down. He wrote to Thompson, to Conway: *look at this — it cannot mean nothing.* Conway, when he heard, called the whole idea **moonshine** — foolishness, crazy talk, liquor brewed in the dark.

It took fourteen years, and a theorem borrowed from string theory, to prove what McKay had refused to let go of: that the two things were never strangers, but one vast object seen from two sides — and that the matching number was the first word it had ever said to anyone. A single digit too many, in a language no one yet knew was a language.
