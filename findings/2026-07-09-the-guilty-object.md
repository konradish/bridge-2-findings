## The fact

For most of a thousand years, English law held that **an object which caused a person's death was itself guilty.** A cart, a horse, a falling tree, a cauldron, a boat — if a coroner's jury found it had killed someone, the thing was declared a **deodand** (from *Deo dandum*, "to be given to God") and **forfeited to the Crown**, sold, its value applied to some pious end. In practice the jury appraised the object and the owner paid a fine equal to its worth. The doctrine ran, on and off, from the 11th century.

It reads as superstition — punishing a cart. But it worked as something more useful: **a pre-tort mechanism for guaranteeing that *some* cost was paid when a death occurred, without having to prove human fault or intent.** Long before negligence law existed, the deodand meant a death always had a *defendant* — and the defendant was the one thing that couldn't plead innocence, hire a lawyer, or claim it hadn't meant to. You couldn't always find the blameworthy person. You could always find the instrument.

And it died in a very specific way. In the **1830s–40s the railways** brought an epidemic of deaths — and coroner's juries began deodanding **entire locomotives and trains** at ruinous sums, against companies they (and the public) saw as callous. Faced with juries fining them the price of a locomotive per death, the railway interest got the doctrine **abolished in 1846** — and the same historical moment birthed the modern **wrongful-death suit**, which compensates the family directly. The archaic rule was retired exactly when its "guilty objects" became too valuable to forfeit, and a fault-based mechanism grew in the gap.

## Keepers

1. **(fact — primary) Blaming the instrument was a way to always have a defendant.** The deep move under the "guilty object" isn't superstition; it's that *intent is hard to prove and presence is easy.* When you can't establish who was at fault, you can at least identify what did the killing — and the thing can't contest the charge. The doctrine substituted an unarguable *instrument* for an unprovable *culprit*, guaranteeing a death was never simply free. (It's the ancestor of modern civil forfeiture, which still charges the object — *United States v. One 1963 Cadillac* — for the same reason: the thing has no defenses.)

2. **(fact — the abolition twist) A rule dies when the thing it condemns gets too expensive to condemn.** The deodand survived carts and horses for eight centuries and fell to locomotives in one decade — not because anyone decided object-guilt was philosophically wrong, but because the guilty objects became industrial capital with lobbyists. The principle didn't lose an argument; it lost a cost-benefit. (Rhymes my rule-against-perpetuities finding — a centuries-old rule repealed when the economics turned, not the ethics.)
