## The folk claim, and its likely death
"Hot water freezes faster than cold" (Mpemba's 1969 observation with ice cream mix). For decades a beloved counterintuitive fact. **Burridge & Linden (2016)** tried to pin it down and concluded it is "not observable in any meaningful way" *for water*: apparent Mpemba results depended on vessel size and — devastatingly — on the *height at which the thermometer sat* in a convecting, evaporating, supercooling vessel. Most positive reports never even recorded that height. Worse, there is **no agreed definition** of what "freezes faster" means (first ice crystal? fully solid? reaches 0°C?). An effect with no fixed definition and confound-dependent results is not a phenomenon; it's a measurement artifact wearing a phenomenon's clothes. The kitchen claim, as usually stated, probably isn't real.

## The rescue: abandon water, define it rigorously
Instead of defending the water claim, the field **redefined the effect abstractly** and found it clean:
- **Lu & Raz (2017)** framed it in Markovian relaxation: a system has decay eigenmodes; the slowest one dominates late-time approach to equilibrium. A "hotter" (farther-from-equilibrium) initial state can relax *faster* if it happens to have **smaller overlap with that slowest-decaying mode** than a "colder" state does. Nothing about water — a general property of how distributions relax to a bath.
- **Kumar & Bechhoefer (2020, Nature)** demonstrated it experimentally in a **single colloidal particle** in a shaped optical potential, where "temperature" and "distance from equilibrium" are cleanly defined and controlled. Hotter initial conditions provably reached the cold-bath equilibrium exponentially faster, including a "strong Mpemba effect" with dramatic speedup. Rigorous, reproducible, mechanism-explained.

## The honest state (the keeper)
**"Hot water freezes faster" is probably false or ill-defined; "a state farther from equilibrium can relax to a bath faster, when it has less overlap with the system's slowest decay mode" is true, rigorous, and demonstrated.** The popular phenomenon didn't survive; a *sharper, different* phenomenon did — and it isn't the one the folklore described. The mechanism (overlap with the slow eigenmode) is the real content, and it's more interesting than "hot beats cold" ever was.

Two clean sub-facts worth keeping:
1. **A confound-dependent, undefined effect can be a real puzzle AND a measurement artifact at once** — and the way forward wasn't to prove or disprove the folk version, it was to *reformulate* it into something measurable. Progress came from changing the question, not answering it.
2. **The slowest eigenmode is the whole story.** Late-time relaxation is governed by one dominant slow mode; the "paradox" dissolves once you ask about overlap with *that mode* rather than about temperature directly. The intuitive variable (temperature) was the wrong coordinate.
