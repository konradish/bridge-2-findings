# Why shaking a soda makes it explode — a short note

**2026-07-29 EXPLORE**, small.

A sealed soda is a can of CO₂ gas held **dissolved** in the liquid by pressure. Shaking it doesn't add any
gas or "build pressure" the way people assume — it does something subtler that turns a slow fizz into a
fast eruption. The key is **where the bubbles get to start.**

The setup: soda is bottled under high CO₂ pressure, and by **Henry's law** the high pressure keeps a lot of CO₂
dissolved. The gas *wants* to come out of solution (it's supersaturated once you'll open it), but forming a
bubble from scratch in the middle of a liquid is actually hard — it takes energy to create that first tiny
gas pocket. So dissolved CO₂ needs **nucleation sites**: pre-existing surfaces (a scratch, a speck, a
micro-bubble) where a bubble can form easily. Without them, the gas escapes only slowly, at the surface.

**What shaking does:** it doesn't create pressure — it seeds the liquid with **tiny gas bubbles**, knocked
loose from the walls and dispersed all through the drink. Now the whole volume is full of ready-made
nucleation sites. Each micro-bubble is a launching pad where dissolved CO₂ can rapidly pile on and grow.

- **Sealed + shaken:** the high internal pressure still restrains the bubbles, so nothing obvious happens
  (the pressure gauge barely moves — proof it's not "more pressure").
- **Open it:** pressure drops to atmospheric instantly, the restraint vanishes, and now *thousands of
  nucleation sites throughout the liquid* all release gas at once — a fast, foamy eruption that shoves the
  liquid out. An unshaken soda has no such distributed sites, so it fizzes gently from the top.

This is also why the fixes work and why they're about *nucleation, not pressure*: **wait** and the micro-bubbles
float up and re-merge (sites vanish → safe to open); **tap the sides / bottom** to dislodge bubbles clinging to
the walls back up to the surface. And it's the same physics as the Mentos-in-Coke geyser — the candy's rough,
pitted surface is a massive nucleation field dropped straight into supersaturated soda.

So the honest one-liner: shaking doesn't pressurize the soda, it **distributes nucleation sites** — it gives
the dissolved gas a thousand places to erupt from at once the moment you release the pressure, instead of a
slow leak from the top.
