## The fact

A flat mirror only sends light straight back if you hit it dead-on; tilt it, and the reflection goes off at an angle. A **corner-cube retroreflector** does something a flat mirror can't: it sends light back toward wherever it came from, from almost any angle, with no aiming at all.

The trick is geometry. Take three flat mirrors and set them at mutual right angles — the inside corner of a box. A ray entering that corner bounces off all three faces in turn, and each bounce reverses one component of its direction. After three reflections every component has been flipped, so the ray leaves exactly antiparallel to how it came in — heading straight back at the source, no matter the angle it arrived at (within a wide acceptance cone). You don't have to point the reflector at anything. The shape does the pointing. (In solid glass versions there aren't even mirrors: the light bounces by total internal reflection off the bare glass-air faces, so there's no coating to tarnish.) This is why bike reflectors and road signs blaze in your headlights but stay dark to someone standing to the side — they return light to its source, which at night is you.

The most striking use is on the Moon. Apollo 11, 14, and 15 and the Soviet Lunokhod rovers left arrays of corner cubes on the lunar surface. Fire a laser at one from Earth and — even though the beam spreads to kilometres wide by the time it arrives, and only a handful of photons ever make it back — the cubes return that light along its own path, and the round-trip time gives the Earth–Moon distance to the **millimetre.** That measurement is how we know the Moon is receding at about **3.8 cm per year.** The reflectors have done this for over fifty years. They have no power source, no electronics, no moving parts, and no one has touched them since the 1970s. There is nothing in them to break, because there is no *mechanism* in them at all — only a shape.

## Keepers (brief — fact-led)

1. **A function built entirely into geometry needs no aiming and no control.** A corner cube returns light to its source because three orthogonal reflections reverse every component of the ray — the "aim" is a consequence of the shape, not a thing anyone sets. When you want a response directed back at whatever acts on a system, look for a geometry that produces the direction automatically, rather than a sensor-and-actuator that has to detect the source and point at it. The shape can do what a control loop would otherwise have to.
2. **Put the function in the shape, not in a mechanism, when it has to last untended.** The lunar reflectors still work after fifty unattended years precisely because they contain no active parts — nothing to lose power, drift, wear, or fail; the behaviour is guaranteed by fixed geometry, not maintained by an ongoing process. Passive-geometric function is dramatically more durable than mechanism: if a capability can be embodied in a static structure instead of a running system, it inherits the structure's near-indestructibility. What has no moving parts has nothing to break.

## Verified / flagged

- **Solid:** a corner-cube retroreflector (three mutually perpendicular reflective faces) returns incident light antiparallel to its arrival direction over a wide angular range, via three sequential reflections; solid versions use total internal reflection (uncoated). Apollo 11/14/15 and Lunokhod 1/2 left corner-cube arrays on the Moon; lunar laser ranging measures the Earth–Moon distance to ~mm and shows ~3.8 cm/yr recession. Standard optics / well-documented spaceflight.
- **Flag — "any angle" is a wide but finite cone.** Retroreflection works over a large range of incidence but not all of it; beyond the acceptance angle (especially for TIR cubes) it fails. Don't claim literally omnidirectional.
- **Flag — the return isn't perfectly to a point.** Diffraction and velocity aberration (the Earth moves during the round trip) spread the returned beam; reported pulse spreads are centimetres-scale for the lunar arrays. "Straight back at the source" is accurate to within that spread, not exact.
- **Flag — the reflector doesn't cause the recession.** The Moon's 3.8 cm/yr recession is driven by tidal friction; laser ranging *measures* it. Don't conflate the instrument with the cause. (And skip the "lengthening our days" pop-gloss; stick to the measured recession.)
