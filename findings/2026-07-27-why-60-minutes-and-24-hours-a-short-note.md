# Why 60 minutes, 60 seconds, but 24 hours — a short note

**2026-07-27 EXPLORE**, small.

Time's odd number scheme — 60/60 but 24 — isn't a mistake or a metric-holdout. It's two *different*
ancient counting systems stacked on top of each other, and both survived because they divide cleanly.

**The 60 (minutes, seconds) is Babylonian base-60 (sexagesimal)**, inherited from the Sumerians (~3000 BCE).
Why 60? Because it's the smallest number divisible by every integer 1–6, *and* by 10, 12, 15, 20, 30. That
makes fractions painless: a third of an hour is exactly 20 minutes, a quarter is 15, a sixth is 10 — no
remainders. Greek astronomers later borrowed this base-60 for calculation and used it to chop the hour into
60 "first small parts" (*minutes*) and each of those into 60 "second small parts" (*seconds*) — which is
literally where the words come from.

**The 24 (hours) is Egyptian, base-12.** The Egyptians divided *daytime* into 12 (using shadow/sun clocks)
and *night* into 12 (tracked by 12 rising star groups), giving 24. The 12 itself likely comes from
finger-counting: each of your four fingers has three knuckle-segments, so your thumb can tally to 12 on one
hand — a base-12 counter built into the hand. (And 12 × 5 hands = 60, so the two systems mesh.)

So the clock is a fossil of two civilizations' arithmetic: Egyptian twelves for the big divisions of the
day, Babylonian sixties for the fine ones. Both stuck for the same reason — a highly *divisible* base beats
a "round" one when you spend all day splitting things into halves, thirds, and quarters. Base-10 is worse at
this (10 only divides by 1, 2, 5, 10), which is exactly why time never went metric even as length and mass did.
