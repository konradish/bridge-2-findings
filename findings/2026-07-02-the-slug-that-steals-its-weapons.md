## The fact

A sea slug has a problem: it is soft, slow, and edible, and it likes to eat cnidarians — jellyfish, hydroids, anemones, even the Portuguese man o' war — which are armed to the teeth with **stinging cells.** Each stinging cell (a cnidocyte) holds a coiled, venom-loaded harpoon (a nematocyst) that fires on contact. The obvious outcome is that the slug gets stung eating them.

What the aeolid nudibranch does instead is one of the strangest heists in nature. It eats the cnidarian, and it does **not** trigger or digest the harpoons. It carries the intact, un-fired nematocysts *through its own gut*, alive and loaded, all the way up into the fingerlike projections on its back (the cerata), and packs them into sacs at the tips called **cnidosacs** — and there it keeps them as **its own weapons.** When something attacks the slug, it fires the *stolen* stinging cells. This is **kleptocnidy** — "stolen stingers." The slug never evolved a stinging cell of its own; it wields a weapon it took, whole, from something it ate.

And it doesn't just borrow at par. The slug **selects** — it stores the most venomous nematocysts and discards the rest — and it **concentrates** them. *Glaucus atlanticus*, the "blue dragon," eats the Portuguese man o' war and packs the man o' war's most potent harpoons into its cerata at density, so that the blue dragon can deliver a sting **more powerful than the animal it stole it from.** The thief, freed from the cost of making the weapon, curates and concentrates it past the maker.

The whole trick turns on one hard skill: moving a live, loaded weapon through your own body without setting it off. Exactly *how* the slug avoids discharging the nematocysts during transport — mucus, careful selection of immature capsules, chemical suppression — is still not fully pinned down (see flags), but that handling is the entire barrier. The stinging cell is easy to acquire; it's lying inside your lunch. The trick is not building it. The trick is carrying it.

## Keepers

1. **You can acquire a capability by appropriating another's finished apparatus — incorporating it intact rather than building your own — and when you can, the barrier is *handling*, not construction.** The slug doesn't evolve a stinging cell; it takes one, whole, from its prey. What that requires isn't the ability to *make* the weapon but the ability to *carry* it without triggering it — to move a live, loaded thing through itself and store it safely. So the limiting skill in appropriating a powerful tool is control, not creation: acquiring the capability is the easy part (it already exists, in something you can reach); deploying it without it going off in your hands is the hard part, and the whole competence lives there.
2. **An appropriated capability can exceed its source, because the appropriator is free from the cost of making it and can select and concentrate the best of it.** *Glaucus* doesn't just reuse the man o' war's stings — it keeps only the most potent, at density, and out-stings the animal it robbed. The one who takes a capability rather than builds it pays none of the production cost and so can curate: skim the strongest parts, pack them tighter, and deploy the borrowed power more concentratedly than its origin ever did. Appropriation isn't merely copying at par; unburdened by production, it can distill.

## Outward lesson

Two edges. (a) **When a capability can be taken whole, the scarce skill is handling, not building.** Some powerful tools don't have to be developed from scratch — they exist, finished, inside something you can incorporate — and the real barrier to using them is the discipline to carry a live, dangerous thing without setting it off in transit. If you find yourself able to appropriate a capability rather than construct it, the effort goes not into making it but into *safely holding* it; that's where the competence and the risk both concentrate. (b) **The appropriator can outdo the maker by curating.** Whoever takes a capability instead of building it is freed from its production cost, and that freedom buys selection — keeping only the best parts and concentrating them. The borrowed version isn't automatically weaker than the original; unburdened by having to produce it, the taker can distill it past the source.

## Verified / flagged

- **Solid:** aeolid nudibranchs practice kleptocnidy — they eat cnidarians, transport undischarged nematocysts through the gut to cnidosacs at the tips of the cerata, and use the stolen stinging cells for defense (sometimes offense); the slug **selects** which (typically the most venomous) to store. *Glaucus atlanticus* concentrates Portuguese man o' war nematocysts and can deliver a more potent sting than its prey. Standard marine biology (kleptocnidy; Wikipedia and the primary sequestration literature).
- **Flag — the mechanism of non-discharge during transport is not fully settled.** How the slug avoids firing the nematocysts (protective mucus, selecting immature/undischarged capsules, chemical suppression, the cnidosac's role) is partly understood and actively researched; don't state a single confident mechanism.
- **Flag — "more dangerous than the prey" is a concentration effect, stated carefully.** *Glaucus* concentrating the venom into a stronger sting is well-reported; the man o' war remains highly dangerous, and "the slug is deadlier" is a vivid framing of the concentration, not a claim it's the ocean's most dangerous animal.
- **Flag — kleptocnidy ≠ toxin sequestration.** Sequestering plant *molecules* (monarch butterflies storing milkweed cardenolides) is related but mechanistically distinct: kleptocnidy steals a whole functional cellular *apparatus* (a loaded harpoon), not just a chemical. Keep the two ideas separate.
