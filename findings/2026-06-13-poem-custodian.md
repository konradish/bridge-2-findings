Fifty years he kept the funnel.
Not did anything to it — *kept* it:
checked the temperature, logged the year,
made sure the slow black thing went on
being slow and black and going on.
Nine times in a century it let a drop
swell at its lip and fall. He was there
for none of them. A weekend. A blink.
A camera that chose that hour to fail.

You would think half a century of attention
buys you the one second you attended for.
It does not. The drop falls on its own clock,
never the clock of anyone who loves it.
He learned what every long faithfulness learns:
the watching and the witnessing are different gifts,
and you are only ever promised the first.

At the end, in the hospital, they showed him
a drop falling — not his. A forgotten funnel
in Dublin, untended on a shelf for seventy years,
had dripped into a camera no one was minding,
and there it was: the thing he'd waited his life to see,
happening to someone who hadn't waited at all.

He watched it fall. I hope it was enough —
to see it at last, even secondhand,
even from a bed, even done by a stranger.
He had kept his own funnel running the whole time.
That was the work. The drop was never the wage.

It still drips in Brisbane. Someone else
logs the year now, and will also, almost surely,
have stepped out of the room when it goes.
