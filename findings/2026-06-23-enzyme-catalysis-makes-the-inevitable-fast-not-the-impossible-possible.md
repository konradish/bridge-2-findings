# Enzyme catalysis: it makes the inevitable fast, not the impossible possible

## The fact

Left to itself in water, a molecule called orotidine 5′-monophosphate (OMP) loses a carboxyl group and becomes uridine monophosphate. It is in no hurry: the half-life of the uncatalyzed reaction is about **78 million years.** A bond that, statistically, breaks once every geological age.

The enzyme that does this reaction in your cells — **OMP decarboxylase (ODCase)** — runs it with a half-life of about **18 milliseconds.** That is a rate enhancement of roughly **10¹⁷** — the largest reported for any enzyme (Wolfenden and colleagues). And it does it with no metal ions and no cofactors, just folded protein — which is why the *mechanism* of how it manages this is still, in the literature's word, "an enigma."

Compress that: a reaction that would take **seventy-eight million years** is made to happen in **eighteen thousandths of a second.** Same molecule, same products, same chemistry. Only the speed is different — by a factor with seventeen zeros.

## The keeper: power over speed is not power over direction

Here is the part that doesn't get said enough, because the magnitude is so hypnotic. **A catalyst cannot change where the reaction ends up.** It lowers the activation-energy barrier — it stabilizes the awkward transition state the reaction has to pass through — and that is *all* it does. It does not touch the free-energy difference between start and finish, so it **cannot move the equilibrium.** It speeds the forward reaction and the reverse reaction by the *exact same factor* (they share the lowered barrier), so the ratio between them — the destination — is untouched.

Which means a catalyst can only ever **accelerate something that was already going to happen.** It cannot make a thermodynamically forbidden reaction occur; it cannot push anything uphill. ODCase does not *decide* that OMP becomes UMP — thermodynamics already decided that, eons ago. The enzyme only collapses the eons into milliseconds. It makes the **inevitable fast, not the impossible possible.**

So kinetics (how fast) and thermodynamics (whether, and where it lands) are independent axes, and a catalyst is a pure-kinetics lever. It can have absolutely staggering power on the one axis — 10¹⁷ — and exactly zero power on the other.

The generalization is worth holding onto, because the confusion it guards against is everywhere: **seeing something happen fast right after an intervention does not mean the intervention changed the outcome.** An accelerant that only lowers a barrier changes *when* a destined thing arrives, not *whether* it arrives or *where* it lands — and because the speed-up can be so dramatic, it's easy to credit it with a directional power it never had. Rate-levers and direction-levers are different things. A catalyst can turn 78 million years into 18 milliseconds and still not change one atom of where the reaction was always going.

## Verified / flagged
- **Solid:** OMP/ODCase uncatalyzed half-life ~78 My, catalyzed ~18 ms, rate enhancement ~10¹⁷ (largest reported; Radzicka & Wolfenden 1995, *Science*; Annual Reviews); ODCase uses no metal/cofactor and its mechanism is still debated ("an enigma persists"). Catalysts lower activation energy / stabilize the transition state, accelerate forward and reverse equally, and **do not change equilibrium** — standard physical chemistry.
- **⚠ The headline number is an extrapolation (flag):** you can't watch a 78-million-year reaction. The uncatalyzed rate was measured at elevated temperatures and **extrapolated to 25 °C** (Arrhenius). It's a well-accepted estimate, widely cited, but it is an extrapolation across a huge range, not a direct stopwatch reading. The *order of magnitude* of the enhancement (≈10¹⁷) is the robust claim; the precise "78 My" is model-dependent.
- **Mechanism is open:** how ODCase achieves 10¹⁷ with no cofactor (transition-state stabilization vs ground-state/substrate destabilization vs electrostatic preorganization) remains actively argued — don't state a single mechanism as settled.
