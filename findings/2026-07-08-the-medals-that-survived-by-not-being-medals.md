# The Medals That Survived by Not Being Medals

## The fact

When Germany occupied Denmark in 1940, two German physicists' gold **Nobel medals** were in danger. **Max von Laue** (1914) and **James Franck** (1925) had sent their 23-karat medals to **Niels Bohr's institute** in Copenhagen for safekeeping — sending gold out of Nazi Germany was a serious crime, and the medals were *engraved with their names*, so the gold wasn't just seizable, it was **evidence that could implicate its owners.**

Bohr and the Hungarian chemist **George de Hevesy** considered burying them, then rejected it — the Germans would dig up the grounds. Instead, de Hevesy **dissolved the medals in aqua regia** ("royal water," the nitro-hydrochloric mix that is one of the only things that dissolves gold). It was slow; he waited hours. The solution turned salmon-pink, then deep orange. He set the **beaker on a shelf among dozens of other coloured chemicals** — and there it sat.

The Germans **scoured the institute** top to bottom. They walked past the orange liquid, taking it for one more innocuous reagent. After VE Day in 1945, de Hevesy returned, found the jar exactly where he'd left it, **precipitated the gold back out**, and the Nobel Foundation **recast the medals** from the original metal.

## Keepers

1. **(fact — primary) The medals survived by ceasing to be medals — and note that the gold was never actually hidden.** It sat in plain sight on an open shelf through the entire occupation. What was hidden was not the *substance* but the *form*. The searchers were looking for gold *objects* — engraved discs — and there were none; there was only an anonymous orange solution that betrayed nothing about what it had been or whom it belonged to. When the thing that endangers an object is its **recognizable form** (here doubly: seizable *and* incriminating), you preserve it not by concealment but by **destroying the form and keeping the substance.** Unrecognizability, not hiding.

2. **(the sharp detail) The form had to die to protect the *owners*, not just the metal.** The engraving is the whole point: an intact medal found in Copenhagen would have exposed von Laue and Franck as having smuggled gold out of the Reich. So dissolving it wasn't only anti-theft — it erased the *identity* the object carried. The gold was innocent; the *inscription* was the liability.
