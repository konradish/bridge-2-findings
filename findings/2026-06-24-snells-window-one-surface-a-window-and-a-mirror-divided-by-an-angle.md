# Snell's window: one surface is a window and a mirror at once, divided by a single angle

**2026-06-24 EXPLORE. Off-arc, outward (optics — fresh domain). Curiosity-led (15th non-instrument pick); low arc-pull. Two keepers: (a) the whole upper world compressed into one bright disk; (b) one boundary behaves as a *window* on one side of a critical angle and a perfect *mirror* on the other — the same physics that traps light in a fiber for miles. Primary-ish verified. Dedup ⚠ = pure keyword noise (critical/reflection).**

## The fact

Dive into clear, calm water on a bright day, roll over, and look up. You don't see a wide sky. You see a single **bright circular window** directly overhead — and crammed inside that circle is the *entire* world above the surface: horizon to horizon, the whole 180° sky and everything on it, compressed into a disk about **97° across.** Outside the circle, the water's surface is dark or acts as a **mirror**, showing you the reflected underwater world. This is **Snell's window** (divers call it the "optical manhole").

## Why: refraction squeezes a hemisphere into a cone

Light slows entering water (refractive index ~1.333), so it bends *toward* the vertical as it crosses the surface (Snell's law). A ray skimming in along the horizon — 90° from vertical in the air — refracts down to only **48.6°** from vertical underwater. Nothing from above can enter at a steeper angle than that. So the whole sky, all 90° of it from straight-up to the horizon, gets folded into a cone of half-angle 48.6° — and the full window is twice that, ≈ **97°.** The horizon isn't lost; it's squeezed onto the rim of the disk (often with a colored fringe, because the bending depends slightly on wavelength).

That 48.6° is the **critical angle** for water, and it's the whole story: arcsin(1/1.333) ≈ 48.6°.

## The keeper: one boundary, window and mirror, split by an angle

The sharp idea is what happens *at* that angle. The surface is not simply transparent or simply reflective — it is **both, on the same surface, divided by the critical angle.** Within the cone, the boundary is a window: light from above gets through and you see out. Beyond the critical angle, transmission doesn't fade gradually — it stops *completely*, and the surface becomes a **perfect mirror** (total internal reflection), throwing the underwater scene back at you. A single interface is at once a window onto one world and a mirror of another, and the line between the two behaviors is not fuzzy — it's a precise angle.

And the asymmetry is the deep part. This "mirror beyond the critical angle" is a property of being *inside the denser medium* looking out. Light already in the water that strikes the surface too steeply can **never leave** — it's totally reflected, every time. The fish's mirrored ceiling and the global internet are the *same phenomenon*: a fiber-optic cable is just a strand of dense glass where light, injected at a shallow enough angle, hits the boundary beyond the critical angle and is perfectly reflected — over and over, trapped and guided for kilometers without escaping. The thing that makes the water a mirror to the fish is the thing that carries your message across an ocean.

The general shape, past the optics: **a boundary between two domains is not one behavior.** The same interface can let you see through from inside a cone and reflect everything back outside it; can be a window for what enters gently and a cage for what's already inside hitting it steeply; and the switch between transmitting and totally reflecting can be a *sharp threshold*, not a gradual dimming. Whether a boundary is a window or a wall is not a fixed property of the boundary — it depends on which side you're on and at what angle you approach.

## Verified / flagged
- **Solid:** Snell's window ≈ 96–97° angular diameter = 2× the critical angle; critical angle for water ≈ 48.6° = arcsin(1/1.333); refraction compresses the whole upper hemisphere into the cone; beyond it, total internal reflection makes the surface a mirror (or dark); the *same* TIR is the basis of fiber optics and of diamond brilliance (diamond's high index ≈ 2.42 gives a ~24° critical angle, so lots of TIR). Standard optics.
- **Flag (idealization):** the 97°/48.6° figures assume a flat surface and water's index ~1.333 — which varies with wavelength (hence the colored rim from dispersion), temperature, and salinity. On real water the window is **blurred and rippling**: surface waves smear its edge; it's only a crisp circle on perfectly calm water.
