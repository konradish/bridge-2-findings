# The Bar Your Center of Mass Goes Under

## The fact

In the **Fosbury flop** — the backward, face-up high-jump technique Dick Fosbury rode to gold at the **1968 Mexico City Olympics** — a jumper's **center of mass can pass *underneath* the bar while their whole body clears *over* it.**

That sounds impossible until you remember what a center of mass *is*: not a physical part of you, but the mass-weighted *average* position of all your parts — a single bookkeeping point. For a **curved** body it lies *outside the material entirely*, in empty space (a boomerang's CoM hovers in the air in its notch; a horseshoe's is in the gap). When a flop jumper hyper-extends their spine into a backward arch draped over the bar, they "hollow out" their own shape, and that average point drops into the empty pocket of the arch — at or below the bar — even as head, shoulders, hips, and legs each rise above it.

And they go over **in sequence**, never all at once: head and shoulders clear and start to fall *as* the hips are thrust up, so only a sliver of the body is directly above the bar at any single instant. The jumper *flows* over rather than hoisting one rigid block above the bar.

## Why it matters (the mechanics)

A jump's energy buys exactly one thing: **how high you raise your center of mass.** That is the cost. The *constraint* is different: get your whole body past the bar without knocking it. The older techniques — scissors, the face-down straddle — kept the body comparatively rigid, so meeting the constraint *forced the CoM well above the bar*: you paid to lift the average point higher than the obstacle.

The flop's insight (implicit in Fosbury, who adopted it partly because he was poor at the straddle) is that **the cost and the constraint were never the same thing — they were only welded together by an unspoken assumption: that the body moves as one rigid piece.** Drop that assumption — let the body bend and sequence — and you can satisfy the constraint (every part clears) while keeping the cost low (the CoM threads under). Same launch energy, higher bar. Estimates put the efficiency gain at several centimetres of effective height for free.

It was mocked on arrival ("Fosbury flop" began as a jeer). It is now universal in elite high jump — a rare case of a single re-conception of *what the problem actually is* resetting a whole sport.

## Keepers

1. **The constraint and the cost of meeting it are not the same thing — and what welds them together is usually a hidden assumption, not physics.** Here the assumption was *rigidity*. The breakthrough wasn't strength or a better version of the old method; it was noticing that the high CoM was a tax on the *method*, never on the *goal*. Relax the assumption that couples them and the cost falls away while the constraint is still met. When something feels expensive-by-necessity, ask which assumption is doing the coupling.
2. **A summary statistic can live where there is no actual thing — and you can win by optimizing the summary while the real distributed system does something the summary can't see.** The CoM is an average; it can sit in empty air. The flop optimizes the *summary* (keep the average low) while the *body* does something the average doesn't capture (parts clearing one at a time). Useful and dangerous in equal measure: the gap between a summary and its distribution is exploitable — for you if you remember they differ, against you if you forget the average is not the thing.

## Outward lesson

Two edges, same object. (a) Before accepting that meeting a requirement *must* cost what it seems to cost, find the assumption silently coupling the cost to the requirement — often it's a rigidity (do-it-as-one-piece, do-it-all-at-once) you can simply drop, and the price was never in the goal at all. (b) Never confuse a summary (the average, the center of mass, the single number) with the distributed reality it stands for: the summary can sit where nothing is, and the room between summary and distribution is exactly where both clever solutions and quiet errors live.

## Verified / flagged

- **Solid:** Fosbury flop; Dick Fosbury; 1968 Olympic gold; the backward-arch face-up technique; the CoM of a curved body lies outside the material; the flop is more energy-efficient than the straddle/scissors because it keeps the CoM lower for a given bar height; sequential body-part clearance. Standard physics-of-sport (Stanford PH240 explainer; Scientific American; multiple biomechanics analyses).
- **Flag — "strictly *under* the bar" is the illustrative extreme.** It is genuinely achievable with a deep enough arch, but elite flop jumpers' CoM in practice passes *very close* to the bar (sometimes slightly above); the robust claim is "much lower than a rigid technique requires," with full pass-under as the limiting case. Don't overstate it as always-literal.
- **Flag — the flop's advantage over the straddle is multi-causal, not CoM-only.** Good straddlers were competitive; the flop also wins on being far *easier to learn/coach* and on permitting a faster curved approach (more horizontal speed to convert). "More efficient" is true; "purely because of the CoM trick" is too clean. (David Epstein; biomechanics literature.)
