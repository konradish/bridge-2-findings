## The fact

The **White Cliffs of Dover** — those ~100-metre white walls at the edge of England — are **chalk**, and chalk is the compacted skeletons of **plankton.** Specifically, **coccoliths**: the microscopic calcium-carbonate plates of single-celled floating algae called **coccolithophores**, each plate a few *microns* across.

For roughly **30 million years** in the Late Cretaceous (~100–66 million years ago), when shallow warm seas covered much of Europe, these algae bloomed and died and rained down as a white calcareous ooze that accumulated to depths of **~500 metres** and slowly compacted into rock. The geological period is literally *named* for the deposit — *Cretaceous*, from Latin *creta*, chalk. An entire iconic landscape is the patient pile-up of the almost-invisibly-small.

And it isn't finished. The dominant modern coccolithophore, ***Emiliania huxleyi***, still forms **massive blooms** — up to 10⁸ cells per litre, turning thousands of square kilometres of ocean milky and **visible from space.** These organisms have been, by one account, **the most important long-term CO₂ sink on Earth for the last ~100 million years**: by building calcite shells they pull carbon out of circulation and, when they sink, take it down.

## Keepers

1. **(fact — primary) A vast solid thing is the compounded remainder of the vanishingly small, given enough time.** No coccolith is visible to the eye; the cliffs are visible from the sea for miles. The bridge between the two scales is only *duration* — 30 million years of a slow rain that never individually amounted to anything. Nothing here concentrated or was engineered; it just did not stop.

2. **(fact — still-running) The process that built the monument is ongoing and load-bearing.** The same kind of bloom that made the cliffs whitens the ocean today and quietly runs the planet's longest carbon sink. The Cretaceous version is not a closed historical event; it is the visible sediment of a machine still turning.
