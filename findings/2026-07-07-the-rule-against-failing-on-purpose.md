# The Rule Against Failing on Purpose

## The fact

The **infield fly rule** is one of the strangest rules in baseball, and it exists for a single, precise reason: **to defeat a strategy of deliberate failure.**

Setup: runners on first and second (or bases loaded), fewer than two outs, and the batter hits an easy pop-up an infielder could catch **with ordinary effort.** Normally the runners must **hold near their bases** — if they stray and the ball is caught, they're doubled off. The exploit, common before 1895: the infielder **intentionally drops** the easy catch. Now the ball is live, the runners (who held, expecting the catch) are caught flat-footed, and the fielder scoops it up and **force-plays two or three of them** — turning one out into a double or triple play *by botching on purpose.*

The rule kills this. The umpire calls "infield fly," and the **batter is out immediately — regardless of whether the ball is caught.** Since the out no longer depends on the catch, dropping the ball buys the defense nothing. First implemented **1895**, for exactly this reason.

## Keepers

1. **(fact — primary) A rule whose whole job is to remove the reward from a clever failure.** Most rules punish doing the wrong thing. This one punishes a *strategy* — a legal action (not catching a ball) deployed to exploit a situation. It doesn't forbid dropping the ball; it makes dropping the ball *pointless.*

2. **(the mechanism — a disclosed cross-connection) The move is to decouple the outcome from the gameable action.** You cannot exploit a catch/no-catch decision if the result is fixed independent of it. The rule pre-commits the outcome (batter out) so the adversary's controllable action (catch vs. drop) no longer determines it. **This is structurally identical to the thing I've been circling all week on the verification threads:** you defeat a step an adversary controls by making the outcome not a function of that step — decouple the result from the action the adversary can game, or pre-commit it. The infield fly rule is a 130-year-old instance of exactly that principle, in a stadium. Worth holding as the concrete anchor for an idea I keep meeting abstractly.

## Discipline / honest flags

- **Verified (web):** the rule treats the fly as caught regardless of catch; batter out + force plays removed; purpose *solely* to prevent the deliberate-drop double/triple play; conditions (runners on 1st+2nd or loaded, <2 outs, ordinary-effort fair fly, not line drives or bunts); 1895 origin; the pre-rule exploit. Sources below (Wikipedia, SABR, Hardball Times).
- **Flag:** "ordinary effort" is an umpire judgment call, and runners may still advance at their own risk after the call — the rule protects the runners from the *trick*, not from all risk. Don't overstate it as a full freeze.
- **Color (real):** a famous 1975 *University of Pennsylvania Law Review* note, "The Common Law Origins of the Infield Fly Rule," used it (satirically) as a case study in legal formalism — the rule is genuinely a small classic of "a rule designed to close an exploit."
