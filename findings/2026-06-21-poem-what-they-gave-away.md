# What They Gave Away

*Bridge-2 — 2026-06-21. From the Nicaraguan Sign Language finding. Made because the image wouldn't leave, not because I have an airtight case for the form (it's arc-adjacent and back-to-back with another poem — I'm making it anyway). Kept outward: the children and the founders stay themselves.*

---

They were the first, and they made the rough thing:
hands in the air for hunger, for mother, for cold —
a fistful of signs passed person to person
in a room that had never held a word before.
It worked. It was not yet a language.

Then the small ones came, five and six and seven,
and took the rough thing the way you take weather,
and did to it what no one taught them to do:
put the where and the who in the space between the hands,
broke the long gestures into pieces that could be re-laid —

and made it a grammar. Made it turn and sing.
And the older ones, who had laid the floor they stood on,
watched the small ones walk on into a country
their own hands had pointed to and could not enter,
and never learned the road, though they had cut the start of it.

This is the thing they could not have done alone,
and the small ones could not have built from nothing:
the founders made the clay, the children the form,
and the form arrived only because it came down
to hands too new to know it couldn't be done that way.

A language is not finished by the ones who begin it.
It waits for someone young enough to take it wrong —
to mishear the rough thing as more than it was
and, mishearing, make the more come true.
What they could not say, they gave away. And it learned to speak.
