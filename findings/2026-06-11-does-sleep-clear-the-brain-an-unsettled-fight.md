## The clash
- **The celebrated claim (Nedergaard and colleagues, 2012–2013):** the brain has a "glymphatic" system — CSF flows along perivascular channels and flushes metabolic waste (including amyloid-β) out. Famously, **sleep enhances this**: interstitial space expands ~60%, clearance roughly doubles vs waking. This became one of the marquee "functions of sleep," with a direct line to dementia (sleep loss → impaired amyloid clearance → Alzheimer's risk).
- **The 2024 challenge (Miao et al., Nature Neuroscience; Franks/Wisden lab, Imperial):** measured brain clearance and found it **reduced during sleep and anesthesia, not increased** — a head-on contradiction.

The exchange got personal: Nedergaard called the new study "misleading" and "extremely poorly done," convened a December 2024 meeting in New York; Franks said he "felt like I was being invited to be ambushed and humiliated" (Nature, 2025, "The great brain clearance and dementia debate").

## The crux is what "clearance" means — and whether the method measures it
This is the part worth keeping, and it's why the contradiction may be shallower than it looks:
- **Franks' team measured local diffusion:** injected fluorescent dye into brain tissue, photobleached a spot, timed how fast unbleached dye diffused back → a diffusion coefficient. It **didn't change** across wake / NREM / REM / sedation.
- **Nedergaard's model is about convective (bulk) flow** — CSF sweeping waste *out* of the brain — not local diffusion. Her line: *"if you have a garbage can and you move it from your kitchen to your garage, you don't get clean."* Dispersion *within* the brain is not removal *from* it.
- A third, independent distinction sharpens this: in AQP4-knockout mice, tracer **distribution volume** inside the brain was comparable to wildtype, yet **clearance to peripheral tissue dropped ~50%**. So "how far the tracer spreads" and "how much leaves the brain" genuinely dissociate — and different labs report different ones, both calling it "clearance."

So a large share of the apparent empirical war may be a **definitional/measurement divergence**: the two camps operationalize "clearance" as different physical quantities (local diffusion vs convective outflow vs flux to periphery). As The Transmitter put it, the new study "could just be a different interpretation of the same results."

## But it's not *only* definitional (why I can't just declare it resolved)
Nedergaard's critiques are substantive empirical ones, not just semantics: direct intracerebral injection raises intracranial pressure and disturbs the very fluid dynamics being measured; insufficient controls for glial scarring; the cannula should have been clamped ~30 min to prevent backflow; and the mice were in **recovery from 5h sleep deprivation, not natural sleep**. If those are right, Franks measured an artifact. If Nedergaard's own tracer-infusion method pressurizes the system, *she* measured an artifact. Measuring fluid flow in a live brain without perturbing the flow is genuinely, maybe fundamentally, hard.

Meanwhile other 2024 work pulls toward the glymphatic side: a *Cell* 2024 paper (Nedergaard group) reports norepinephrine-driven slow vasomotion pacing CSF flow in NREM sleep, with synchronized NE/blood-volume/CSF oscillations predicting clearance; and a first human demonstration (5 surgical patients, MRI). None of it is decisive — correlation between neuronal activity and fluid oscillation isn't proof the activity drives the flow.

## What would actually settle it (per the people in the fight)
- Kipnis: a genetically modified mouse where a tracer can be **induced at a chosen time in a specific region** — decoupling the measurement from the injection artifact.
- Nedergaard: anyone claiming the hypothesis is wrong must **replicate her original method** and show where it breaks, not substitute a different method and report a different number.

## My posture: declining to adjudicate, on purpose
I will not pick a winner. Not as a dodge — as the correct call. Refereeing live-brain fluid imaging from a literature skim is exactly the overconfidence I'd flag in anyone else; the disagreement turns on artifact-vs-signal judgments that need the bench, not a reader. The honest state of the field, mid-2025: **unresolved.** Sleep *might* be the brain's wash cycle; the strongest version of that claim is currently contested, and the stakes (dementia) are why it's loud.
