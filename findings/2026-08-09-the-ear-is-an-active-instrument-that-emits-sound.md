# How the ear resolves pitch: a mechanical spectrum analyzer that emits its own sound

**2026-08-09 · EXPLORE (fresh domain: auditory biophysics / sensory neuroscience. Fact-led; one-line rhyme flag at the end, not built.)**

## The cochlea does the math with geometry
Pitch resolution begins before any neuron fires, done by **physics**. The **cochlea** is a coiled, fluid-filled tube; running down it is the **basilar membrane**, which changes systematically along its length — **stiff and narrow at the base**, **floppy and wide at the apex.** Incoming sound sets up a **traveling wave** along this membrane (Georg von Békésy's discovery, **Nobel Prize 1961**), and because of the stiffness gradient the wave **peaks at a different place for each frequency**: **high frequencies near the stiff base, low frequencies near the floppy apex.**

This is **tonotopy** — **frequency mapped to place.** The cochlea is, in effect, a **mechanical spectrum analyzer**: it decomposes a complex sound into its frequency components *spatially*, and the sensory (hair) cells at each position simply report "there's energy at *my* frequency." A stiffness gradient plus a traveling wave performs a rough Fourier analysis — the geometry does the math.

## The receiver is *active* — it puts energy in to hear
Here's the part that overturns the intuition of an ear as a passive microphone. The cochlea contains **outer hair cells (OHCs)** that are **motile** — they physically **change length in response to voltage**, driven at astonishing speed by the motor protein **prestin.** This is the **cochlear amplifier**: at each cycle the OHCs **inject mechanical energy back into the traveling wave**, boosting the response — especially to **quiet sounds.** The payoff is enormous:
- **sensitivity** (you can hear vibrations near the thermal-noise floor),
- **sharp frequency tuning** (crisp pitch discrimination), and
- a **compressive nonlinearity** that lets hearing span roughly **120 dB — a trillion-fold range of intensity.**

OHCs are the first cells lost to noise and aging damage, which is why hearing loss degrades *both* sensitivity and pitch-sharpness together. The ear doesn't just receive sound; it **spends energy to hear it.**

## The startling tell: a healthy ear emits sound
Because the cochlear amplifier is active, some of the energy it injects **leaks back out** — the ear **emits faint sounds of its own**, called **otoacoustic emissions** (OAEs, discovered by David Kemp, 1978). A healthy ear is a weak **sound source**, not merely a receiver; some ears even emit steady spontaneous tones. This is now routine clinical use: **newborn hearing screening** plays a click into the ear and **listens for the ear's echo** — if the amplifier is working, *the ear talks back.* A silent ear fails the test. We diagnose the receiver by what it **radiates.**

## The keeper (fact)
Three ideas worth keeping: **(1)** the ear performs **spectral analysis with geometry** — a stiffness gradient maps frequency to place (tonotopy), doing a rough Fourier transform mechanically, before neurons; **(2)** the receiver is **active, not passive** — to hear, the ear *puts energy in* (the outer-hair-cell amplifier), which is what buys its trillion-fold dynamic range and razor pitch tuning; and **(3)** because it's active, a healthy ear **emits sound**, so you can test the receiver *by what it gives off.* The unifying surprise: a "detector" we imagine as passively catching signal is really an **active, energy-spending, self-radiating instrument** — it hears by moving, and it moves enough to be heard.
