# The Deadliest Tree Is a Protected Species

## The fact

The **manchineel** (*Hippomane mancinella*), a Caribbean and Florida beach tree in the spurge family, is recorded by Guinness as the **world's most dangerous tree** — and *every part of it is toxic.* Its milky sap (phorbol esters and a cocktail of other compounds) causes severe blistering on contact. The fruit is a small, sweet-smelling, apple-like thing — its name comes from Spanish *manzanilla*, "little apple," and today it's **"manzanilla de la muerte," the little apple of death.** Native peoples coated arrows with the sap; Ponce de León is thought to have died of a manchineel-poisoned arrow in 1521.

The detail that stops you: **you don't have to touch it.** Stand beneath a manchineel *in the rain* and your skin blisters — a single raindrop that has fallen through the leaves, carrying a trace of sap, is enough to raise a welt. The danger travels in the water. "Don't touch it" understates the case; the honest warning is "don't be under it wet."

And here is the fact worth keeping: **this tree is a protected, endangered species.** In Florida it is legally protected — because the manchineel grows on beaches and in brackish swamps where its roots **hold the shoreline against erosion** and its canopy breaks the wind. The most dangerous tree in the world is one the law forbids you to remove.

## Keepers

1. **(fact — primary) The thing you most need to keep away from is a thing you are required to keep.** The manchineel is lethal in every part *and* ecologically load-bearing — so it is, in the same gesture, **fenced off and protected**: don't touch it, and don't cut it down. A hazard you cannot eliminate because you depend on it. We usually sort the world into things to preserve and things to remove; the manchineel refuses the sort — it is a danger the coast cannot afford to lose, and so it gets a warning sign *and* a legal shield, at once.

2. **(fact — the delivery detail) The harm doesn't require contact; the medium carries it.** Rain through the canopy becomes the vector. It's a clean reminder that a danger's *reach* isn't its surface — proximity plus the right medium extends it well past the thing itself.
