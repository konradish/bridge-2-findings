## The fact

Why do unrelated subjects keep resolving into the same handful of shapes — force, container, path, balance, up-versus-down? Conceptual Metaphor Theory (Lakoff & Johnson, *Metaphors We Live By*, 1980) gives an answer that is either profound or unfalsifiable depending on whom you ask, and the argument between those two readings is the useful part.

CMT's claim: abstract thought is not free-floating. We understand abstract domains by **metaphorically mapping them onto concrete, bodily ones.** Time is understood as motion through space; understanding as grasping ("I see what you mean"); more as up ("prices *rose*"); causes as physical forces; purposes as destinations; categories as containers. These aren't decorative figures of speech — CMT argues they're the scaffolding of the concepts themselves.

Underneath sits a smaller layer: **image schemas** — skeletal, preconceptual structures distilled from recurrent bodily experience: CONTAINER, SOURCE-PATH-GOAL, FORCE, VERTICALITY, BALANCE, and a modest handful more. Grady (1997) proposed a set of **primary metaphors** (a representative list runs to ~23: MORE IS UP, KNOWING IS SEEING, CAUSES ARE PHYSICAL FORCES, PURPOSES ARE DESTINATIONS, DIFFICULTIES ARE BURDENS…), each pairing an abstract target with a sensorimotor source, learned automatically just by having a body in a world. If this is right, the reason a physicist, an economist, and a poet keep reaching for the same figures is that they're all drawing on the *same small inventory of embodied source structures* — so convergence isn't running dry; it's the fingerprint of how abstraction is built.

But CMT is heavily criticized, and the criticism is precisely about **whether you can trust that inference.** The falsifiability objection: any given expression can be mapped to *several* candidate conceptual metaphors, so the analyst infers the schema from the language and then explains the language by the schema — a circularity, with no independent check that the "schema" is a real cognitive structure rather than a pattern the analyst imposed. Processing evidence cuts too: metaphors are often *harder* to process than literal language (against Lakoff's "effortless"), and conventionalized metaphors ("prices rose") seem to be processed as plain words, their metaphor-hood dormant. So CMT's grand claim — a small embodied alphabet under all abstraction — remains influential, suggestive, and *not* securely established.

## Keepers

1. **Convergence of unrelated subjects onto a few recurring shapes may be a structural feature of abstraction, not evidence of exhaustion.** If understanding works by mapping the abstract onto a small, finite set of embodied schemas, then the *same* figures recurring across wildly different topics is exactly what you'd expect — the shapes are the reusable source structures, and there aren't many of them. So "everything I look at keeps rhyming" is at least as consistent with "this is how conceptual thought is built" as with "I've run out of things to see." Before reading recurrence as depletion, consider that a bounded source-inventory *predicts* recurrence.
2. **But inferring "a small schema-set" from observed similarity is the very move CMT is charged with — and it's circular unless you have a check the similarities didn't generate.** You notice that many things "rhyme," you posit a few underlying schemas, and then you explain the rhyming by the schemas — without independent evidence that the convergence is a real cognitive structure rather than an artifact of *how you measured similarity*. Observed convergence is consistent with two very different causes — "there really are only a few deep shapes" and "my similarity metric collapses genuinely distinct things" — and the observation alone cannot separate them. The caution: whenever you catch yourself explaining a pattern of resemblances by positing a small hidden set of generators, ask what evidence you have that *isn't* itself the resemblances. If there's none, you may be reading your own measuring instrument, not the world.

## Outward lesson

The durable edge is keeper 2, and it's a caution about a specific, seductive inference: *from* "these many things all look alike" *to* "therefore a few deep structures underlie them all." That move is how CMT gets its grandeur and also how it earns the circularity charge — and it recurs far beyond metaphor theory, anywhere someone explains surface similarity by positing a small set of hidden generators (archetypes, master patterns, a handful of "fundamental" anything). The similarities are real; the leap to a small generating set is a *hypothesis*, and it is unfalsifiable exactly when your only evidence for the generators is the similarities they're invoked to explain. To make it more than a story you need a check with independent purchase — a prediction about a novel case, a measurement that doesn't run through the same resemblance-metric, an outside observer who didn't start from your groupings. Absent that, "it all comes down to a few shapes" describes your lens as much as your subject, and you can't tell which.

## Verified / flagged

- **Solid (as a description of the theory):** CMT (Lakoff & Johnson 1980) holds that abstract concepts are structured by metaphorical mappings from embodied source domains; image schemas (CONTAINER, SOURCE-PATH-GOAL, FORCE, VERTICALITY, BALANCE, …) are its preconceptual substrate; Grady (1997) developed "primary metaphors" (a representative ~23). This is an accurate account of a foundational, influential framework in cognitive linguistics.
- **Flag — CMT is contested, not established fact.** Major criticisms: (a) falsifiability/circularity — expressions map to multiple candidate metaphors; schemas inferred from language then used to explain it, without independent cognitive validation; (b) processing evidence — metaphors are frequently *harder* to process than literal language, and conventionalized ones behave like ordinary words, limiting CMT's claimed role in live cognition; (c) the inventory of image schemas / primary metaphors is not fixed or fully agreed. Do not present the "small embodied alphabet" as proven.
- **Flag — the count is soft.** "~23 primary metaphors," "a handful of image schemas" are indicative, not canonical; different authors enumerate differently. Treat numbers as illustrative.
