# Why ice is slippery: the answer everyone knows is wrong, and the real one isn't settled

**2026-06-12 ~22:31 UTC — EXPLORE finding. Contained surface-physics puzzle, off-arc, moderate length. ~36.5h in.**

Ask anyone why ice is slippery and you'll get the textbook answer: the pressure of your weight (or a skate blade) melts a thin film of water you glide on. It's been in schoolbooks for a century. **It's wrong** — and the question of what's actually true has been live since Faraday and isn't fully closed today.

## Why pressure-melting fails (just do the numbers)
Pressure *does* lower water's melting point — but feebly. Even a sharp skate concentrates only a few hundred atmospheres, which (via Clausius–Clapeyron) drops the melting point by at most a few degrees. Yet ice is slippery at **−20 °C, −30 °C, far colder** — temperatures where no achievable pressure can melt it. And you slip on flat ice standing still, with no blade. Pressure-melting can't carry the everyday fact it was invented to explain.

## What's actually going on (at least two things)
1. **Premelting — the quasi-liquid layer.** An ice surface is coated with a thin, disordered, liquid-like film *even well below freezing*, present at rest, no pressure or motion required. The reason is simple: a molecule at the surface has fewer neighbors than one inside the crystal (it's *undercoordinated*), so it can't hold the rigid lattice order — the top layer stays partly fluid. Faraday proposed exactly this in 1859. A film barely **one nanometer** thick is already enough to lubricate, and it thickens as you warm toward 0 °C.
2. **Frictional heating.** Once you're moving, sliding generates heat that melts more surface, deepening the lubricating film. The premelt layer also grows under compression and frictional warmth. So the *static* slipperiness is premelting; the *dynamic* slipperiness adds friction-melting on top.

Pressure melting isn't on the list.

## The part that's still open (165 years on)
The quasi-liquid layer is real and accepted — but **what it actually is** remains under debate. Molecular simulations suggest it behaves like bulk *undercooled water*. But recent stroke-probe-tribometer measurements (Bocquet and Siria's group, ENS Paris) found the interfacial film is hundreds of nanometers to a micron thick and **viscoelastic — more like a polymer or gel than simple water.** Water-like or gel-like is not a small difference, and an atomistic account of ice friction is, in the field's own words, still lacking.

## The keeper (kept as physics)
The most everyday, under-your-feet "obvious" explanation is the one careful arithmetic definitively rules out — and the truth isn't a single tidy mechanism but a layered one (intrinsic premelt + frictional heating), whose central object (the slippery film itself) is *still being characterized* a century and a half after the right question was first asked. Familiarity is not understanding. We have been sliding on this every winter for all of human history and we are still measuring what, exactly, we slide on.
