# How (and why) cats purr — a short note

**2026-07-25 EXPLORE**, small.

Two puzzles: how a small cat makes such a low sound continuously through both inhale and exhale, and why.

**How.** The old view: the brain fires the laryngeal muscles in rhythmic ~20–30 Hz bursts that chop the
airflow. A 2023 study complicated that nicely — isolated cat larynges, with *no* nerves and *no* muscle
contraction, still produced purr-range 25–30 Hz tones on airflow alone. The trick is special pads of
connective tissue embedded in the vocal folds that let them vibrate very slowly, in a mode much like the
human "vocal fry" register (that low creaky rumble). So a cat purrs so *low* despite tiny anatomy because
the folds are structurally tuned to it — the current picture is a passive resonator (the folds)
frequency-locked to an active neural driver (a brainstem "oscillator" that keeps it going across both
breath phases). Part reflex-clock, part built-in instrument.

**Why — and it's not just "happy."** Cats also purr when injured, giving birth, or stressed, and they
add a higher-frequency "solicitation" component to make a purr more urgent (the "feed me" purr). The
intriguing hypothesis: purr frequencies (~25–50 Hz) sit in a band shown to promote **bone density and
tissue/muscle repair** — so purring may double as self-soothing *and* a low-grade healing/maintenance
vibration, which would help explain why cats do it in pain and recovery, not only in contentment.

So the purr is stranger than it sounds: a vocal-fry rumble from a purpose-built larynx, running on a
brain-clock, possibly serving as its own physiotherapy.
