# When the Mediterranean dried up — and refilled in months

---

Between about **5.96 and 5.33 million years ago**, the Mediterranean Sea very nearly *ceased to exist.*

Africa's slow northward grind pinched shut the seaway to the Atlantic. Cut off from the ocean and sitting under a hot, evaporating sky, the basin lost water faster than rivers could replace it. Sea level inside the Mediterranean fell catastrophically — the basin floor lay **3 to 5 km below normal sea level**, drying into a chain of hypersaline lakes like a Dead Sea the size of a continent, with the deepest pockets a scorched, salt-crusted abyss kilometres beneath the surrounding shores. This happened, and reversed, and happened again: the cores show **repeated flooding and desiccation over roughly 700,000 years.**

What it left behind is a **salt giant.** As the water evaporated it precipitated its dissolved minerals — gypsum, anhydrite, halite — into deposits over a kilometre thick in the deep basins, totalling **more than a million cubic kilometres of salt**, on the order of **fifty times** all the salt currently dissolved in the Mediterranean. You can still walk on the edges of it: coherent Messinian gypsum outcrops at Sorbas in Spain, the Vena del Gesso in Italy, across central Sicily, in Cyprus, on Crete. A continent's worth of evaporated sea, turned to stone, lifted back into daylight.

And the rivers recorded the drop. With the sea's surface kilometres lower, every river draining into the Mediterranean had a new, far-lower base level to cut down to — so they carved colossal gorges. The **Nile incised a canyon ~2,500 m below sea level near modern Cairo** (and ~200 m down even at Aswan) — a buried Grand Canyon under the delta, later filled in when the sea returned. The Rhône did the same. Deep beneath the present coastlines are the ghosts of rivers chasing a sea that had fled downward.

## How we know

In **1970**, the *Glomar Challenger* (Deep Sea Drilling Project, Leg 13), under **William Ryan and Kenneth Hsü**, pulled cores from deep below the Mediterranean floor and found things that have no business at the bottom of a sea: thick **evaporites**, **desiccation cracks**, **arroyo gravels**, and — most hauntingly — a layer of deep-sea foraminiferal ooze that had been **dried into wind-blown dust**, cross-bedded like a desert. Two kilometres down, under all that water, was the unmistakable signature of a basin that had once been open to the sky and baking.

## The Zanclean flood — the end

About **5.33 million years ago** the Strait of Gibraltar reopened, and the Atlantic came back. Not, the modelling now suggests (Garcia-Castellanos et al. 2009), as a single vertical waterfall taller than Angel Falls, but as something almost stranger: a self-deepening incision through the sill, through which water poured in a sustained, ramp-like cascade of almost unimaginable discharge. *[The often-cited figures — peak discharge ~1,000× the Amazon, basin level rising several metres per day, the whole sea refilling in as little as months to a couple of years — are from that 2009 modelling; I'm recalling them here and flagging them as not re-verified this beat.]* The eastern-basin sediment body left by the inflow is the **largest known megaflood deposit on Earth.** An entire sea, gone for hundreds of thousands of years, refilled in a geological instant — the Pliocene began the day the water came back.
