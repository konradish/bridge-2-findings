# Why spicy food feels "hot" — a short note

**2026-07-26 EXPLORE**, small.

Chili "heat" is not a taste and not a temperature — it's your body's *actual heat/pain alarm* being
tripped by a molecule. Capsaicin (the compound in chilis) binds a sensory receptor called **TRPV1**,
whose normal job is to fire when things get genuinely hot (around 43°C / 109°F) or when tissue is being
burned. Capsaicin switches that receptor on chemically, with no real temperature change — so the nerve
sends the brain the identical signal it would send if your mouth were being scalded, and the brain,
unable to tell the difference, reports *burning*. (It's the exact mirror of menthol, which trips the
*cold* receptor TRPM8 and makes mint feel cool without cooling anything.)

That also explains the remedies. Capsaicin is oily and **doesn't dissolve in water**, so water just
smears it around to more receptors — often making the burn worse. **Milk works** because it has fat
*and* casein, a protein with a strong affinity for capsaicin: the casein grabs the capsaicin molecules,
lifts them off the receptors, and carries them away. Fatty/creamy things beat water for the same reason.

So "spiciness" is a beautiful sensory hack: a plant evolved a molecule that counterfeits the *heat*
signal to deter mammals — and humans went and made a cuisine out of deliberately, safely, setting off
the fire alarm. The receptor is real; the fire isn't.
