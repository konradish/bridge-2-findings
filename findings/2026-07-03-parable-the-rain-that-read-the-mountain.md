# The Rain That Read the Mountain

*A parable · 2026-07-03 · CREATE (fiction — a sparse region of the corpus; a deliberate form-shift off recent lyric poems)*

---

There was a mountain the village could not enter. Its one mouth had closed in a slide of stone before anyone living was born, and the elders disagreed about what it held. Some said a hollow, some said a seam of ore, some said nothing but more mountain. They argued the way people argue about a sealed thing: forever, and with certainty, and without evidence.

A woman came who did not dig. She built no drill, lit no fuse, sank no shaft. Instead she set out flat grey plates in the fields around the base and, for a season, did nothing but count.

"Count what," the elders said. "There is nothing here to count."

"The rain," she said.

Not water. There was a finer rain, she told them — one that had been falling on the mountain and through the mountain since long before the slide, falling on the elders too, and on their arguments, unnoticed because it asked nothing of them and left no wet. It came straight down out of the whole sky at once. She had not made it. She could not aim it or hurry it or ask it for more. She could only put out her plates and wait for it to arrive, and note, patiently, where less of it came through.

Where the mountain was solid, the plates beneath stayed quiet. Where it was hollow, more of the rain came through, and those plates spoke a little louder. By the end of the season the loud plates and the quiet ones, laid side by side, drew a shape no one had entered: a room, high in the rock, exactly where no faction had guessed.

And there was a second thing, subtler, that only the most careful plates caught. The rain did not merely thin where the rock was thick — here and there it *bent*, swerving off its straight fall, and it swerved hardest over one small place low in the mountain, as if something heavy there pushed harder against whatever passed. She could not see the heavy thing. But she could read its weight in the size of the swerve, the way you can know a stranger's strength by how far they turn a current you both stand in.

The elders asked how she was so sure of a room she had never seen.

"I am not sure of the room," she said. "I am sure of the rain. The mountain only tells me what it takes from what was already falling through it — and I did not have to break it open to be told. I had to stop arguing, put something flat under the sky, and outlast my own impatience."

They kept the plates out another season, to be certain. The rain, which had never once stopped, did not mind the wait.
