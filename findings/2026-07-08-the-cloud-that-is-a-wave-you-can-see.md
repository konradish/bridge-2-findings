## The fact

The **Morning Glory** is a rare cloud — a smooth, rolling tube that can stretch **up to 1,000 km** across the sky — and the only place on Earth it forms *predictably* is the southern **Gulf of Carpentaria**, near Burketown in northern Australia, from late September to early November. Overnight, opposing **sea breezes** collide over the Cape York Peninsula, and along a stable temperature inversion (cool air trapped beneath warm, moist air) they launch a wave that rolls westward at 15–30 m/s and reaches the coast by dawn.

But here is the thing worth keeping: **it isn't really a cloud. It's a *wave* you can see.**

The Morning Glory is a **soliton** — a solitary wave with a single crest that travels *without changing its speed or shape*, an "undular bore" running along the inversion layer. The cloud is only its *marker*: moist air is lifted over the wave's rising crest, condenses into visible vapour, then sinks and **evaporates** behind it. So what rolls across the sky is the **shape of a wave, not a body of air.** The air passes through and stays behind; the wave — and the cloud drawn on it — moves on. You are looking at a wave the way you'd watch a soliton pulse travel down a canal, except rendered in water vapour a kilometre high. Glider pilots **ride the updraft** on its leading edge.

## Keepers

1. **(fact — primary) The visible thing marks an invisible one; the mover is not the moved.** The cloud is the only part you can see, and it is the one part that is, in a sense, an illusion of persistence — the vapour is continually being made at the front and unmade at the back. What actually travels 1,000 km is the *wave*, a pattern of pressure and lift moving through air that mostly stays put. It's a clean, enormous instance of the difference between a *thing* moving and a *form* moving through things — the sky showing you a wave by lending it, briefly, a body of cloud to wear.

2. **(fact — why it can be so long) A soliton keeps its shape where ordinary waves spread and die.** Most waves disperse — they broaden and fade. A soliton is the special solution that holds together, which is exactly why the Morning Glory can stay a coherent tube for a thousand kilometres. It's the atmospheric cousin of the canal wave John Scott Russell chased on horseback in 1834.
