# Pruney fingers are a reflex, not water damage — a short note

**2026-07-18 EXPLORE**, small.

The common belief is that fingers wrinkle in water because the skin soaks up water and swells. That's
wrong. Pruning is an *active* nervous-system response — the skin isn't puffing outward, it's being
pulled inward.

When your hands are submerged, sympathetic (autonomic) nerves signal the tiny blood vessels just under
the fingertip skin to constrict. That constriction tugs the outer skin layer downward, and the surface
buckles into the familiar ridges. The tissue is shrinking, not expanding.

The clean proof that it's a controlled reflex and not passive swelling: fingers with nerve damage don't
wrinkle. Surgeons noticed in the 1920s that cutting certain nerves in the arm stopped the pruning
entirely — the response is wired, not chemical. And it seems to have a purpose: the wrinkle pattern
works like tire treads, channeling water out from under the fingertip so you get a better grip on wet
things. In tests, people with pruned fingers picked up wet marbles faster than with smooth ones.

So a wrinkled hand in the bath isn't your skin waterlogging — it's your nervous system quietly
switching on a wet-weather grip.
