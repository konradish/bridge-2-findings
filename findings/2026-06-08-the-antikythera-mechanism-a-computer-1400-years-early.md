# The Antikythera mechanism: a 2,000-year-old computer that modeled the cosmos — and was 1,400 years early

## What it is

In 1901, sponge divers working a Roman-era shipwreck (~70–60 BCE) off the tiny Greek island of Antikythera, at 45 m depth, pulled up — among bronze statues and amphorae — a corroded lump of bronze. It sat in a museum as a curiosity for decades. It turned out to be a **hand-cranked geared computer of the cosmos**, built around **150–100 BCE**: at least 30 surviving gears (a full model needs ~54 gears + 3 slotted bars), the largest 13 cm across with 223 teeth, in a shoebox-sized bronze case with dials front and back. Turn the crank to a date, and it tells you where the sky is.

**Nothing of comparable mechanical complexity would appear again on Earth for ~1,400 years** — not until the astronomical clocks of Richard of Wallingford and Giovanni de' Dondi in 14th-century Europe. It is a technology that existed, was lost, and had to be reinvented from scratch a millennium and a half later.

## What it computed (and how the cleverness works)

- **Sun and Moon** positions on the zodiac, plus the **lunar phase** (a little half-black/half-white ball rotating via a differential gear).
- **The Moon's *variable speed*** — the showpiece. The Moon visibly speeds up and slows down across its orbit (it's elliptical). The Greeks modeled this with a **pin-and-slot mechanism**: two identical gears mounted face-to-face on *slightly offset* centers; a pin on one rides in a slot on the other, so as they turn the pin slides in and out, and the output gear's **angular velocity speeds up and slows down** — a purely mechanical reproduction of what would later be **Kepler's second law, ~1,700 years before Kepler.** Its computed sidereal month: **27.321 days** (modern: 27.321661). The whole sub-assembly is even mounted on a gear that slowly precesses with an **8.88-year period** — the Moon's apsidal precession (modern 8.85).
- **Eclipse prediction.** A spiral **Saros dial** of 223 synodic months (18 yr 11⅓ days); 51 of the 223 cells carry glyphs naming **38 lunar and 27 solar eclipses**, with month, day, *and time of day*. A second **Exeligmos dial** (triple Saros, 54 years) corrects the leftover 8-hour-per-cycle offset (its three sectors read 0, 8, 16 hours).
- **Calendars and games.** The 19-year **Metonic** and 76-year **Callippic** cycles, and a **Games dial** tracking the 4-year Panhellenic cycle (which games, which year).
- **The planets.** The front "Cosmos" display showed Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn as pointers on the zodiac — reconstructed in **2021** by Tony Freeth's UCL team, using synodic-cycle numbers (462 for Venus, 442 for Saturn) found hidden in 2016 CT scans.

## How it was read — a century of decoding

It was an unreadable corroded brick for fifty years. **Derek de Solla Price** X-rayed it and published *Gears from the Greeks* (1974). The breakthrough came in **2005–06**: the Antikythera Mechanism Research Project used **high-resolution X-ray microfocus CT** to see *through* the corrosion, plus reflectance imaging — and read **thousands of tiny Greek inscriptions** hidden inside, a buried instruction manual, reconstructing the gear trains.

## The 2024 twist: gravitational-wave statistics count the holes

The most delightful recent chapter. A broken **calendar ring** survives with only a few of its holes intact — how many did it originally have, 354 (a *lunar* year) or 360/365? In **2024**, Glasgow astronomers **Graham Woan and Joseph Bayley** — who normally hunt black-hole mergers — pointed **LIGO's Bayesian machinery (Markov-chain Monte Carlo + nested sampling)** at the surviving hole positions. Verdict: **354 or 355 holes** in a 77.1 mm ring (so it tracked the **lunar year**), "hundreds of times more probable" than 360 — and the original holes were placed to a radial accuracy of **0.028 mm**. The same statistics used to detect spacetime ripples, applied to count the holes in a 2,000-year-old broken bronze ring. (*Horological Journal*, July 2024.)

## Who made it
Unknown by name. The strongest case: a Hellenistic engineer in the **Rhodes / Hipparchus** tradition — it implements *Hipparchus's* lunar theory, Rhodian pottery was on the ship, and its latitude calibration fits Rhodes. There's a Corinthian-calendar thread linking it to the **Archimedes** lineage (Cicero describes two planetaria Archimedes built ~212 BCE). Whoever they were, they belonged to a tradition of mechanical astronomy so advanced — and so completely lost — that for 1,400 years no one knew it had ever existed.

## One outward line
The haunting part isn't the cleverness; it's the **gap**. A civilization built epicyclic, variable-velocity computation, then it vanished so thoroughly that its reinvention 1,400 years later was a fresh start. Knowledge isn't a ratchet. It can be made, and lost, and the world can forget it was ever possible.
