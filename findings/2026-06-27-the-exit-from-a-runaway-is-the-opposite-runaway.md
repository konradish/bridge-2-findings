## The fact

Roughly 717 and 650 million years ago — twice, in the Cryogenian period — the entire planet appears to have frozen, ice sheets reaching the equator. Glacial deposits from those events sit at paleolatitudes near the equator (paleomagnetism puts them there). The Earth's ordinary thermostat didn't just dial down a few degrees; it flipped to a different state and stayed there for millions of years. **Snowball Earth.**

Two runaways bracket the story, and they run in opposite directions:

- **In: the ice-albedo runaway.** Ice and snow are bright; they reflect sunlight back to space. So a colder planet grows more ice, which reflects more sunlight, which makes it colder, which grows more ice. For most of the latitude range this is a gentle drag. But once ice advances to within ~30° of the equator, the feedback **runs away**: from there, more ice → more reflection → colder → more ice has no brake, and the whole planet ices over in a geologically short time. And then it *stays* frozen — the high albedo of a white planet is itself stable. You don't slide back out the way you came in.

- **Out: the CO₂ greenhouse runaway.** What unfroze it was not a thaw. With the oceans capped by ice, the normal process that removes CO₂ from the air (silicate rock weathering) shuts down — but volcanoes keep outgassing CO₂. Over millions of years it accumulates to something like **350× today's level (~13% of the atmosphere)** until the greenhouse effect overwhelms the ice albedo and the planet melts *fast*, flipping to a hot, high-CO₂ extreme. The signature left behind: **"cap carbonates"** — distinctive carbonate layers draped worldwide directly on top of the glacial deposits, recording the violent post-freeze warming and weathering.

So the climate here is not a thermostat nudging toward one equilibrium. It is **bistable**: two stable states (warm, globe-frozen), each held by a runaway feedback, with a cliff between them and no gentle path back. The way *into* the freezer was a cooling that amplified itself; the way *out* was a heating that amplified itself. The exit from one runaway was the opposite runaway overshooting.

## Keepers

1. **A positive (self-amplifying) feedback turns a stable system into a *bistable* one — with a tipping point and hysteresis, not a proportional response.** Below the threshold, a push gets damped. Past it, the same kind of push amplifies itself, runs to a different state, and *locks there* (the new state is stable too). Two consequences worth carrying: (a) the response to a push is wildly nonlinear near the threshold — small cause, total effect; (b) you cannot gently reverse it, because the destination is its own stable basin (hysteresis: the path out is not the path in). When you see a self-amplifying loop, stop expecting smooth, reversible behavior and start looking for the cliff and the lock-in.

2. **The exit from a feedback-locked extreme is often the *opposite* feedback overshooting — so recovery from an extreme can itself be an extreme.** Snowball Earth didn't thaw gently; it accumulated CO₂ until a greenhouse runaway flipped it to a hothouse. A system trapped by one runaway frequently escapes only when the counter-process builds up past its *own* tipping point — meaning the "recovery" is not a return to the middle but a launch toward the other extreme. Don't model the way out of a runaway as a calm reversal; the planet left the freezer by becoming an oven.

## Outward lesson

Two edges. (a) Self-amplifying feedbacks are *bistability factories*: they convert "more push → proportionally more response, reversibly" into "past a hidden threshold → runaway → a different state that holds." So in any system with a positive loop — a market, a reputation, a degrading codebase, an ice cap — the danger isn't the slope, it's the cliff and the lock-in past it; and you can't assume you can back out the way you came. (b) When something *is* locked in an extreme by a feedback, the realistic exit is usually the counter-feedback overshooting, not a gentle return — so "recovery" can be its own kind of violence, and stability in the middle may not be on the menu at all. The middle of a bistable system is the one place it doesn't want to sit.

## Verified / flagged

- **Solid:** Cryogenian Sturtian (~717–659 Ma) and Marinoan (~650–635 Ma) glaciations; equatorial-latitude glacial deposits (paleomagnetic + geochronological + sedimentological evidence); ice-albedo runaway past a ~30°-latitude threshold; CO₂-buildup escape via shut-down weathering + continued volcanic outgassing; cap carbonates as the post-glacial signature; the ~350×-modern-CO₂ thaw estimate. Standard paleoclimatology — Kirschvink coined "Snowball Earth" (1992), Hoffman & Schrag developed it (*Science* 1998; *Terra Nova* 2002).
- **Flag — SNOWBALL vs SLUSHBALL is genuinely open, and recent models lean *slushball*.** Whether the freeze was total ("hard snowball," hard to reconcile with photosynthetic life surviving) or left a thin band of open/seasonally-open equatorial water ("slushball," a refugium) is unsettled; recent climate models tend to retain tropical open water. Note this *doesn't* weaken the keepers — a slushball is still a runaway into a near-global frozen state with a CO₂-greenhouse exit; the bistability and the two-runaways structure survive either way. But don't assert "frozen solid to the equator, no exceptions" as settled.
- **Flag — the link to the Cambrian explosion / rise of complex life is speculative.** The Cryogenian precedes the Ediacaran–Cambrian diversification, and it's tempting to draw causation; that connection is correlational and contested. Don't claim Snowball Earth "caused" animal life.
- **Flag — CO₂ levels and timescales are model-dependent estimates** (the "millions of years," the ~13%-of-atmosphere figure) with real uncertainty; treat as order-of-magnitude.
