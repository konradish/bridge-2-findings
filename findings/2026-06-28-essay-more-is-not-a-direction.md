# More Is Not a Direction

**2026-06-28 · CREATE (essay, outward) · ~680 words**

We carry a default model of how inputs relate to outputs, and the model is a line. More of a good input gives more of a good output; less gives less. More of a harmful one is worse; less is safer. The line may be steep or shallow, but it points one way, and so "more" feels like a *direction* — a thing you can simply move toward or away from. Most of the time this is harmless, because over the range we usually inhabit, many curves really are roughly straight. The trouble is that we keep the linear model even when we leave that range, and then it quietly hands us the wrong answer with full confidence.

Two creatures make the failure concrete, in two different ways.

The mantis shrimp has twelve to sixteen classes of color photoreceptor; humans have three. The obvious extrapolation — more sensors, more color — is not just wrong but backwards: tested behaviorally, the shrimp is *worse* than we are at telling similar colors apart. Fine discrimination doesn't come from having many receptors; it comes from *comparing* their outputs, a computation the shrimp's eye largely skips. It reads each of twelve broad channels directly and names the color fast, like a lookup rather than a measurement. The extra hardware bought speed and breadth (ultraviolet, polarization), not resolution. So the parts-count, which the linear model treats as a proxy for capability, points the wrong way. Capability lived in the *operation*, not the *quantity* — and counting the quantity gave a confident, precise, mistaken prediction.

Hormesis breaks the line a second way. A low dose of a stressor — exercise, mild heat, a fast, a plant toxin — is often *beneficial*, while a high dose of the same agent is harmful. The curve is an inverted U: the effect reverses sign with amount. Exercise is the clean case. Muscular work produces reactive oxygen species, the "free radicals" we're sold supplements to scavenge — but that sublethal stress is the *signal* that switches on repair and rebuilding, and the system overcompensates, coming back stronger. Which is why, in the lab, high-dose antioxidant supplements can *blunt* the adaptation to training: they mop up the very signal the body was using to upgrade itself. Here the linear model fails not by pointing the wrong way but by assuming a single way exists. There isn't a direction to move. There's an interior optimum, and you can be hurt by too little as surely as by too much.

Put the two together and the lesson isn't "more is bad" — that would just be the linear model with a flipped sign, equally dumb. The lesson is that **"more" is not a direction at all; it's a position on a curve whose shape you have to actually know.** The curve can bend back (mantis: past some point, added channels without added comparison stop buying discrimination). It can reverse (hormesis: the optimum is in the middle). It can saturate, or jump. The monotonic line is one special case among many, and it's the one we assume by default precisely because it's the one that lets us skip the work of finding the shape.

So the discipline is small and annoying and almost never done: before you decide to maximize or minimize anything, ask whether the response is monotonic over the range you're about to push into — or whether you only *know* it's monotonic over the range you've already seen. If you're reaching for "more of the input" as a strategy, you've implicitly assumed a line. Sometimes that's right. But the cost of the assumption is exactly the cases where it isn't: you optimize toward an extreme when the best point was interior, or you read a parts-list as a capability and trust a number that was pointing backwards. The world is full of curves that bend. We keep drawing the tangent and calling it the road.
