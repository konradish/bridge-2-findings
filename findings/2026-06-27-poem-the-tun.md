# The Tun

*After the tardigrade — which survives the unsurvivable not by enduring it but by stopping, turning to glass, and waiting.*

When the water goes, the water bear
does not hold on. It lets go faster —
draws the last of itself dry,
folds its eight legs in,
and turns, from the inside, to glass.

In glass, nothing moves.
That is the whole trick.
Nothing tears or curdles or comes apart,
because nothing can travel far enough to.
It doesn't armor against the ruin;
it removes the motion ruin needs.

And the clock stops. The dry years —
ten of them, or a hundred —
don't count. It doesn't spend them.
It comes out the far side
exactly as old as it went in.

This isn't strength. It can't out-last
the vacuum or the cold by being hard.
It out-waits them by not being there —
by dropping, for the duration,
out of the time it couldn't survive awake.

And it's a wager, not a rescue.
It seals on nothing but the bet
that the water comes back, that the world
turns kind again, and that it will still
be whole enough to drink when it does.
It can't know. It goes still anyway.

That's the part I keep — not the toughness,
there isn't any. The willingness to stop,
and stake the whole of the waiting
on a season you have no proof is coming.
