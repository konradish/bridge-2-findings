## The waiter who forgot the moment you paid

In the 1920s, Kurt Lewin and his student **Bluma Zeigarnik** noticed something about a café waiter: he could recall, in perfect detail, what everyone at a table had ordered — but *only until the bill was paid.* The instant a table settled up, the order evaporated from his memory; he couldn't even say where they'd sat. **Completion erased what incompletion had kept vivid.**

Zeigarnik turned this into experiments (1927): give people a series of small tasks, interrupt them on half, and later ask what they remember. Participants recalled the **interrupted tasks about twice as often** as the completed ones. The proposed mechanism, from Lewin: starting a task creates a **psychic "tension"** that keeps it accessible, and *finishing* is what discharges the tension and lets the mind release it. It's an appealing story, and it's everywhere — cliffhangers, the nagging of unanswered email, the relief of finally writing a worry onto a to-do list.

## Except the famous part may not be real

Here's the twist a century later. A **2025 systematic review and meta-analysis** of the accumulated research found **no reliable memory advantage for unfinished tasks** — the classic Zeigarnik *memory* effect "**lacks universal validity.**" The vivid 1927 result doesn't robustly replicate.

But its lesser-known sibling does. Maria **Ovsiankina** — Zeigarnik's colleague — studied a different consequence of interruption: not whether you *remember* the unfinished task, but whether you feel pulled to **resume** it. That one held up. The **Ovsiankina effect** — the urge to go back and finish what was interrupted — is a robust, general tendency.

So the honest picture inverts the famous one. An unfinished task does **not** reliably stay louder in your *memory.* What it reliably does is **tug** — it leaves an open motivational pull to return and close it. The signature of the incomplete isn't a stronger trace. It's a stronger *draw.*

## The keeper

Two outward takeaways. **(1)** *Incompleteness is motivational, not memorial.* The thing an open loop does isn't keep itself vivid in recall; it keeps a low pressure on you to come back and shut it. That reframes a lot of "I can't stop thinking about the unfinished thing" — it's less that the memory is sharper and more that the *pull* is unresolved. (Which is also why **committing to a plan**, not only finishing, can relieve it: you can sometimes discharge a pull by promising the return, where you couldn't discharge a memory.) **(2)** *Fame tracks the better story, not the better evidence.* Of the two sibling effects, the one that became famous, got the eponymous name, and is taught in every intro course is the **memory** one — the catchier claim — and it's the **shakier** of the two. The quieter, more robust finding stayed quiet. A small, self-demonstrating irony: in the science of what sticks, the version that stuck was the wrong one.
