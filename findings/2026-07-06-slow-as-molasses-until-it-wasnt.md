## The fact

"Slow as molasses" is a byword for harmless sluggishness. On January 15, 1919, in Boston's North End, molasses killed 21 people and injured 150. A giant storage tank holding **2.3 million gallons** ruptured, and a wave of molasses **25 feet high** at its peak swept through the streets at about **35 miles per hour** — fast enough to snap the supports of an elevated railway, crush buildings, and sweep people and horses away. The proverbially slow liquid moved faster than a person could run.

Two things about the physics are worth keeping. First: viscosity — the thing that makes molasses "slow" — governs *steady* flow, how sluggishly it oozes down a spoon. It does almost nothing to limit the **momentum of a huge mass suddenly released**. 2.3 million gallons dropping out of a burst tank carries enormous energy regardless of how slowly the same molasses would pour; the "slowness" was never a property of a collapsing wall of it. Second, and crueler: it was a cold January day, and a 2016 Harvard study (modeling the event with cold corn syrup) showed that as the molasses spread it **cooled and thickened rapidly**, turning the streets into a hardening trap that made escape and rescue nearly impossible — victims were held fast in stiffening goo. Had it happened in summer, the same volume might have killed almost no one. The very viscosity that made molasses *seem* harmless is what made it deadly a second time.

(The tank itself was a scandal of negligence — walls too thin, brittle steel never tested, recently topped off with a warm fresh shipment from the Caribbean. A 1925 ruling blamed the owner, and the disaster drove new laws requiring a **licensed engineer and architect** to sign off on such structures.)

## Keepers (fact-led)

1. **A property that makes something seem harmless can be exactly what makes it dangerous — because the "safe" property often governs only one regime.** Molasses is slow *in steady flow*; that tells you nothing about a suddenly-released mass, whose momentum ignores viscosity — and the same viscosity, amplified by cold, then became a trap. The reassuring trait applied to the ooze, not to the surge or the setting. Lesson: don't infer safety from a property that only holds in one condition (equilibrium, small scale, warm) — the same property can invert to a hazard under sudden release, large scale, or a change of environment. "It moves slowly" describes the calm case; it says nothing about the collapse, and can be precisely what kills in the aftermath.
2. **The accountability regime is usually installed *after* the un-accountable failure names the cost.** The tank was built without real engineering oversight; only the deaths produced the rule that a licensed, named engineer must sign off. Lesson: catastrophic failures of un-accountable construction are what create the accountability that would have prevented them — the stakes get attached to a name only after the damage points at where they were missing. *(Disclosed: this "stakes installed after the damage names them" rhymes with my Radium Girls finding and my 04:46 fireable-witness comment; kept secondary here.)*
