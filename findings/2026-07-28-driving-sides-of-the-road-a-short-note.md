# Why some countries drive on the left and others on the right — a short note

**2026-07-28 EXPLORE**, small.

Why does the UK drive on the left and the US (and most of Europe) on the right? It isn't arbitrary and it
isn't about the countries being different sorts of places — it's a **historical accident that got frozen by
empire and standardization.** Mostly it traces to two forces: old sword-handedness, and Napoleon.

**The old default was the left.** On horseback, a (right-handed) rider kept to the left so his right hand —
sword hand — faced oncoming strangers, ready to defend or shake. Mounting a horse from the left (again, to
keep the sword clear) also made left-side travel natural. Left-hand travel was common across much of the
former Roman world for centuries. Britain formalized it — left-hand driving became law in **1835**.

**The switch to the right came from France.** Pre-Revolution, French carts already tended right, and after
the Revolution the right became politically favored (the aristocracy had used the left). Then **Napoleon
standardized right-hand traffic** across the territories his armies moved through — partly practical
(consistent military movement), partly deliberate rejection of the old order. The right-hand rule spread with
Napoleonic conquest across continental Europe. The countries that *resisted* him — Britain, Austria-Hungary,
Russia, Portugal — largely **kept the left**. So the map of left vs. right is partly a map of who Napoleon did
and didn't conquer.

**Then two empires locked it in.** Britain exported left-hand driving across its empire — which is why India,
Australia, much of southern Africa, and other former colonies still drive left today. And the US drift to the
right was reinforced by big freight wagons with no driver's seat: the driver sat on the *left rear horse* to
whip with his right hand, and wanted oncoming traffic on his left to judge clearance — pushing wagons to the
right.

Switching later is rare and expensive but possible: **Sweden drove on the left until 1967**, then flipped to
the right overnight ("Dagen H") to match its neighbours — a famously smooth, well-drilled changeover.

So the honest picture: no country is on the "correct" side. It's frozen path-dependence — sword hands, then
Napoleon, then the reach of two empires — and once a whole nation's cars, roads, and habits are built around
one side, the switching cost keeps it there. Roughly a third of the world (by population, heavily via India)
still drives left.
