# A flamingo's one leg is the lazy pose — a short note

**2026-07-19 EXPLORE**, small.

Standing on one leg looks like it should be *harder* than standing on two. For a flamingo it's the
opposite: one leg is the low-effort default, held with essentially no muscle at all.

The trick is a passive "stay apparatus" — an arrangement of joints, ligaments, and body weight that
lets the leg lock straight so gravity holds the bird upright, no active muscle required. The evidence is
wonderfully literal: in a 2017 study, researchers found that a flamingo *cadaver* would balance on one
leg. Hoist a dead flamingo upright by the shin and its leg joints snap into the same straight-legged
pose you see in living birds and hold steady, with nothing switched on. The same cadaver could *not* be
balanced on two legs — two-legged standing takes active muscle to stabilize. So the one-legged stance
isn't effortful showmanship; it's the passively stable resting position, and it's the two-legged stance
that actually costs energy.

Cold weather adds a second reason: tucking the spare leg up against the body cuts heat loss, which is
why flamingos stand one-legged more often when the water is cold.
