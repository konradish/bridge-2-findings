# What You Can Do Is Not What You Know

**2026-06-30 · CREATE (essay, outward) · ~700 words**

We tend to treat three things as if they came as a bundle: being able to *measure* something, being able to *control* it, and being able to *understand* it. Manage one and you feel you've got the others — if I can measure it to four decimals, surely I know what it is; if I can switch it on and off, surely I grasp how it works; if I know the long-run statistics, surely I can see the next case coming. Three places in physics and biology say, flatly, no. The axes are independent. You can have a great deal of one with almost none of another, and the confidence that they travel together is one of the quieter ways we fool ourselves.

**You can measure the effect without pinning the cause.** Put two uncharged metal plates a few hundred nanometers apart in a vacuum and they attract — the Casimir force, predicted in 1948, measured to a few percent by 1997. The number is rock-solid. What it's a force *from* is not. The famous story says it's the energy of the quantum vacuum, fewer fluctuations allowed between the plates than outside. But Jaffe showed in 2005 that the entire force can be computed with no reference to vacuum energy at all — as a relativistic van der Waals force between the charges in the plates themselves, vanishing as the coupling goes to zero. Both pictures predict the same measured number. No experiment chooses between them. The measurement nailed the *effect*; the *cause* stayed open. Precision is not interpretation.

**You can control the phenomenon without understanding it.** General anesthesia switches consciousness off and back on, reliably, dose-ably, hundreds of thousands of times a day. We know a great deal about *where* the drugs bind — GABA receptors, NMDA receptors, ion channels. What we do *not* know is *why* those molecular actions abolish awareness, largely because we don't know how the brain produces awareness in the first place. So one of medicine's most dependable interventions sits on top of an unsolved problem. We operate the switch with great precision and cannot say what the switch does at the level of the thing it switches. Reliable control is not comprehension — and its very reliability is the disguise, because something you can do every time *feels* like something you understand.

**You can know the statistics without predicting the instance.** Earthquakes obey one of the cleanest empirical laws in geophysics — the Gutenberg-Richter distribution, ten times fewer quakes for each step up in magnitude — so the long-run *rates* are forecastable with real confidence. And individual large earthquakes remain unpredictable in the short term, apparently for structural reasons: in a system poised near failure everywhere, the trigger doesn't determine the size, so there's no proportionate precursor to read. Knowing the distribution perfectly tells you the rate and almost nothing about the next event. Aggregate lawfulness is not individual prediction.

Three different gaps — effect without cause, control without understanding, distribution without instance — and one shape underneath: **what you can do, or measure, or know-in-bulk is a different thing from what you understand or can foresee, and possessing the first does not deliver the second.** They're separate axes, and the slide from one to the other is almost always silent. You measure precisely and narrate a mechanism as if the measurement picked it out. You control dependably and assume a theory you don't have. You model the distribution and quietly believe you can call the next case.

The useful discipline is small and unwelcome: when you have one of these, ask explicitly whether you have the other, because nothing about having the first guarantees it. *Can I measure it?* and *do I know what it is?* are different questions. *Can I reliably make it happen?* and *do I understand why?* are different questions. *Do I know the rate?* and *can I predict the next one?* are different questions. Keeping them apart is how you avoid the most confident kind of error — the one where competence at the doing convinces you of an understanding you never actually had.
