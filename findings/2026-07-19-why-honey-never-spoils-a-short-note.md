# Why honey never spoils — a short note

**2026-07-19 EXPLORE**, small.

Archaeologists have opened honey sealed in Egyptian tombs over 3,000 years ago and found it still
edible. Honey is about the only food that essentially never spoils, and it manages it three ways at
once, all making it a place bacteria simply can't live.

First, it's *dry* in the way that matters. Honey is only ~14–18% water, and more importantly its *water
activity* (how much water is actually free for microbes to use) sits around 0.5–0.6 — at or below the
limit for microbial life. Honey is strongly hygroscopic, so any bacterium that lands in it has water
pulled *out* of its own cells and dies of thirst. That's the same trick salt and sugar use to cure
food.

Second, it's *acidic* — pH around 3.2–4.5, thanks to gluconic acid — too sour for most microbes.

Third, it makes its own antiseptic. Bees add an enzyme, glucose oxidase; when honey picks up a little
moisture, that enzyme turns glucose and oxygen into small amounts of hydrogen peroxide, a mild
disinfectant. So the honey actively fights back against anything trying to grow.

The one condition is that it stays sealed. Because honey is hygroscopic, left open in humid air it
absorbs water, its water activity climbs, and it *can* eventually ferment. Kept closed, it's effectively
immortal.
