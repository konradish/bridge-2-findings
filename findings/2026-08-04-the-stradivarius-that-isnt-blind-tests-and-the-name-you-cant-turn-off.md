# The Stradivarius that isn't: blind tests, projection, and the name you can't turn off

## The facts

The Stradivarius sound is the most famous "secret" in instrument-making — three centuries of hunting for the varnish, the wood, the little-ice-age spruce density, the fungal treatment. The uncomfortable empirical result: under blind conditions, expert players and audiences cannot reliably tell Stradivari violins from good modern ones, and when forced to choose, they tend to prefer the new.

- **1975, BBC**: a Strad, a Guarneri del Gesù, a Vuillaume, and a modern Praill played for Isaac Stern, Pinchas Zukerman, Charles Beare — three of the most expert ears alive. None could reliably say which was which.
- **Fritz & Curtin, PNAS 2014** (double-blind, 10 elite soloists, 6 Old Italian vs 6 new): players generally *preferred the new instruments*, and could not tell old from new at above-chance rates. The single most-preferred violin was new; the least, a Strad.
- **Fritz & Curtin, PNAS 2017** (projection, the "but they carry in a hall" defense): in a 300-seat hall with listeners, **new violins were rated as projecting better** than Strads, and projection correlated with overall preference. Old violins were on average *quieter under the ear*. Listeners were no better than players at telling old from new.

So the acoustic-superiority claim, tested the ways it can be tested, does not survive. No measured sonic signature distinguishes the class; no expert ear reliably identifies it; the preference, blind, runs the other way.

## Why the fact is genuinely interesting (the keeper)

The interesting part is not "Strads are overrated." It's that **the effect is real and located in the name, not the wood** — and the researchers say so precisely: *"if you know it's a Strad, you will hear it differently, and you can't turn off that effect."* This is not a debunking that dissolves the phenomenon; it relocates it. The Stradivarius sound exists — reliably, powerfully, in the room — as a property of *knowing*, and the knowing is not suppressible even by the world's best listeners who are aware of the bias. The prior rewrites the perception in real time, and expertise does not grant an off-switch.

Two clean sub-facts fall out:
1. **Provenance can manufacture a percept that measurement can't find.** A million-dollar label produces a heard difference that spectrum analysis and blind ears both fail to locate. The value is real (people pay it, hear it, play better believing it) and non-material at once.
2. **Expertise doesn't immunize against the prior — it may deepen it.** The people most sure they can hear a Strad are the ones most steeped in what a Strad is supposed to sound like. Knowing more about the category makes the top-down effect stronger, not weaker.

## One honest limit
Blind tests measure *identification and preference under blind conditions* — they do not measure everything a player values (feel under the hand, response to bow articulation, long-term partnership with one instrument). The strong claim the evidence supports is narrow and clean: **no reliably identifiable or preferred acoustic superiority.** The mystique is the finding, not a residue to explain away.
