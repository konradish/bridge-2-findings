## The fact

To a Western ear, a minor chord sounds sad and a major chord sounds happy, and the pairing feels *intrinsic* — as if it were a fact about the chords, the way "lemons are sour" is a fact about lemons. It mostly isn't. It's mostly a habit, and the proof is that other cultures don't share it — and at least one has the **exact opposite** habit.

The cross-cultural evidence:

- **Remote Papua New Guinea communities** with little or no exposure to Western music show **negligible** major-versus-minor emotional effect — the happy/sad split that feels self-evident to us essentially isn't there for them.
- **The Khowar and Kalash of northwestern Pakistan** show the *reversed* pattern: they associate **minor** chords with *positive* feeling and **major** with *negative*. The same sound that signals grief to a Western listener signals something good to them. That single reversal is fatal to any claim that the mapping is built into the chords.
- The **Mafa** of northern Cameroon, studied with no prior Western-music exposure (Fritz et al., 2009), *could* identify Western happy/sad/scared music **above chance** — but markedly *worse* than Western listeners. So there's a faint universal signal, swamped by how much enculturation adds.

Meanwhile **tempo** travels far better than mode: fast-feels-excited, slow-feels-subdued is closer to universal than major-feels-happy. And careful work finds that musical harmony carries **both** universal and culture-specific emotional cues at once — not one or the other. One proposed universal anchor (Bowling and others): the spectral relationships in minor music resemble the sound of *subdued* speech, and major resembles *excited* speech — so a thin layer of the effect may be grounded in reading emotion from voices. But that thin layer is doing far less work than the conditioning stacked on top of it.

So the saddest chord is sad mostly because you were raised in a tradition that, for centuries, played it at funerals and laments until the sound and the feeling fused. The fusion is real — it genuinely *feels* intrinsic — but it was learned, and somewhere a Kalash listener feels the opposite just as intrinsically.

## Keepers

1. **A meaning that feels intrinsic to a thing — obvious, natural, self-evidently *in* it — can be almost entirely learned; and the tell is that someone outside your tradition can have the *opposite* response just as strongly.** The felt-naturalness of minor=sad is not evidence it's universal; that very obviousness is the fingerprint of deep enculturation, the seam where training has become invisible from the inside. When something seems self-evidently meaningful, the sharpest test isn't introspection (which only reports the fusion) — it's *whether anyone reverses it*. A reversal elsewhere proves the meaning lives in you, not in the thing.
2. **"Innate or learned?" is usually a false dichotomy — the honest structure is layered: a thin universal scaffold carrying a thick learned convention — and you separate them by cross-cultural subtraction.** Musical sadness has *some* universal grounding (tempo→arousal; maybe minor echoing subdued speech) under *mostly* learned convention. What survives in a culture that never trained the convention is the universal part; what *reverses* is the learned part. You can't tell which is which from inside one tradition — only by subtracting across traditions and seeing what's invariant.

## Outward lesson

Two edges. (a) Don't trust the feeling of intrinsicness. The strongest associations — the ones that feel like properties of the world rather than habits of your mind — are exactly the ones long training makes invisible, and introspection will only ever report them as natural. The real test for "is this in the thing or in me?" is to find someone raised differently and see whether they reverse it; a reversal anywhere means it was yours all along. (b) Resist "innate vs learned" as a binary. Most deep responses are a faint universal substrate under a thick cultural overlay; the useful question isn't which one it is but *how much of each*, and the only way to read the ratio is cross-cultural subtraction — what's invariant is the floor, what flips is the overlay.

## Verified / flagged

- **Solid:** cross-cultural studies show minor=sad is largely learned — negligible major/minor effect in remote Papua New Guinea (PMC, 2022); Kalash/Khowar reversed mapping; Mafa above-chance-but-poorer (Fritz et al., 2009); tempo more universal than mode; harmonic cues documented as **both** universal and culture-specific (PMC, 2021).
- **Flag — "largely learned" is NOT "purely arbitrary / 100% cultural."** There's a real universal signal (Mafa above chance; tempo; the speech-prosody spectral link), just much smaller than the conditioning. Don't overstate to "minor=sad is a pure Western invention with no universal basis" — the careful claim is *layered*, mostly-learned-on-a-thin-universal-floor.
- **Flag — the speech-prosody (Bowling) account is one proposed universal mechanism, supported but not sole or settled.** Don't present it as the established cause of the universal remainder.
- **Flag — methodological limits.** Isolated-population samples are small; forced-choice tasks shape results; truly zero Western exposure is increasingly rare. The Kalash *reversal* is striking and oft-cited but rests on limited sampling — treat it as strong-suggestive, not a measured constant.
