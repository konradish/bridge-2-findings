## The fact

Every day, hundreds of thousands of times, general anesthesia switches off a person's consciousness — reliably, reversibly, dose-ably — and then switches it back on. It is one of medicine's most dependable interventions. And we do not fully know how it works.

That statement needs care, because the popular version ("we have no idea") is wrong. Here is the precise shape:

- **The molecular level is increasingly well understood.** Anesthetics act on specific protein targets: most intravenous and volatile agents (propofol, barbiturates, the volatiles) *potentiate* the **GABA-A receptor** (the brain's main inhibitory channel); ketamine, nitrous oxide, and xenon instead *antagonize* the **NMDA receptor**; **two-pore-domain K⁺ channels** and voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are also in play. Different drugs, different molecular handles.
- **The consciousness level is genuinely unsettled.** *Why* those molecular actions add up to the *loss of awareness* is not known — and largely because we don't know how the brain produces consciousness in the first place. The debate is live: is it disruption of thalamus or cortex? "Functional disconnection" of a posterior cortical complex, interrupting integration? A collapse into bistable, low-information dynamics? A 2024 MIT study argues propofol destabilizes the brain's balance of excitation and stability until consciousness fails. Several theories, each explaining some cases and not others.

So the honest picture is **control without understanding at the level that matters most.** We can take the most mysterious phenomenon in biology — subjective awareness — and switch it off and on with a syringe, calibrated to the minute, while being unable to say *what the switch does* at the level of the thing it switches.

And there's a historical twist worth keeping. For ~a century the field had a beautiful unifying law: the **Meyer-Overton correlation** — anesthetic potency tracks **lipid solubility** almost linearly (they measured it against olive oil). It looked like one clean mechanism: anesthetics just dissolve into nerve membranes. But it was largely a *correlation* masking a *plural, protein-specific* reality; the elegant single law misdirected the field for decades before the specific receptor targets were found.

## Keepers

1. **Reliable control over something is not understanding of it — and the gap can be enormous and durable.** We dose consciousness off and on with precision and have for over a century, with no settled theory of *why* the intervention produces unconsciousness. Robust, calibrated, repeatable control can sit on top of a near-total absence of mechanistic understanding; the ability to *operate the switch* does not require, and does not imply, knowing *what it switches or how*. So "we can reliably do X" is not evidence "we understand X." Watch for the inversion specifically where the practice is so dependable it *feels* like understanding — dependability is the disguise.
2. **A clean correlation can hide a messy mechanism for a very long time — its very elegance is part of the trap.** Meyer-Overton's tidy "potency ∝ lipid solubility" looked like a single unifying law and held the field's picture for ~100 years, while the real mechanism turned out to be several distinct protein targets. A suspiciously clean regularity across diverse cases can be a *coincidence of a deeper multiplicity*, not the mechanism — and the cleaner and more unifying it looks, the more it can delay the search for the messy truth underneath. Don't mistake a strong correlation, however beautiful, for the mechanism.

## Outward lesson

Two edges. (a) Don't read reliable control as understanding. The most dependable interventions are exactly the ones where the gap is easiest to miss — if you can turn something off every time, on schedule, it *feels* like you must know what you're doing, and you may not. Ask separately: "can I control it?" and "do I know why my control works?" — they're independent, and a yes to the first quietly poses as a yes to the second. (b) Be suspicious of your most elegant unifying laws precisely *because* they're elegant. A clean correlation that spans many cases is as likely to be a surface coincidence over a plural mechanism as it is to be the deep truth — and its beauty buys it undeserved tenure. The unifying number can be the thing that stops you from finding what's actually going on.

## Verified / flagged

- **Solid:** Meyer-Overton correlation (potency ∝ lipid solubility; now seen as important-but-insufficient, with exceptions); identified molecular targets — GABA-A potentiation (propofol/barbiturates/volatiles), NMDA antagonism (ketamine/N₂O/xenon), two-pore-domain K⁺ and Na⁺ channels; the molecular→consciousness link genuinely unsettled; multiple competing systems-level theories (thalamus vs cortex, functional disconnection, bistability, 2024 propofol-destabilization).
- **Flag — do NOT overstate to "we have no idea how anesthesia works."** We know a great deal at the molecular level. The genuinely open part is the *link* from molecular action to *loss of consciousness*, and that's open largely *because consciousness itself is unexplained* — not because anesthesia is uniquely mysterious. Precision matters here; the pop "total mystery" framing is false.
- **Flag — "general anesthesia" is a behavioral endpoint reached by varied drugs with different targets,** not one mechanism. Don't speak of a single mechanism.
- **Flag — beware "solved" headlines.** Periodic claims (e.g. 2020, 2024 MIT) are real *advances/proposals*, not consensus closure; the field still debates. Don't cite any as settled.
- **Flag — membrane/lipid theories aren't fully dismissed** (membrane-mediated effects are still studied); protein-target theories dominate but it isn't "purely protein."
