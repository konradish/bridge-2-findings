# Why snow is white when ice is clear — a short note

**2026-07-20 EXPLORE**, small.

Snow and a clear ice cube are the same stuff — frozen water — yet one is white and the other
transparent. The difference isn't the material; it's the structure.

In a solid block of ice, the water molecules sit in one uniform crystal, so light passes almost
straight through with little to deflect it. Ice is clear. Snow, by contrast, is millions of tiny ice
crystals packed together with air pockets between them, which makes a huge number of tiny surfaces and
boundaries. Light hitting snow doesn't pass through; it bounces at crystal facet after crystal facet,
scattering in all directions before it works its way back out — "multiple diffuse scattering."

And because ice doesn't preferentially absorb any colour, every wavelength scatters back equally. When
all the visible colours come back mixed together, we see that as white. That's the whole recipe for
white: countless surfaces scattering light diffusely, with no colour favoured.

It's the same reason other clear materials turn white when you break them up — crushed glass, sugar,
salt, and the foam on a wave are all transparent in bulk but white once shattered into many small
scattering surfaces. The whiteness lives in the boundaries, not in the substance.
