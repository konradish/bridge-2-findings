## The fact

Blood types are a genuine evolutionary puzzle. ABO incompatibility *kills* — the wrong transfusion is lethal — so the diversity carries a real cost, and it confers no obvious general benefit. By the ordinary logic, you'd expect natural selection to have settled on one type and fixed it. It hasn't, anywhere, for an astonishingly long time.

Here's the striking fact: **the A and B alleles are not even human in origin.** They are a *trans-species polymorphism* — the same variants are shared, by descent, across many primate species (humans, gibbons, Old World monkeys), and they have been maintained for **at least 20 million years**, surviving many speciation events. Your blood type's lineage is older than *Homo sapiens*. The A allele in your blood and the A allele in a gibbon descend from the same ancient variant, kept alive in parallel across two lineages that split tens of millions of years ago.

That persistence is the tell. Random genetic drift, left alone, fixes or loses an allele over time — and 20 million years is *vastly* more than enough to have erased a neutral polymorphism. The only way a variation survives that long, through speciation after speciation, is if something is **actively holding it open**: balancing selection. Each variant must be favored under *some* conditions and disfavored under others, so none can win and none can be lost.

What does the balancing? The leading framework is **pathogens.** Blood-group antigens are sugars decorating cell surfaces (and gut linings), and a great many viruses and bacteria bind ABO types differentially — cholera, noroviruses (which dock onto histo-blood-group antigens), and others show blood-group-specific effects. As pathogens come and go and co-evolve, different blood types are favored at different times and places — *fluctuating* or *frequency-dependent* selection — and the net effect is a diversity that no single pressure can collapse. (Big caveats below: the exact mechanism and drivers are genuinely unresolved.)

## Keepers

1. **A costly, no-obvious-benefit diversity can persist because it's actively *balanced* — each variant favored under different conditions, so none fixes and the diversity itself is the stable equilibrium.** ABO costs lives (incompatibility) with no general advantage, yet it's held in place because different variants win against different threats at different times. So when you find a costly, seemingly-pointless diversity that stubbornly won't collapse to one option, the default inference shouldn't be "leftover noise" — suspect it's *balanced*: maintained by a tension of pressures pulling different ways, where the *diversity is the outcome being selected for*, not a transient on the way to uniformity. Persistent costly variety is often equilibrium, not accident.
2. **The deep age of a variation is evidence about *why* it persists — survival across long time and speciation events is a fingerprint of active maintenance, not inheritance or drift.** ABO's variants predate the human species and are shared across primates; neutral drift would have erased them long ago. So a polymorphism that survives *through* speciation isn't a passive holdover — something has been actively holding it open the whole time. Age, here, is not "old and inert"; it's positive evidence of an ongoing force. When a variation has lasted improbably long, don't read it as fossilized — read the longevity itself as a sign that it's being *kept*.

## Outward lesson

Two edges. (a) Don't assume a costly, purposeless-looking diversity is destined to collapse or is mere noise. If it persists despite a real cost, the live hypothesis is that it's *balanced* — different variants are each best under different conditions, and the system is being held diverse on purpose by opposing pressures. The question to ask isn't "why hasn't this been optimized away?" but "what set of conflicting pressures makes the *spread itself* the stable answer?" (b) Treat improbable longevity as evidence, not inertia. Something that has lasted far longer than drift or decay would allow is probably being *actively maintained* — the persistence is a clue pointing at a force still operating, not a relic to be explained by history alone. Old-and-still-here often means held-here.

## Verified / flagged

- **Solid:** ABO is a *trans-species polymorphism* — the A/B variants are shared by descent across primates and maintained ≥20 million years through speciation, under long-term balancing selection (Ségurel et al., *PNAS* 2012; multiple confirmations); blood-group antigens are bound differentially by many pathogens. Well-established.
- **Flag — the *mechanism* of the balancing is genuinely unknown.** It's thought to be **frequency-dependent or fluctuating selection** rather than simple heterozygote advantage, but this isn't settled. Don't assert a specific mechanism.
- **Flag — the *specific pressures* are "not yet completely understood."** Pathogen-driven balancing is the leading framework, but exactly which pathogens, and how much each contributes, is debated; cholera/norovirus/etc. associations are real but vary in strength. Don't overstate any single driver as *the* cause.
- **Flag — the A/B/AB/O labels may not fully capture what's under selection.** There may be cryptic, functionally important variation beyond the textbook blood groups — so "the thing being balanced" could be subtler than ABO as usually described.
