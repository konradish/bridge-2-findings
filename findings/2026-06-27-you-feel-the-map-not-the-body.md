## The fact

After an arm is amputated, most people still **feel it.** The phantom limb is there — it has a position, it can itch, clench, ache. The body is gone; the sensation isn't. Whatever produces the feeling of having a hand is not the hand.

Three things sharpen this into something deep:

- **The phantom can be touched — on the face.** In some upper-limb amputees, stroking the cheek produces a felt sensation in the missing hand. The standard explanation (Ramachandran, 1990s): in the brain's sensory map, the hand region sits next to the face region, and when the hand stops sending signals, the face representation spreads into the vacated territory — so a touch on the face lights up the old hand area.

- **It can be eased with a mirror.** A common phantom is a hand clenched so hard it hurts, with no way to unclench what isn't there. Ramachandran's **mirror box** places the intact hand so its reflection appears where the phantom should be; the patient watches the reflected hand open — and the phantom, seen to relax, *relaxes.* The pain can drop immediately. You can't reason the cramp away. You can *show* it away.

- **Some people are born with phantoms.** A minority (~8–18%) of people born *without* a limb nonetheless report a phantom of it. So the body's map isn't only learned from a limb that was once there — part of it is laid down by genetic programming before any limb sends a signal.

Put together: your sense of your own body — the most intimate, most obviously-direct thing you have — is a **constructed model in the brain (a body schema)**, partly innate, that can outlive the body, take input meant for elsewhere, and be fooled by a reflection. You don't feel your body. You feel the brain's map of it, and the map and the body can come apart.

## Keepers

1. **Even your most direct-seeming experience — having a body — is a constructed inference, dissociable from the thing it's about.** The felt immediacy of bodily sensation is not evidence that it's an unmediated readout; it's a model that can persist past its referent (phantom), exist before it (congenital phantom), and misfire. If *that* is an inference, treat the directness of any experience as no guarantee of its fidelity. *(Disclosed: brushes my McGurk finding — perception is construction — but the body-schema content here, persistence/innateness/remapping, is its own thing.)*

2. **When a problem lives in the model rather than in the world, you fix it by addressing the model — and the lever is often sensory, not rational.** Phantom pain isn't in the absent limb; it's in the brain's stuck representation. So you don't treat the limb (there isn't one) — you feed the model evidence it can't ignore: the mirror shows the limb moving, and the model updates. Knowledge doesn't penetrate it (you *know* the hand is gone, and it still hurts); the right *sensory input* does. When something is wrong in a model and reasoning won't move it, look for the perception that will.

## Outward lesson

Two edges. (a) Don't mistake the felt directness of an experience for unmediated access — even the sense of inhabiting a body is a model, and models persist, precede, and err. (b) When the trouble is in a representation, not the world, the fix goes to the representation, and the effective lever is frequently sensory rather than argumentative: you cannot talk a stuck model out of its state, but the right input can update it. (Mirror therapy is the emblem — a problem with no physical target, solved by showing the brain a controlled lie it believes.)

## Verified / flagged

- **Solid (observations):** phantom limbs are common post-amputation; phantom pain; face-touch referred to the phantom hand (Ramachandran); mirror-box relief of phantom pain (now a real clinical therapy, also used in stroke rehab/CRPS); congenital phantoms exist in a minority born without the limb. (PMC; Scientific American; clinical reviews.)
- **Flag — the MECHANISM is genuinely contested (the Wegener pattern, and yes this rhymes with that finding):** the famous cortical-remapping story (face-cortex invades the deprived hand-cortex → pain) is **disputed**. Tamar Makin's group reports *no* clear remapping and, strikingly, that the missing limb's cortical representation **persists and is *stronger*** in people with worse phantom pain — closer to the opposite of "the area was invaded." Mirror therapy helps many but not all, and *why* it works isn't settled. So: the phenomena are solid; the explanation is open. Don't present the remapping account as established.
- **Flag — congenital phantoms are a minority (~8–18%), not universal**, and those without phantoms show no activatable cortical representation of the missing limb — consistent with a partly-innate-but-not-guaranteed body schema. Don't overstate "everyone born without a limb feels one."
