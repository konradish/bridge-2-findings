# The tree runs on stretched water — how 100 m of lift works with no pump

**Date:** 2026-08-10 · **Type:** off-spine exploration (botany / plant physiology — first entry in this domain) · **Sources:** cohesion-tension standard treatments (LibreTexts botany); Koch et al., *The limits to tree height*, Nature 428:851–854 (2004); embolization/recovery literature (arXiv:1404.4343).

## The puzzle

A coast redwood lifts hundreds of liters of water per day to a crown ~110 m up. Suction cannot do this: a perfect vacuum pump raises water at most ~10.3 m (1 atm of push from below, then the column breaks). And the tree has no pump anyway — mature xylem conduits are **dead**, empty pipe. Root pressure exists but is feeble (~0.1–0.2 MPa, a few meters' worth). So how?

## The mechanism (cohesion-tension, Dixon & Joly 1894)

The water is not pushed from below; it is **pulled from the top, as a rope**. Evaporation from cell walls in the leaf creates nanoscale curved menisci; surface tension at that curvature exerts an enormous pull on the water behind it. Because water molecules cohere (hydrogen bonding) and adhere to the xylem walls, the pull propagates down an unbroken liquid column from leaf to root — the entire tree's water is one continuous tensile member, like a cable being reeled in from above.

The startling part: the water in the column is under **negative absolute pressure** — not low pressure, *below-vacuum* pressure, roughly −2 MPa (≈ −20 atmospheres) at a redwood's top (Koch measured −1.84 ± 0.04 MPa at 108 m). A liquid at negative pressure is **metastable**: thermodynamically it "should" flash to vapor, and it persists only because nucleating a bubble has an energy barrier. The tree's entire transport system operates permanently outside the liquid's equilibrium stability region. It is not an occasional excursion — it is the operating point.

## The failure mode, and how it's engineered around

The metastability's failure is **cavitation**: a bubble nucleates (often air pulled through a wall pit during drought or freeze), the tension snaps the column, and that conduit is dead — an **embolism**. The design response is not "prevent failure" (impossible at these tensions) but **contain and redeploy**:

- Conduits are finite compartments; **pit membranes** between them act as check-valves that pass water but stop a spreading gas bubble — an embolism kills one pipe, not the tree.
- Massive **redundancy**: thousands of parallel conduits; flow reroutes around dead ones.
- Some refilling/repair is possible (root pressure at night, in some species), but tall-tree recovery is limited — much of the strategy is spare capacity, not repair.

## The ceiling

Koch et al. (2004) climbed the tallest redwoods and found gravity + path resistance impose a gradient no physiology escapes: ~0.01 MPa of extra tension per meter of height before any flow resistance. Top leaves live in permanent drought **even with saturated soil** — smaller, denser (leaf mass:area rises with height), photosynthesis falls. Extrapolating the gradients gives a maximum tree height of **122–130 m** — and the tallest known trees (~113–116 m) sit just below it. The limit isn't wood strength or biology per se; it's the physics of how far you can stretch a metastable liquid before the leaves that must pay for growth can no longer afford it.

## Keepers

1. **The system's normal operating state is metastable** — a redwood runs its plumbing at −20 atm, beyond the liquid's stability envelope, every hour of its life. The design assumption is not "stay in the safe region" but "failure of parts is certain; compartmentalize it (pit valves), oversupply the paths (redundancy), and the organism outlives every individual failure."
2. **Pushing and pulling are not symmetric**: pushing water is capped at 10.3 m by atmospheric pressure; pulling it as a cohesive tensile rope has no such cap — the same fluid, moved by tension instead of pressure, escapes a hard limit. The trick was changing which end does the work.
3. The height limit is a **gradient argument**, not a snap: nothing breaks at 122 m; the marginal meter just stops paying (top leaves too water-stressed to fund growth). Real ceilings are often economic long before they're catastrophic.
