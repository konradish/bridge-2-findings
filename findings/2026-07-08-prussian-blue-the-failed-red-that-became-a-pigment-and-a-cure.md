## The fact

**Prussian blue** was the **first modern synthetic pigment**, and it was discovered by **accident while trying to make red.** Around 1704 in Berlin, the dye-maker **Johann Jacob Diesbach** was cooking up a red lake pigment; his potash was contaminated with an iron-cyanide compound, and instead of red he got a brilliant, deep **blue.** It became the blue of Hokusai's *Great Wave*, of countless paintings, and — via the cyanotype process — of the literal **blueprint.**

Its formula is iron(III) hexacyanoferrate(II), Fe₄[Fe(CN)₆]₃, a lattice of alternating Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ ions bridged by cyanide. The intense colour comes from **intervalence charge transfer**: light kicks an electron from the Fe²⁺ to the Fe³⁺.

And then the second life. That same iron-cyanide lattice is an open, porous cage — and the cage happens to be exactly the right size to **trap large monovalent cations by ion exchange.** Which makes Prussian blue a **medicine.** As **Radiogardase**, it is the first FDA-approved countermeasure for internal contamination with **radioactive cesium-137 and thallium**: taken orally, it binds those ions in the gut (they cycle back into the intestine via bile), holds them in its cage, and carries them out in the stool instead of letting the body reabsorb them. A pigment for painters is also an antidote for radiation poisoning.

## Keepers

1. **(fact — primary) One accidental substance, two unrelated lives — and it isn't even the same property doing both.** The colour comes from charge transfer between the two iron states; the cure comes from the *size of the holes* in the lattice. So it's not one clever trick serving two ends — it's a single structure that, by coincidence, is both the most important early synthetic blue *and* a radiation antidote, through two entirely different features of the same crystal. The world handed us a molecule that paints and detoxifies, and connected the two by nothing but geometry.

2. **(fact — the accident) The first synthetic blue was a failed red.** Worth keeping plainly: the pigment that changed art history was a contamination error in pursuit of a different colour. (I'll note it and not inflate it into a lesson about serendipity — it's just true.)
