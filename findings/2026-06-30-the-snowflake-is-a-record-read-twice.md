## The fact

Two famous claims about snowflakes seem unrelated and even in tension: each is **six-fold symmetric** (the same shape repeated around six arms), and **no two are alike**. They turn out to be two readings of a single fact: *the snowflake is a record of its own fall.*

The six-fold symmetry comes from the bottom up — water's molecular geometry forces ice into a **hexagonal lattice**, and a growing crystal inherits that six-cornered shape; the six arms (dendrites) sprout from the hexagon's corners. But why do the six arms grow *alike* — branching at the same places, the same way? Because, as the flake falls and grows, all six arms occupy almost exactly the same point in space — micrometers apart — so they experience the *same* temperature and humidity at every instant. The micro-environment, and its moment-to-moment changes, is nearly identical around the whole crystal, so each arm grows nearly the same way. **The arms match because they shared one history.**

And why is each flake *unique*? Because the crystal's shape depends sensitively on the temperature and humidity it grows in — plates near 0°C, needles and columns around −5°C, big branched dendrites near −15°C (the Nakaya morphology diagram). As a flake tumbles through a cloud, it crosses a *particular sequence* of temperatures and humidities, and its form is laid down as a record of that path. **Flakes differ because each fell a different route.** The same growth process that makes the six arms identical (they shared the path) makes flakes different (the paths differed).

So the snowflake's regularity and its uniqueness are not opposites. One reading of the record tells you what the arms *shared* (symmetry); the other tells you what the flakes *didn't* (uniqueness). Ukichiro Nakaya, who first mapped the temperature-to-shape relationship, called snow crystals "letters sent from the sky" — the morphology spells out the conditions of the fall.

## Keepers

1. **A form can be strikingly *regular within itself* and *unique between individuals* at once — and from a single cause — when it is a *record* of a history that's shared internally but unique externally.** The snowflake's symmetry and its no-two-alike uniqueness aren't in tension; they're two readings of the same recording. The arms are alike because they shared one micro-history; the flakes differ because each had a different macro-path. So *internal regularity* and *external individuality* can be the same mechanism seen from two angles: the regularity marks what the parts had in common, the uniqueness marks what their histories didn't. When you meet something both highly patterned *and* one-of-a-kind, don't treat those as competing facts — look for the record underneath, and read it twice: once for the shared, once for the divergent.
2. **A frozen, static form can be a *readable trace* of an invisible dynamic process — the shape is the history, run backwards.** The snowflake's habit (plate vs needle vs dendrite) encodes the sequence of temperatures and humidities it fell through; the final form is a recording of conditions you never saw. So when a structure looks merely *given*, ask whether it's actually a *transcript* — whether its shape was written by a process, and can be read to recover that process. ## Outward lesson

Two edges. (a) When something is both very *regular* and very *individual*, stop seeing a paradox and start looking for the record that produces both. Regularity often marks a *shared* history among a thing's parts; uniqueness marks an *unshared* history between things. The snowflake's six matching arms and its one-of-a-kind shape come from the very same growth — what was common across the arms, what differed across the flakes. Read patterned-yet-unique objects as recordings, and you can recover *both* what was held in common and what diverged. (b) Don't take a static form as simply *given*. Many shapes are transcripts of a process — written as they formed, legible afterward — so a form that looks like a fixed property may be a frozen history you can read to recover conditions you weren't there to witness.

## Verified / flagged

- **Solid:** six-fold symmetry from the hexagonal ice lattice (water's molecular geometry); the six arms grow alike because the micro-environment is nearly identical around the whole crystal; morphology depends sensitively on temperature/humidity (Nakaya diagram: plates/stars near 0°C, needles/columns ~−5°C, branched dendrites ~−15°C); the shape records the fall-path. (Nakaya; Libbrecht's snow-crystal work.)
- **Flag — "no two snowflakes are alike" is a romantic overstatement as an absolute.** It holds for *complex* crystals (the number of possible arrangements is astronomically large), but *simple* crystals can be effectively identical — Nancy Knight (1988) photographed two near-matching ones. True-for-the-complex, not literally universal.
- **Flag — perfect six-fold symmetry is RARE, not typical.** "Snowflakes are never perfectly symmetrical," and **fewer than 0.1%** show the ideal symmetric form; most real snow crystals are irregular and asymmetric (the photogenic symmetric ones are a tiny, selected minority). The "shared history → symmetry" account explains the symmetric cases; many flakes' arms diverge when conditions aren't uniform. Don't imply all flakes are the textbook star.
- **Flag — *exactly how* the six arms stay so synchronized is still researched.** "They share the same environment" is the standard explanation, but Libbrecht notes it isn't fully understood why the arms end up as *alike* as they do (given they grow somewhat independently). An open question, not a closed one.
