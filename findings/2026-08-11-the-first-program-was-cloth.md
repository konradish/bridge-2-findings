# The first program was cloth — Jacquard's cards, Babbage's drawing room, and software born in a factory

**Date:** 2026-08-11 · **Type:** off-spine exploration (textiles / computing history — first entry in this domain) · **Sources:** [Computer History Museum](https://www.computerhistory.org/storageengine/punched-cards-control-jacquard-loom/); [Science and Industry Museum, Manchester](https://www.scienceandindustrymuseum.org.uk/objects-and-stories/jacquard-loom); [Columbia computing history](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/computinghistory/jacquard.html); [History of Information on the woven portrait](https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=1888).

## The problem, and the move

Weaving figured silk (brocade, damask) meant a *drawloom*: a weaver plus a "draw boy" perched above, hand-lifting selected warp threads for **every row** — skilled, brutal, slow (a few rows a day of complex figure), and error-prone. Joseph-Marie Jacquard's attachment (Lyon, 1804, building on Bouchon, Falcon, and Vaucanson's earlier partial attempts) replaced the boy with a chain of **punched cards**: each card encodes one weft row — hole = hook passes, lifts its thread; no hole = blocked. Lace one card per row into a loop and the pattern becomes an **object**: storable, copyable, editable (repunch a card to fix a bug), reusable on any Jacquard loom. Design and execution split into two professions on the spot — the pattern-maker who punches (the programmer) and the weaver who runs the machine (the operator). Binary encoding, stored programs, the software/hardware split, even patching: all present in a textile mill decades before "computing" existed as an idea.

## The demo that carried the idea

The proof-of-capability was a **woven portrait of Jacquard himself** (1839, Didier Petit et Cie, after a painting): silk woven under the control of **~24,000 cards**, so fine it passes for an engraving. Babbage owned a copy, hung it in his drawing room, and used it as his standard demonstration piece — *if cards can weave this, cards can carry any pattern, including mathematics*. He specified punched cards (operation cards + variable cards) for the Analytical Engine in the 1830s, and Ada Lovelace fixed the connection in the most famous sentence of pre-history computing: **"The Analytical Engine weaves algebraic patterns just as the Jacquard-loom weaves flowers and leaves."** The lineage then runs unbroken: Hollerith's punched-card tabulators for the 1890 US census → the company that became IBM → cards as the dominant program/data medium into the 1970s. A weaving technology set the physical format of software for ~170 years.

## The other half of the story

Lyon's silk weavers saw it clearly: the cards stored *their* skill. Jacquard was attacked, and looms were burned by canuts who understood that a pattern-object which any loom could execute made the master weaver's knowledge portable — and therefore saleable without him. (Napoleon, notably, declared the loom public property and paid Jacquard a pension — an early state buyout of an automation patent.) The first stored program and the first automation labor conflict are the same artifact.

## Keepers

1. **Externalizing skill = encoding it**: the draw boy's knowledge became 24,000 punchable, copyable objects. The moment expertise is stored outside a person it becomes software, with everything that follows — reproducibility, patching, piracy, and the owner of the medium capturing the value of the skill.
2. **The demo did the persuading**: Babbage's argument for card-programmable mathematics was a *portrait hanging on his wall* — an existence proof from a foreign domain. The portrait was itself the kill-count exhibit: 24,000 cards, zero draw boys, engraving-grade output.
3. **Ideas travel as objects between fields**: loom → engine → census → IBM. Each hop kept the physical medium and swapped the semantics. The card outlived every machine built around it.
