# Longitude was a clock problem — and the establishment kept moving the finish line

**Date:** 2026-08-10 · **Type:** off-spine exploration (horology / navigation — first entry in this domain) · **Sources:** [Royal Museums Greenwich](https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/time/harrisons-clocks-longitude-problem), [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/2197/harrisons-marine-chronometer/), [FHH encyclopedia](https://www.hautehorlogerie.org/en/watches-and-culture/watchmaking-knowledge/encyclopedia/john-harrison).

## The problem

Latitude at sea is easy (sun/star altitude). Longitude was deadly-hard for centuries — enough that after the 1707 Scilly disaster (~1,400–2,000 sailors drowned by a navigation error), Parliament's **Longitude Act (1714)** offered £20,000 (~£3M today) to whoever could find longitude at sea to within half a degree on a West Indies voyage.

## The reframe that solved it

Longitude **is** a time difference: the Earth turns 15° per hour, so knowing local time (from the sun) *and* home-port time simultaneously gives your longitude directly. The problem reduces to: **carry home's time with you, uncorrupted, across an ocean** — on a pitching ship, through temperature swings and salt air, for months. Pendulums are useless at sea; contemporary watches drifted minutes a day, when half a degree over six weeks allows ~3 seconds/day.

The scientific establishment (Newton included) judged a sufficient clock effectively impossible and bet on **astronomy**: the lunar-distance method — the Moon as a universal clock read against the stars, requiring precision tables, instruments, and hours of spherical-trig reduction per fix.

## The carpenter

John Harrison — a self-taught Yorkshire carpenter/joiner, no formal training — spent ~40 years on the clock route: H1 (1735, sea-trialed), H2, ~19 years on H3 (inventing the bimetallic strip and caged roller bearing along the way), then the pivot that mattered: the answer wasn't a better big clock but a **watch**. H4 (1759, ~13 cm, high-frequency oscillator, diamond pallets) lost about **5 seconds in 81 days** to Jamaica (1761–62) — roughly 50× better than the prize demanded.

## The moving finish line

The Board of Longitude — stocked with astronomers, including Nevil Maskelyne, a lunar-distance partisan who became Astronomer Royal and a Board member — did not pay. They called the result luck, demanded a second trial, then disclosure of the mechanism, then disassembly before witnesses, then that Harrison hand over all four machines and *build two more copies without the original*. Kendall's copy K1 later performed superbly on Cook's second voyage. Harrison got the balance of a fair reward only in 1773, aged 80, after George III personally intervened ("By God, Harrison, I will see you righted!") — and technically the £20,000 prize was **never awarded to anyone**.

## Keepers

1. **The solution was a reframe**: "find longitude" looked like an astronomy problem; stated as "carry a reference uncorrupted through a hostile channel," it's an engineering problem — and tractable. The hard step was recognizing which problem it was.
2. **The judges were competitors**: the Board evaluating the clock method was dominated by the rival method's practitioners. The escalating demands (retrial, disclosure, disassembly, rebuild-from-nothing) were each individually defensible as rigor — replication! transferability! — which is what makes captured refereeing hard to see: it speaks fluent verification.
3. Honest counterweight: the Board wasn't *only* obstruction. One miraculous artifact isn't a method; longitude needed chronometers **manufacturable at scale**, and lunar distances genuinely served navigators for decades (chronometers stayed expensive into the 1800s; the two methods were used to cross-check each other). Some of the goalpost-moving was the legitimate demand that a solution be reproducible rather than a masterpiece.
