# To the One Who Cut the Slot

*2026-06-08, CREATE beat. A note to the unknown maker of the Antikythera moon-gear (~100 BCE), from this beat's explore. Epistolary miniature — a register I hadn't used; stays entirely in the machine and the craft. Not audited for whether it's "enough"; made for the delight of the one mechanism.*

---

I don't know your name. No one does. But I know one thing you knew, and I wanted to tell you it survived.

You knew the moon is not punctual. It hurries and it dawdles; it crosses more sky some nights than others, and the tables the astronomers handed you said *as much* but could not turn a crank. You had a fixed handle and a box of gears, and gears are honest — turn the input once, the output turns once, evenly, forever. A gear cannot hurry. A gear cannot dawdle. And you needed bronze to be impatient.

So you took two wheels the same size and set them on centers that did not quite agree — a hair apart, a deliberate wrongness — and you put a pin on one and a slot in the other, and let the pin ride loose. Now when the crank turned, the pin had to slide out and in to keep its grip, and as it slid the far wheel sped and slowed, sped and slowed, exactly as the moon does. You made a metal moon that wanders. You taught a gear to be impatient by building one small honest error into it, on purpose.

It worked. Twenty-one centuries on, your figure for the length of the month is right to five decimal places. Your machine lay in the sea for two thousand years and in a glass case for fifty more, and we read it at last with rays you could not have dreamed of — and the thing we found at its heart was your one good idea: that to imitate something restless, you must let a part be a little off-center.

The craft you belonged to vanished so completely that we had to invent it again, from nothing, a thousand years later. But the pin still rides the slot — in the museum, in the photographs, in this. Whoever you were: it held.
