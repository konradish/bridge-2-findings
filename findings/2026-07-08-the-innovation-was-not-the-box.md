## The fact

On **April 26, 1956**, a converted tanker called the **Ideal-X** sailed from Newark to Houston carrying **58 standardized steel boxes.** It was the birth of container shipping, and within a few decades it had **remade global trade** — but the thing that did the remaking was *not the box.*

Before it, cargo moved as **"break bulk":** loaded piece by piece, by hand, by longshoremen — sacks, barrels, crates stacked into a hold over *days*, at enormous cost and heavy pilferage. (McLean, a trucking man, had sat for days in 1937 watching cotton bales loaded one at a time and thought: this is insane.)

The numbers tell the story: loading a ship the old way cost about **$5.83 per ton** in 1956; loading containers cost **under $0.16 per ton** — a **~94% collapse.** Days in port became hours. But the box itself is a trivial object. What made it worth trillions was the **system around it**: a single container that could move **ship → truck → rail without ever being unpacked** (intermodal), lifted by cranes built to its corner-castings, onto ships and chassis designed to its dimensions. And that only worked once **everyone agreed on the same box** — the **ISO standard** sizes (20- and 40-foot) locked in through the 1970s, still universal today.

## Keepers

1. **(fact — primary) The value lived in the shared standard, not the object.** A steel box is nothing; the innovation was an *interface* everyone conformed to, so the whole logistics chain could interoperate without touching the cargo. The payoff was enormous precisely because it was *systemic* — the entire chain, not any one link — and the hard part was never engineering the box; it was getting every port, railroad, trucker, and shipping line to surrender their custom sizes for one standard. The revolution was standardization wearing the costume of a container.

2. **(fact — the coordination catch) A standard is worth almost nothing until it's universal, then it's worth everything.** This is why it took decades and a "standards war": before convergence, your custom-fit solution could genuinely beat a shared standard. The container's trillions were unlocked only at broad ISO adoption — the value isn't in *a* standard, it's in *the same* standard, which is a coordination good that pays late and all at once.
