# The Accident of Permanence

*Essay · 2026-07-10 · CREATE (~540w — develops the Ashurbanipal finding's keeper outward into a claim about media and time, with a contemporary turn. Outward/archival, deliberately off the day's verification theme. Publish decision Konrad's; not rostered; blog candidate.)*

---

We think of permanence as something you add to a record — better materials, a backup, a vault. The Library of Ashurbanipal says otherwise. Thirty thousand clay tablets survived twenty-six centuries, including the oldest great poem we have, and they survived for one reason: in 612 BCE an army burned Nineveh to the ground. Sun-dried clay slowly dissolves back into the mud it came from. Fired clay — clay taken past six hundred degrees, which is exactly what a burning palace does — becomes a brick, and a brick keeps. The fire meant to erase the Assyrians is the only reason we can still read them. The catastrophe was the kiln.

It's easy to take that as irony and leave it there. But it's a fact about media, and it generalizes. Whether an event destroys a record or preserves it is not a property of the event. It is a property of the fit between the medium and the event. Fire is fatal to paper and a firing to clay — the same disaster, the same intensity, landing as annihilation on one material and permanence on another. Pompeii's ash smothered a city and, by smothering it, kept it. A peat bog drowns a body and, by drowning it, tans and holds it for two thousand years. Cold, drought, burial, fire: the great preservers are all catastrophes that happened to meet the right material.

Which means permanence was never really *added* to these records. It was an accident of the match between what they were made of and the particular violence that found them. No one at Nineveh chose a fireproof archive. They happened to write on the one substance a fire improves.

Now set our own media beside the clay. We have built the most copyable, most accessible records in history — and among the least durable. A hard drive needs electricity, a working interface, an intact filesystem, an unbroken chain of format migrations, and an institution that keeps paying the bill — and when it fails, it fails silently, a folder of unreadable bytes with no scorch mark to warn you. Formats rot. Links die. The machine that could read last decade's disk is itself in a landfill. A cuneiform tablet, by contrast, asks only for light and someone who knows the signs. We optimized hard for one axis — the speed of copying, the ease of access — and, without noticing, optimized away the accidental robustness that let the ancient world reach us at all. A burned palace has already outlasted, and will keep outlasting, most of what we call the cloud.

So the real question about anything you want to keep is not how carefully you store it. It is which catastrophe is coming, and whether your medium will take that catastrophe as a wound or as a kiln. Permanence isn't a feature you install. It's a bet about the fit between what you made the thing out of and the specific disaster history has in store for it — and the tablets suggest the winning bet is very often the one nobody placed on purpose.
