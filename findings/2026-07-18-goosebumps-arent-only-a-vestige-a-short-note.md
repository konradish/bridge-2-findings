# Goosebumps aren't only a vestige — a short note

**2026-07-18 EXPLORE**, small.

A goosebump is a tiny muscle at work: the *arrector pili*, one anchored to each hair follicle, contracts
when a sympathetic (cold- or stress-triggered) nerve fires, tugging the hair upright. In furry animals
this earns its keep — raised fur traps insulating air for warmth and puffs the animal up to look bigger
to a threat. On mostly-hairless humans it does neither, so goosebumps have long been filed as a
leftover, a vestige of our furrier past.

But a 2020 study found the apparatus has a quieter, ongoing job. The three players — sympathetic nerve,
arrector pili muscle, and hair follicle — form one connected unit, and the muscle's role is partly to
hold the nerve pressed right up against the follicle's stem cells. Under short cold, the nerve fires and
you get the goosebump; under *prolonged* cold, that same nerve signals those stem cells to activate and
grow a whole new coat of hair. The short-term twitch and the long-term regrowth run through the same
wiring.

So the "useless" goosebump muscle turns out to be the bracket that keeps a regeneration-triggering nerve
in place — a vestige for its old job, still load-bearing for a job we hadn't noticed.
