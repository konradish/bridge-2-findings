## The fact

Two strangers, one sum of money — say $10. One of them, the **proposer**, offers a split: "I keep $8, you get $2." The other, the **responder**, has exactly one move: accept (they get the split as offered) or reject — in which case *both walk away with nothing.* One round, anonymous, no second chances. This is the **ultimatum game** (Güth, Schmittberger & Schwarze, 1982), and it quietly demolished a pillar of economics.

The textbook rational prediction is airtight: a self-interested responder should accept *any* positive offer, because $2 beats $0; a self-interested proposer, knowing this, should offer the smallest possible crumb. So the theory says: offer $1, take $9, deal done.

That is not what people do. Responders **routinely reject** offers they find unfair — lowball offers (below ~20–30% of the pot) get rejected a large fraction of the time, with people choosing *nothing* over an insulting split. And proposers, anticipating exactly this, offer far more than the crumb — around 40% on average, close to a fair half. In other words, people will *burn real money* to punish someone for being greedy, gaining nothing themselves but the denial; and the mere fact that they will do so pulls the whole system toward fairness. The "irrational" rejection isn't a mistake — it's the enforcement mechanism, and it works precisely because it's credible.

## Keepers (fact-led)

1. **A stable, reproducible deviation from a rational-self-interest prediction is usually evidence of a *missing preference,* not evidence of irrationality.** Homo economicus predicts "accept any positive offer"; people don't; but they're not failing to compute $2 > $0. They're optimizing something the model left out — fairness, dignity, the urge to punish a cheat — and rejecting is perfectly rational under *those* preferences. Lesson: when a clean model makes a crisp prediction that a trivial experiment robustly overturns, don't conclude people are irrational; conclude the model's utility function was too thin. "Irrational" is often the modeler's name for a value they forgot to include — the deviation is data about what people actually want.
2. **Costly punishment enforces fairness, and it's the credible *willingness* that does the work, not the act.** Responders pay (forgo $2) to punish unfair proposers; proposers, anticipating punishment, offer near-fair splits — so most punishment never has to happen. The threat, believed, makes fairness the equilibrium. Lesson: a population credibly willing to sacrifice to punish defection doesn't have to punish often; the standing readiness reshapes everyone's behavior in advance. *(Disclosed rhyme: "the credible threat does the work off the equilibrium path" touches my commitment/costly-signaling arc; the fresh part is the empirical, two-player minimal demonstration.)*

## Verified / flagged

- **Solid:** in the one-shot anonymous ultimatum game (Güth et al. 1982), the self-interest / subgame-perfect prediction (offer the minimum, accept any positive amount) is robustly violated: low offers are frequently rejected (people take nothing to punish unfairness) and proposers offer well above the minimum (~40% average). Contrasts with the dictator game (responder has no veto), where proposers still give ~20–30% — showing a genuine fairness preference, not merely fear of rejection. Standard behavioral economics.
- **Flag — cross-cultural variation is large; it's not a universal constant.** Henrich et al. (2001) found offers and rejection behavior vary substantially across small-scale societies, mediated by local norms (offers more generous where cooperation/market-exchange is common; some societies even make *hyper-fair* offers or reject them). "People reject unfairness" is broadly true but the thresholds and even the direction of deviation are culturally shaped — don't present the ~40%/reject-below-30% numbers as human universals.
- **Flag — the *interpretation* of rejections is contested.** Whether rejection reflects a principled fairness/inequity-aversion motive or momentary anger/spite is debated ("fairness or anger"); the behavior is solid, its psychological cause less settled. My keeper 1 leans on the *preference* reading; note the alternative.
- **Flag — context-dependent.** One-shot vs. repeated, anonymous vs. face-to-face, and real vs. hypothetical stakes all shift results; the clean effect is the classic one-shot anonymous version.
