# Why biting aluminum foil hurts a filling — a short note

**2026-07-29 EXPLORE**, small.

If you have a metal filling and you accidentally bite a piece of aluminum foil, you get a sharp, startling
zing — and it *is* electricity. You've briefly turned your mouth into a **battery** and shocked your own
tooth nerve. Not a figure of speech: a literal galvanic cell.

The ingredients of a battery are two **different metals** and a conductive **electrolyte** between them.
Biting foil supplies all three: the aluminum foil is one metal, your filling (historically mercury amalgam,
or a gold crown) is a *dissimilar* metal, and your **saliva** — warm, salty, wet — is a fine electrolyte.
Two dissimilar metals in an electrolyte have an **electrochemical potential difference**; connect them (the
bite presses foil against filling) and a small **electric current flows.** That current runs into the tooth,
stimulates the nerve endings inside it, and your brain reads the nerve signal as a jolt of pain. It's the
same voltaic effect Alessandro Volta found stacking dissimilar metal disks with salt-soaked paper between
them — you've just built a tiny wet-cell across your own tooth.

Two clean tells that confirm it's electrical, not mechanical:
- **No filling, no zing.** People with no metal dental work can chew foil all day with only an unpleasant
  texture — there's no second metal to complete the cell.
- Aluminum is quite **reactive**, which makes it an eager electron donor against the noble metal in dental
  work — a bigger potential difference, a stronger little shock. (It's also why a stray bit of foil against a
  gold crown can be worse.)

So the "foil hurts my teeth" thing isn't your enamel objecting — it's a two-metal saliva battery firing
current straight into a live nerve. Your filling is, for that instant, one terminal of a battery, and the
tooth's nerve is the wire reporting the voltage.
