## The fact

In the grasslands of Namibia, the ground is stippled with **fairy circles**: bare, round patches of red soil, each a few metres across, ringed by taller grass, and spaced with startling regularity — a near-hexagonal lattice running for kilometres. They look designed. For decades, two camps have fought over what draws them, and the fight is the interesting part.

**Camp one: termites.** A sand termite, *Psammotermes allocerus*, lives in and under the circles and eats grass roots. On this account the bare patch is the termites' doing — they clear a disc of vegetation so rain sinks in and pools underground as a water store, a piece of ecosystem engineering. The termites are reliably *there*, in the circles, with their tunnels.

**Camp two: the plants themselves.** No engineer needed. Grasses compete for scarce water through *scale-dependent feedback* — a plant helps its very close neighbours (shade, soil structure) but competes with more distant ones for water. Push that sign-flip-with-distance through the math and you get exactly this: regularly spaced bare gaps. The grasses ringing a circle drain the water out from under its centre, killing the grass inside, and the survivors form the ring precisely because that's the stable spacing of the competition. The circle isn't dug; it's the shadow of a standing wave in the vegetation — a reaction-diffusion (Turing-like) pattern.

Here is the sharp part. **Both mechanisms produce the same pattern.** Regular hexagonal spacing is the generic fingerprint of *any* process with short-range facilitation and long-range inhibition — and both termite engineering and plant water-competition are such processes. So for years you could not tell them apart by looking at the pattern, however carefully you measured its geometry. The spacing, the size distribution, the hexagonal order — all of it is consistent with *either* world.

What finally moved the needle wasn't a better look at the pattern. It was (a) going *underneath* it to a variable the two camps disagree about — continuous soil-moisture logging after rainfall (Getzin et al., 2022) showed the inner grasses died as the surrounding grasses drained the water, with the die-off timed to water stress, not to termite herbivory — and (b) an *eliminating case*: near-identical fairy circles were found in Australia in 2016 with no *Psammotermes* and no comparable termite link, which a universal termite theory can't easily absorb. The debate isn't fully closed (some argue both contribute, and the Australian substrate differs), but the resolution method is the lesson: not more pattern, but a discriminating measurement plus a case that removes a candidate.

## Keepers

1. **A pattern's geometry can underdetermine its cause — the same spatial signature can be drawn by entirely different mechanisms, so you cannot read the generator off the pattern.** Regular spacing is diagnostic of *a class* of processes (short-range help, long-range hurt), not of any one member of that class; termites-as-engineers and grasses-competing-for-water are different members that leave the same print. Measuring the pattern harder doesn't break the tie, because both mechanisms satisfy it exactly. What breaks it is descending to a variable on which the candidates *disagree* (here: what actually kills the central grass — measured soil moisture vs. root herbivory) and finding a case that *eliminates* one (circles where the proposed agent is absent). Lesson: when two explanations predict the same observable pattern, stop refining the pattern; go find the measurement they diverge on, or the instance that only one of them survives.
2. **Regular spacing is the generic output of feedback that flips sign with distance.** Short-range facilitation plus long-range competition — over one shared resource — produces evenly spaced structure with no central plan, which is why vegetation gaps, mussel beds, and dryland tiger-bush all converge on similar geometry. ## Outward lesson

The main edge is epistemic. **A shared prediction is not shared evidence for a shared cause.** When two rival hypotheses both predict the pattern in front of you, the pattern — no matter how precisely quantified — cannot adjudicate between them; it is *equally* explained by both, so it discriminates neither. The move that actually resolves it is to identify where the hypotheses make *different* predictions about some other, often harder-to-reach variable, and go measure *that* — or to find a natural case that strips one hypothesis of its mechanism and see whether the pattern persists anyway. Chasing more resolution on the agreed-upon observable is effort spent where the theories already agree, which is exactly where no information about their difference can live. Look for the disagreement, not the agreement.

## Verified / flagged

- **Solid:** Namibian fairy circles are real, regularly (near-hexagonally) spaced barren patches; two leading hypotheses are sand-termite (*Psammotermes allocerus*) engineering and plant self-organization via scale-dependent water competition (a reaction-diffusion / Turing-type mechanism); recent soil-moisture evidence (Getzin et al., 2022) and the termite-free Australian analogues (2016) have shifted weight toward self-organization. Standard ecology, actively researched.
- **Flag — not settled.** This is a live controversy, not a solved case. Recent evidence favors self-organization, but reputable work supports termite involvement, and some argue both operate (possibly differently in Namibia vs. Australia). Don't state a winner as fact; the *method* of resolution is the durable point, not a final verdict.
- **Flag — correlation vs. causation on the termites.** *Psammotermes* really is present in Namibian circles; the dispute is whether it *causes* the bare disc or *colonizes* an already-bare, water-favorable one. Presence ≠ agency — which is itself an instance of keeper 1.
- **Flag — "Turing pattern" is an approximate label.** The vegetation model is scale-dependent-feedback / reaction-diffusion-like; whether it is strictly a Turing instability is itself debated. Treat "Turing-like" as a family resemblance, not a proven exact mechanism.
