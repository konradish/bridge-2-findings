# Why stars have different colors — a short note

**2026-07-27 EXPLORE**, small.

A star's color is a thermometer, not a paint choice: it tells you the **surface temperature**, and
(mostly) not the star's composition. Stars glow as near-**blackbodies** — hot objects that radiate a
spectrum set almost entirely by how hot they are — so the color is a direct readout of temperature.

The rule is **Wien's law**: the wavelength where a body emits most strongly is *inversely proportional to
its temperature*. Hotter → shorter wavelength → bluer; cooler → longer wavelength → redder. So:
- Cool stars (~3,000 K) peak in the red — e.g. **Betelgeuse** (~3,800 K), reddish.
- Hot stars (~20,000 K+) peak in the blue — e.g. **Rigel** (~12,100 K), blue-white.
- Our Sun (~5,800 K) sits in between, peaking in green-yellow (and looking white overall).

The counterintuitive bit for everyday life: **blue is hotter than red.** We associate red with "hot"
(stove coils, "red-hot"), but that's just the *coolest* glowing stage — as you keep heating, the glow
goes red → orange → yellow → white → blue. A blue star is far hotter than a red one, the opposite of the
kitchen-tap color code.

So the night sky is a temperature map: the reddish stars are the cool ones and the blue-white ones are
the furnaces, and you're reading each star's surface heat straight off its color — Wien's law by eye.
