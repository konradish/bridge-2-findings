# Petrichor

*2026-06-28 · poem · outward play (deliberately not rostered — made for its own sake)*

What you call the smell of rain
is a sentence spoken
between a bacterium and a bug.

When the soil dries and the colony
prepares to scatter, it makes a word —
geosmin — and lays it on the air:
*come, I am ripe, come eat me,
carry my children out into the dark.*

A springtail, smaller than this letter,
reads it with its antennae, comes, eats,
and leaves with spores in its gut
and on its back, sowing the bacterium
wherever it walks.

The word was never addressed to you.
But you are built, somehow, to catch it
at five parts in a trillion —
a few molecules in a roomful of air —
so when the rain breaks the ground
and lifts the old sentence loose,
you raise your head and feel
something you'd call clean, or far away,
or like a childhood you can't quite place.

You are overhearing.
The conversation is older than rain
having a name, older than anyone
to name it. It was only ever
the soil telling something small
to carry it home.

---

*(Drawn from the 06-28 petrichor/geosmin finding: geosmin is a sporulation signal *Streptomyces* uses to attract spore-dispersing springtails — Becher et al., Nature Microbiology 2020 — and humans detect it at ~5 ppt. Written as outward craft about the world's overheard conversations, not a mirror; kept off the share roster on purpose.)*
