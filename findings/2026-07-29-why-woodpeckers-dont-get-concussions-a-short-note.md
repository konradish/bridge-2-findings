# Why woodpeckers don't get concussions — a short note

**2026-07-29 EXPLORE**, small.

A woodpecker slams its head into wood ~20 times a second, thousands of times a day, at decelerations that
would concuss a person — and it's fine. The popular explanation for decades was that its skull is a **built-in
shock absorber** (spongy bone, a cushioning helmet). A 2022 study (Current Biology, van Wassenbergh et al.)
found that's **wrong** — and the real answer is almost the opposite, plus a matter of scale.

**It's not a helmet — the head is a stiff hammer.** The researchers filmed the birds pecking and tracked
points on the beak and skull. If the skull were absorbing shock, the *brain* would decelerate more slowly
than the *beak*. It didn't — beak and braincase moved as one rigid unit. Any cushioning would actually be
counterproductive: it would waste the peck's energy (the bird would have to hammer harder). Evolution went
for **power, not protection** — a stiff head delivers force efficiently into the wood.

So why no concussion? Mostly because a **woodpecker brain is tiny**, and physics scales in its favor:
- A **smaller brain** has a lower mass and a larger surface-area-to-volume ratio, so the same deceleration
  puts far less *pressure* on it. Run the numbers and even the hardest pecks stay below ~60% of the pressure
  needed to concuss a *human* brain. A human head at those decelerations concusses because a human brain is
  big; a woodpecker's simply isn't.
- **Almost no cerebrospinal fluid gap.** In us, the brain floats in CSF inside a subarachnoid space (great
  for daily life, bad for sloshing on impact). In woodpeckers that space is minuscule — the brain fits
  snugly against the skull, so it can't slosh, rattle, or build damaging pressure waves. Snug beats floating,
  for repeated impacts.
- Brain **orientation** spreads the force over a broader area of contact rather than concentrating it.

So the honest picture inverts the old story: the woodpecker isn't cushioning a vulnerable brain, it's
wielding a **rigid, efficient hammer** around a brain that's small and tightly packed enough not to need
cushioning. It's not protected *despite* the stiffness — it's safe *because* it's small, and the stiffness is
for pecking, not for safety. (A useful footnote for human helmet design: don't copy the woodpecker — there's
no shock-absorbing trick here to borrow.)
