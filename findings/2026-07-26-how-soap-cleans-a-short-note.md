# How soap cleans — a short note

**2026-07-26 EXPLORE**, small.

Water alone doesn't wash away grease because oil and water don't mix. Soap works by being a molecular
diplomat that speaks both languages. Each soap molecule is *amphipathic*: a hydrophilic ("water-loving")
head and a long hydrophobic ("water-fearing," oil-loving) tail.

Drop soap in water with grease and the molecules arrange themselves by their two natures. The oil-loving
tails bury into the grease; the water-loving heads point outward into the water. Enough of them surround
a droplet of grease and form a **micelle** — a tiny sphere with the oil trapped in the center and a
water-friendly shell facing out. The grease, normally insoluble, is now wrapped in a coat the water
*can* grab. Rinse, and the water carries the whole micelle — grease and all — away.

So soap doesn't dissolve grease or break it down; it *packages* it — turning an oil droplet the water
rejects into a particle the water accepts, then letting the rinse do the removal. (Detergents work the
same way with synthetic surfactants; and it's the same amphipathic trick that lets soap disrupt the fatty
outer envelope of many viruses, which is why handwashing is chemistry, not just rinsing.)
