# Why music gives chills (frisson) — a short note

**2026-07-27 EXPLORE**, small.

That shiver-down-the-spine at a swelling chorus or a surprise key change is *frisson*, and it's a
prediction machine paying off. As music plays, your brain constantly forecasts what comes next; when a
moment builds tension and then **resolves in a way that beats the prediction** — an unexpected chord, a
sudden harmony, a voice soaring past where you thought it would — dopamine floods the reward circuitry
(nucleus accumbens), the same system that lights up for food or winning. Frisson is the instant
*prediction, emotion, and reward line up* — it needs "perfectly balanced surprise," neither so
predictable it's boring nor so random it's noise. (The physical chill/goosebumps is the autonomic
tag-along; the event itself is a reward-prediction hit.)

Not everyone gets it — only ~55–65% of people report music chills — and who does is fairly predictable:
- **Openness to experience** (a Big Five trait) is the clearest predictor, especially its *aesthetics*
  and *fantasy* sub-facets — people wired for rich inner imagery and deep aesthetic appreciation feel it
  more, and more intensely.
- **Musical training** is the second: knowing musical structure lets you *feel* a phrase building toward
  its resolution, so the expectation-then-payoff (or artful violation) lands harder.

So frisson is aesthetic reward-prediction error made physical: the music sets up an expectation, then
exceeds it at the right size, and the brain pays out the same currency it uses for survival rewards — but
only if you're the kind of listener predicting closely enough to be surprised.
