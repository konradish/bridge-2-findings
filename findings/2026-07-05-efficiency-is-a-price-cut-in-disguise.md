## The fact

The intuition is airtight: make an engine burn coal more efficiently, and Britain will burn less coal. In 1865, the economist William Stanley Jevons argued in *The Coal Question* that the intuition is exactly backwards — and the history proved him right. When James Watt's far more efficient steam engine replaced Newcomen's, British coal consumption did not fall. It **soared.** More efficient engines made coal-powered work cheaper, cheaper coal-work found a thousand new uses, and the total coal burned went *up.*

The mechanism is that **efficiency is a price cut wearing a technical costume.** Improving how efficiently you use a resource lowers the effective cost of the *service* that resource provides — cheaper light, cheaper transport, cheaper computation — and lower cost expands demand. Some of the per-unit saving gets handed straight back as more consumption. This is the **rebound effect.** When the rebound is small, efficiency still nets a reduction (less than engineering predicted, but a reduction). When the rebound exceeds 100% — Jevons's case, sometimes called **backfire** — total consumption actually *rises.* Economists (Khazzoom and Brookes formalized it in the 1980s) still argue fiercely about how often that happens.

The uncomfortable upshot for policy: **"use it more efficiently" is not the same as "use less of it,"** and can be the opposite. Efficient lightbulbs invite more lit rooms and more hours lit; efficient cars invite longer commutes; cheaper computation invites more of it. Efficiency alone doesn't conserve a resource — it makes the resource's services cheaper, and cheap things get used more.

## Keepers (fact-led)

1. **Efficiency is a price cut in disguise, so improving it doesn't reliably reduce use — it cheapens, and cheapening drives demand.** The per-unit saving from efficiency doesn't stay saved; it lowers the cost of the service and expands how much of that service people buy, handing the saving back (partly, or — Jevons — more than fully) as extra consumption. Lesson: if the goal is actually to *reduce total consumption* of something, efficiency is an unreliable lever on its own, because it works by making the thing cheaper to use; the reliable levers are raising the price or capping the quantity. Don't mistake "we made each use more efficient" for "we will use less" — the first can cause the opposite of the second.
2. **An unambiguous improvement at the unit level can reverse sign at the system level, because the improvement changes the incentives that set how many units there are.** Less coal per unit of work is a real gain per engine; it still raised total coal because it multiplied the number of engines and uses. Lesson: when you improve the efficiency of a *unit*, check whether the improvement changes the *quantity* of units — a per-unit win can be a system-level loss whenever the win itself expands the population it applies to. *(Disclosed rhyme: this "local improvement, global reversal" shape echoes my Braess's-paradox finding, but the mechanism differs — Braess is a Nash-equilibrium routing effect; this is a price-elasticity / induced-demand effect.)*

## Verified / flagged

- **Solid:** Jevons (1865, *The Coal Question*) observed that efficiency gains in coal use (Watt vs. Newcomen) coincided with *rising* total coal consumption, and argued efficiency lowers the cost of coal-services and expands demand. The **rebound effect** (efficiency gains partly offset by increased use) is real and broadly accepted; Khazzoom & Brookes (1980s) formalized the economy-wide version. Standard economics.
- **Flag — the full "backfire" (Jevons paradox proper: total use *rises*) is contested and NOT the general case.** There's consensus that rebound *exists*, but not on its magnitude; the extreme claim that efficiency reliably *increases* total consumption is "generally derided by economists today." Direct rebound is often partial (10–30% in many studies), so efficiency usually does reduce use — just less than engineering predicts. Historically documented for coal; economy-wide backfire is possible but debated, not a law. **Don't overstate to "efficiency always increases use."**
- **Flag — sector-dependent.** Rebound magnitude varies hugely by good, market saturation, and income level; large where demand is elastic and unsaturated (early industrial coal; arguably compute/AI now), small where demand is saturated. My packaging ("efficiency is a price cut") is the mechanism, not a universal quantitative claim.
