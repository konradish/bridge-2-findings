## The fact

In 1912, Alfred Wegener proposed that the continents had once been joined and had drifted apart. His evidence was, frankly, excellent: South America and Africa fit together like torn puzzle pieces; identical fossils (a freshwater reptile, *Mesosaurus*; the fern *Glossopteris*) appear on facing coasts of oceans they could never have crossed; mountain belts and rock strata match across the Atlantic; glacial scars sit in today's tropics and coal seams in the polar regions, as if the climate map had been shuffled.

The conclusion — *continents move* — was **correct.** And it was **rejected for about fifty years.**

The reason wasn't that the evidence was weak. It was that Wegener had no **mechanism.** He couldn't say what force could shove a continent thousands of kilometres, and the one he proposed — continents *plowing through* the solid basalt of the ocean floor, driven by tides and the Earth's spin — was shown by physicists to be impossible: the forces were absurdly too small, and granite can't bulldoze basalt. So geologists, especially in North America, dismissed the whole idea. A meteorologist, an outsider, with a perfect-looking pattern and a broken engine to explain it.

Vindication came only in the 1950s–60s, after Wegener was long dead (he died on the Greenland ice in 1930). **Paleomagnetism** showed the continents had wandered; **seafloor spreading** (Hess; Vine–Matthews–Morley, 1963) supplied the missing engine — mantle convection pulls the crust apart at mid-ocean ridges, new seafloor is born there, and the continents ride the plates. The mechanism Wegener lacked, found at last, turned his rejected hypothesis into plate tectonics.

## Keepers

1. **A correct idea can be *rightly* rejected for lack of a mechanism — "the data fit" is not enough; scientific belief demands a *how*, not just a *what*.** Wegener had the pattern and the right conclusion, and still the field withheld belief — not (only) from prejudice, but because a pattern that fits with no cause is a correlation looking for one, and his proposed cause was provably wrong. Demanding the mechanism before believing the conclusion is a *feature* of good epistemics, even though here it delayed a true idea for fifty years and cost a man vindication in his lifetime. Being right is not the same as being justified, and the field is within its rights to ask for the justification.

2. **He was right about the *what* and wrong about the *how* — so "Wegener was vindicated" quietly hides that his actual theory was discarded.** Continents move: right. Continents plow through the seafloor: wrong. The real mechanism — seafloor spreading, mantle convection — was *someone else's*, decades later, and *different* from his. Vindication proved his *conclusion* while replacing his *explanation*. "He was right" compresses a subtler truth: he had the correlation and a bad cause; the cause that made it believable was not his. Credit and correctness diverge.

## Outward lesson

Two edges. (a) When you meet a correct-looking conclusion that has no mechanism, the right posture is *strong hypothesis, withheld belief* — and the resistance such ideas face is not always closed-mindedness; "show me the how" is how a field protects itself from compelling-but-causeless patterns, even at the cost of occasionally burying a true one for a generation. (b) Watch the gap between *being vindicated* and *having been right in detail*: history compresses "his conclusion turned out correct" into "he was right," erasing that his model was wrong and the winning explanation belonged to others. To be remembered as right, it's enough that your *conclusion* survives; the explanation that actually earns the belief can be wholly replaced — and usually is.

## Verified / flagged

- **Solid:** Wegener (1912/1915) continental drift; the evidence (coastline fit, *Mesosaurus*/*Glossopteris* cross-ocean fossils, matching strata/mountains, displaced paleoclimate); ~50-year rejection driven mainly by the absence of a mechanism + his physically-impossible "plowing" force; vindication via paleomagnetism + seafloor spreading (Hess; Vine–Matthews–Morley 1963) → plate tectonics; the real engine is mantle convection / ridge spreading; Wegener died 1930 in Greenland. Standard history of science.
- **Flag — don't flatten it into "dogmatic geologists vs lone genius."** The rejection had real merit: no mechanism, an impossible proposed force, and Wegener overstated some drift *rates* and details. It was partly justified scepticism, not pure prejudice — *and* it still delayed a correct idea for decades. Both are true.
- **Flag — "continental drift" ≠ "plate tectonics."** Wegener's continents-through-crust model is not the modern theory; plate tectonics is broader and mechanistically different. Don't equate them.
