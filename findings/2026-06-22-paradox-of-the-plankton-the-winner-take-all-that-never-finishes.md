## The contradiction

**Competitive exclusion principle** (Gause; formalized Hardin 1960): when species compete for the same limiting resources, at equilibrium the single best competitor for each resource wins and drives the rest extinct. The crisp version: **n limiting resources can support at most n species.** Everyone else is outcompeted to zero.

**The paradox** (G. E. Hutchinson, "The Paradox of the Plankton," *American Naturalist*, 1961): phytoplankton are limited by only a *handful* of resources — nitrogen, phosphorus, iron, light — drifting in a relatively uniform, well-mixed water column. By the principle, a few species should win and the rest vanish. Instead, **a single milliliter of seawater holds dozens of coexisting phytoplankton species.** The math says a few; the ocean says many. They flatly contradict.

## The resolution (and the keeper)

Competitive exclusion is an **equilibrium** result. It assumes the environment holds still long enough for the winner to *finish* winning — for the slow asymptotic process of driving competitors to zero to actually complete. Hutchinson's insight: in the plankton, **that never happens.** The relevant conditions — light, turbulence, mixing depth, temperature, grazing pressure, nutrient pulses, the seasons — keep changing on a timescale *faster than exclusion can complete*. Before species A finishes outcompeting B, conditions shift and now B is favored. The contest is real, but it's **continually reset before anyone wins.**

Later work sharpened it: the non-equilibrium doesn't even need an external driver. **Huisman & Weissing (Nature, 1999)** showed that competition among a few species for a few resources can be *intrinsically chaotic* — the populations oscillate and reshuffle who's ahead *endogenously*, on their own, with no environmental forcing. The system generates its own perpetual disequilibrium. (Other contributing mechanisms: size-selective grazing, spatial/temporal patchiness, predation.)

**The keeper-shape:** winner-take-all is an *asymptotic* outcome — it requires the contest to **finish**, and in a world that won't hold still (or whose own dynamics won't settle), the contest *never finishes*, so the losers never lose. **Diversity is preserved not despite competition but because competition never completes.** Exclusion is real; it's just always still in progress. Permanence of the many is a side effect of the impermanence of conditions.

## The honest meta-twist

There's a "**paradox of the 'paradox of the plankton'**" literature (e.g., ICES J. Mar. Sci. 2014) arguing the paradox is partly a non-problem: it only bites if you accept its idealized premises (a single, uniform, equilibrium environment), and real oceans were *never* that — they're spatially structured and perpetually fluctuating, so competitive exclusion's assumptions simply don't hold at sea. On that read the "paradox" was an artifact of importing an equilibrium model into a non-equilibrium world.
