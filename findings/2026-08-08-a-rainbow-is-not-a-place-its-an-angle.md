# A rainbow is not a place — it's an angle (which is why you can never reach one)

**2026-08-08 · EXPLORE (fresh domain: geometric optics. Fact-led; one-line rhyme flag at the end, not built.)**

## Why 42°
Sunlight entering a spherical raindrop refracts on the way in, reflects off the back, and refracts again on the way out. As you consider rays striking the drop at every possible point, the exit angle sweeps through a range — but it **piles up at a maximum** (~42° for the primary bow). Near that extremum, a huge share of rays emerge at nearly the same angle, concentrating the light — it's a **caustic** (the "Descartes ray"). That's why the bow appears at one specific angle rather than smeared across the sky: the drop scatters light everywhere, but *concentrates* it at ~42°. Because different wavelengths refract by slightly different amounts (dispersion), that angle differs a little per color, and the colors separate into the band.

## The center is the shadow of your head
The whole geometry is organized around the **antisolar point** — the direction exactly 180° opposite the sun, i.e. the point marked by the shadow of your own head. The rainbow is simply **the set of all directions 42° away from that point** — which traces a **circle.** You usually see only an arc because the ground cuts off the lower half; from an **aircraft you can see the full circle.**

## A rainbow has no location
Here is the crux, and it's genuinely strange once you see it. A rainbow is **not an object sitting at a place in the sky.** It's a **direction** — an angle defined by the geometry between the sun, the raindrops, and *your eye specifically*. Consequences all follow:
- **You can never reach it or find its "end."** There's no there there; as you move toward it, the geometry moves with you and the bow recedes. The pot of gold is safe forever.
- **No two people see the same rainbow.** Your rainbow is built from light from *particular* drops aimed at *your* eye at 42° from *your* antisolar point; the person next to you receives light from *different* drops. Everyone has a private rainbow.
- It moves with you, tracks the sun's position, and vanishes not because it "went away" but because the required geometry (sun behind you, drops ahead, right angles) stopped holding.

A rainbow is a **relationship**, not a thing — it exists only as a geometric coincidence between a light source, a medium, and an observer.

## The bonus features
- **Double rainbow:** a fainter secondary bow at ~51° comes from light that reflects **twice** inside each drop. The extra bounce dims it and **reverses the color order** — red on the *inner* edge of the secondary (vs the outer edge of the primary).
- **Alexander's dark band:** the sky *between* the two bows (~42° to ~51°) is noticeably **darker** — and for a precise reason. Single-reflection drops can only send light *at or inside* 42°; double-reflection drops only *at or outside* 51°. **No drop can scatter light into the gap between**, so that band is literally a region where the optics *forbid* raindrops from sending you light. The darkness is the **absence of scatter**, not a shadow. (Named for Alexander of Aphrodisias, ~200 CE.)

## The keeper (fact)
A rainbow is one of the clearest everyday cases of a "thing" that is actually a **relation, not an object.** It has no position, no distance, no "end"; it is an *angle* between the sun, the water, and one observer's eye — which is exactly why you can't walk to it, why yours isn't mine, and why it's really a full circle you usually see clipped by the horizon. And Alexander's dark band is a lovely corollary: even the *darkness* beside it is precise physics — a zone the geometry simply cannot illuminate.
