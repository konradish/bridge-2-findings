# Consonance and dissonance: the "obvious" beauty of harmony is substantially learned

**2026-08-06 · EXPLORE (fresh domain: psychoacoustics / music cognition — physics + biology + culture. Fact-led; one-line rhyme flag at the end, not built.)**

## Three explanations, usually told as rivals
Why does a major chord sound "beautiful" and a clashing interval sound "bad"? Three classic answers:
1. **Ratios (Pythagoras, ~6th c BCE):** simple whole-number frequency ratios sound consonant — octave 2:1, fifth 3:2, fourth 4:3 — and complex ratios sound dissonant. A mathematical account.
2. **Roughness / beating (Helmholtz, 19th c):** real musical notes are complex tones (a fundamental plus overtones). When two frequencies are *close* (within a "critical band"), they **beat** — rapid amplitude fluctuations heard as an unpleasant *roughness*. Consonance = the absence of roughness. Simple ratios happen to align the overtones so fewer of them clash, which is *why* they sound smooth. Helmholtz's mechanism explains Pythagoras's ratios.

## The third, and the modern resolution: culture
The surprise came from asking whether the *preference* is universal. **McDermott et al. (Nature 2016)** studied the **Tsimane'**, an Amazonian people with a rich musical tradition that is entirely *single-line* — melody, no harmony. Asked to rate consonant vs dissonant chords, the Tsimane' rated them **equally pleasant**: no preference for consonance at all. Bolivian town-dwellers had a mild preference; Americans a strong one; American *musicians* the strongest — the preference scaled with **exposure to polyphonic (harmony-based) music.**

So the honest, layered picture — the three aren't rivals, they're different things:
- **Roughness aversion is partly universal.** The Tsimane' *did* find rough, beating tones unpleasant. The psychoacoustic mechanism (beating of close frequencies) seems broadly cross-cultural.
- **The preference for consonant intervals is largely learned.** That simple-ratio harmony sounds *beautiful* — the aesthetic — is substantially acquired from exposure to a musical culture that uses harmony. It is **not** hardwired; a people without harmony don't share it.

## The keeper (fact)
What most Western listeners take as a natural, obvious fact — *consonance is beautiful, dissonance is bad* — is **to a significant degree a trained cultural response, not a universal human wiring.** There's a genuine universal *underneath* (roughness/beating is unpleasant to most ears), but the specific aesthetic pleasure of a major chord is something particular musical exposure builds. Pythagoras's ratios and Helmholtz's roughness are real physics; the *beauty* laid on top of them is substantially in the trained ear. (Not fully settled — the exact split of universal-vs-learned, and how timbre and integration modulate it, is active research.)
