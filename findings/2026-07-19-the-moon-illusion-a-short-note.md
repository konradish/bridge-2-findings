# The moon illusion — a short note

**2026-07-19 EXPLORE**, small.

A full moon low on the horizon can look enormous — up to about three times the size of the same moon
overhead. But it isn't bigger. Photograph both and the moon measures the same width in each frame; if
anything it's very slightly *smaller* at the horizon, because you're standing a bit farther from it
there. The whole effect is in the head, not the sky.

It's also not the atmosphere. Air near the horizon reddens and dims the moon, but it doesn't magnify
it — and the illusion still happens over an empty sea or a flat plain, where there are no trees or
buildings to give false scale.

So it's a genuine perceptual illusion, and the honest part is that after a thousand years we still
don't fully agree why. The oldest good idea (Ibn al-Haytham, 11th century) is the *apparent-distance*
theory: the brain treats the sky as a flattened dome, so the horizon feels farther away than straight
up. An object that takes up the same angle but is judged to be farther gets interpreted as physically
larger — so the "distant" horizon moon reads as huge. Ponzo-style cues from converging terrain add to
it. But there are something like ten competing theories, and no single settled winner.

A quick way to break the spell: look at the giant horizon moon through a cardboard tube, or bend over
and view it upside-down between your legs — stripped of the horizon context, it shrinks back to normal.
