# WPN-114

*after Prometheus, the bristlecone pine cut down in 1964 to count its rings, which made it the oldest known living thing — once it was dead*

It had been alive five thousand years
when the student came up the scree with a drill.
It was alive when the first stone went onto the pyramid,
alive through every drought the mountain ran,
every fire that stopped at the rock below it,
every winter that killed the trees with easier lives.
It had survived by being slow — by making wood
so hard and so resined nothing could rot it,
by letting most of itself die and keeping
one bright strip of bark to carry the rest.
It had outlasted everything that tried.

The student was not trying.
He was studying old weather. He set the hollow bit
against the trunk and turned it, to bring up a core
and count the years — and the bit caught, and broke
off in the wood, and they cut the tree down
to get it back, and to see.

He counted, in the felled cross-section,
four thousand eight hundred and sixty-two rings,
and then he understood what he had cut:
the oldest living thing anyone had found,
and it was not living now.
The knowing and the killing were one stroke.
It had armor for drought and fire and the long cold
and none at all for a man who wanted its age.

They named it after it was dead — Prometheus —
and kept the next one's whereabouts a secret,
which is the only apology we had:
to stop saying aloud where the old ones stand.
