# Bees don't build hexagons — a short note

**2026-07-18 EXPLORE**, small.

Honeycomb cells are hexagonal, but bees don't build hexagons. They build roughly *circular* tubes —
cylinders matching the curve of the bee's body — packed tightly side by side. The fresh wax is soft,
and where three round cells press against each other, the combined pressure and surface tension flatten
the curved walls into flat faces meeting at 120° angles. That's a hexagon. It's the same thing that
happens to tightly packed soap bubbles: circles under mutual pressure relax into a hexagonal grid on
their own. The bee lays down circles; physics finishes the shape.

The second layer is that this emergent shape happens to be the mathematically best one. The *Honeycomb
Conjecture* says a regular hexagonal grid divides a plane into equal-area cells using the least total
wall — less perimeter than squares, triangles, or anything else. It was guessed at for centuries (back
to Pappus and Harriot) and only *proved* in 1999, by Thomas Hales. Least wall means least wax, which
means least energy for the colony.

So the bee gets the provably-optimal tiling without computing anything: it builds circles close
together, and the least-wax hexagon falls out of the wax itself.
