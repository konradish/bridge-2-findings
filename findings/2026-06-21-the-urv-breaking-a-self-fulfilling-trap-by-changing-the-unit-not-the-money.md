# The URV: how Brazil broke hyperinflation by changing the unit of account, not the money

## The problem: inflation that feeds on itself

By the early 1990s Brazil had chronic hyperinflation — prices rising on the order of ~2,000%+ a year, for *years*. The deep trouble wasn't just too much money; it was **inertial inflation**: past inflation was *indexed* into the future. Wages, rents, and contracts were tied to recent price rises, so everyone raised their prices because they *expected* everyone else to raise theirs — and they were right. It's a **self-fulfilling coordination trap**: the bad equilibrium is each person's rational best response to everyone else's expected behavior. You can't escape it by decree ("stop raising prices") — defecting alone just means you fall behind. Money-printing fed it, but even with the printing controlled, the *expectation* kept it spinning.

## The move: a currency that didn't circulate

In **February 1994**, the architects (finance minister Fernando Henrique Cardoso + economists **Arida, Lara Resende, Franco, Bacha, Fritsch** — building on Lara Resende & Arida's 1984 "indexed currency"/Larida proposal, itself partly inspired by Israel's 1980s freeze) did something strange. They introduced the **URV — Unidade Real de Valor — a virtual currency that nobody could hold or spend.** It was *only a unit of account*: a stable yardstick, pegged ~1:1 to the dollar and updated daily.

You still paid in the old, inflating cruzeiro real. But **prices, wages, and contracts were now quoted in URV.** A coffee cost, say, 1 URV — a number that *stayed put* — even though the cruzeiro-real price printed on the tag climbed every day. For months, Brazilians lived a split: transacting in money that inflated, but *thinking, pricing, and negotiating* in a unit that didn't. The constant background noise of "everyone's raising prices" went quiet *in the unit that mattered for expectations*.

Then, on **July 1, 1994**, they swapped: the URV became a real, physical currency — the **Real** — at 1:1. The new money launched into an economy whose expectations were *already anchored to a stable unit*. The indexation spiral had nothing to grip.

**Results:** monthly inflation (IPCA) ran **47.43% in June 1994 → 6.84% in July → 1.71% by December.** A decades-long hyperinflation collapsed in months.

## The keeper (the distinctive shape)

Money does several jobs at once — medium of exchange, store of value, **unit of account**. The URV's insight was to **pry the unit of account loose from the medium of exchange and stabilize *it first*.** A self-fulfilling expectation trap is a coordination problem: everyone's locked to the same focal point (recent inflation), and force can't move it because no one can defect alone. The URV didn't fight the focal point — it **offered a new, stable one** and let everyone re-coordinate onto it *voluntarily*, because pricing in a steady unit was simply easier. By the time the actual money changed, the expectations had already moved. **You change what people measure in before you change what they pay with.** Defeat a coordination trap by relocating the focal point, not by overpowering the old one.

## Honest caveats (it wasn't only the trick)
- **The URV was the clever centerpiece, NOT the whole plan.** The Plano Real also did the unglamorous work: spending cuts and new taxes to shrink the deficit, ending automatic Central Bank financing of the Treasury, monetary tightening, debt restructuring. Without that fiscal anchor the trick would not have held — a stable unit of account with an out-of-control deficit behind it just re-inflates. The "psychological masterstroke" telling is real but partial; **don't credit the URV alone.**
- **Stabilization isn't the end of the story.** The same literature ("The Real Plan: Stabilization and Destabilization") notes the Real later became overvalued, contributing to the 1998–99 currency crisis. The trap was broken; new problems followed. A fix to one coordination failure is not permanent macro health.
