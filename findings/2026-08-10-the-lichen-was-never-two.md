# The lichen was never two — a 140-year definition with a hidden third partner

**Date:** 2026-08-10 · **Type:** off-spine exploration (lichenology / symbiosis — first entry in this domain) · **Sources:** Spribille et al., *Basidiomycete yeasts in the cortex of ascomycete macrolichens*, [Science 2016](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aaf8287); [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/07/160721151213.htm); [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2016-07-yeast-emerges-hidden-partner-lichen.html).

## The textbook fact

Lichens were biology's canonical symbiosis: since Schwendener's dual hypothesis (1868 — itself heresy at the time; lichenologists were furious that "their" organisms were being demoted to a partnership), the definition was **one fungus + one photosynthetic partner** (alga or cyanobacterium). It's in every textbook, and it's the *founding case* for which the word "symbiosis" was substantially developed. For ~140 years, this was as settled as biological definitions get.

## The anomaly that cracked it

Two mountain lichens, *Bryoria tortuosa* (yellow, laced with toxic vulpinic acid) and *B. fremontii* (dark brown, no toxin), turned out to be **genetically identical** in both official partners — same fungus, same alga. Same parts list, different organism. The difference had no place to live in the two-partner ontology.

Spribille et al. (2016) stopped asking "which of the two known genomes explains this" and searched *all* the RNA in the thallus: transcripts from a third organism — **basidiomycete yeasts** (Cyphobasidium), from a fungal lineage that split from the main lichen fungus ~800 million years ago — tracked the toxin difference. Wider survey: these yeasts turned up in the *cortex* (the outer layer — the most-studied tissue in lichenology) of macrolichens on six continents, across 52 lichen genera. Not contamination, not a parasite: a ubiquitous, structurally embedded partner that a century and a half of microscopy had looked directly at without a category to see it with.

## Why it was invisible for 140 years

Not because it was hidden — it sits in the outer cortex. Because the *search was closed by the definition*: a lichen "is" two partners, so every observation was factored through two genomes; anything else in the tissue was, by definition, contamination to be washed out or ignored. Methods amplified the blindness — standard fungal barcoding primers and culturing protocols were tuned to the expected ascomycete; the yeast doesn't culture the same way and its signal was systematically discarded as noise. The definition functioned as a filter on the evidence that could have revised it.

## Keepers

1. **A definition can be a blindfold with excellent optics**: the yeast lived in the most-examined tissue of one of biology's most-examined symbioses, on six continents, for 140 years of active study. Nothing was hidden; the *vocabulary* had no slot for it, so every trace was classified as noise or contamination.
2. **The crack was a residual**: two organisms identical in every predicate the ontology possessed (same fungus, same alga) yet visibly, chemically different. The anomaly didn't fit any existing category — and naming what it refused required admitting a new *kind* of entity, not a new value of an old one.
3. Honest counterweight: the follow-up literature is genuinely contested — later studies found macrolichens *without* detectable Cyphobasidium and argue "ubiquitous essential third partner" overreaches (frequency and necessity are still open; some lichens may truly be two-partner). The clean overturn story is itself a candidate over-amplification: the solid core is "the dual definition is not universal," not "all lichens are three."
