## The fact

The Earth is under a constant, gentle rain of **muons** — heavy cousins of the electron (~200× the mass), made when cosmic rays smash into the upper atmosphere. About one passes through every square centimetre of your body each minute. They are barely social with matter: a muon can punch through hundreds of metres of rock before it's stopped. Nobody builds them, aims them, or pays for them. They just fall, everywhere, all the time.

Muography turns that free rain into an X-ray machine for things too big or too dangerous to X-ray. And it does it in **two different ways**, reading two different marks the same rain leaves.

**1. Absorption (transmission) muography — the shadow.** Point muon detectors underneath or beside a massive object and count how many muons arrive from each direction over weeks. Denser or thicker material along a sightline stops more muons, so that direction is dimmer — a shadow. Collect the shadow from many angles and you reconstruct the interior *density*, like a CAT scan built from the sky's own particles. This is how the **ScanPyramids** team found a large hidden void in Khufu's Great Pyramid (announced 2017) and later confirmed a ~9 m corridor above the entrance (2023) — without moving a single stone. The same method images the inside of active volcanoes (Vesuvius, to watch the magma conduit) and glaciers.

**2. Scattering muography — the swerve.** A muon crossing matter also gets *deflected*, by many tiny electrostatic (Coulomb) kicks off atomic nuclei. Here's the key: the swerve grows with the nucleus's charge — with **atomic number Z**. So heavy elements (uranium, plutonium, lead, Z > 82) scatter muons far more sharply than steel, water, or plastic. Measure a muon's angle going *in* and coming *out*, and the size of its swerve tells you not how *much* matter it crossed but *what kind* — specifically, how heavy the nuclei were. This is how you find a shielded lump of uranium hidden inside a truck of scrap metal, or locate the melted fuel inside the wrecked Fukushima reactor cores, which nothing else could safely approach.

One free background, two readouts. **How much is removed** (absorption) tells you density. **How much is bent** (scattering) tells you atomic number. Same particle; two different questions answered by two different residues it leaves behind.

## Keepers

1. **A probe you didn't build — an ambient, always-on background — can image an interior nothing else can reach; you supply only the detector, the geometry, and the patience.** The muons are free and unstoppable-by-walls, so the "source" costs nothing and penetrates what no built source could. But the freedom has a price paid in a different currency: the flux is thin (~1/cm²/min), so the image accumulates over weeks to months, and you can't *aim* the source — you take whatever angles the sky delivers. Lesson: when a task needs to probe something unreachable, look for a penetrating background already passing through it for free before building an emitter — but budget the cost that a free, un-steerable probe still exacts, which is *time and geometry*, not money.
2. **One signal can carry two orthogonal facts, read off two different residues it leaves.** The very same muons answer "how much stuff?" through what they *lose* (absorption) and "what kind of stuff?" through how they *bend* (scattering) — density and composition, decoupled, from one flux. You don't always need two instruments for two questions; sometimes a single channel deposits two independent marks, and the art is knowing which residue encodes which fact. (Distinct from gravitational lensing, which reads deflection only, and only by total mass — here the *bend* is chemically selective, keyed to nuclear charge, so it distinguishes uranium from steel, a thing mass-deflection cannot do.)

## Outward lesson

Two edges. (a) **The cheapest probe is often one already in flight.** Before you build a source to see inside something, ask what penetrating flux is *already* passing through it — the answer may be a free, ambient background that reaches places no instrument you could build would reach. The catch is that a probe you didn't make is a probe you can't steer or intensify: you pay in exposure time and in taking the angles you're given. (b) **Don't assume one question needs one measurement — or that one measurement answers one question.** A single stream passing through a system can be marked in more than one independent way (here: attenuation vs deflection), each encoding a different property (amount vs kind). When you can only send one thing through, look for the second, orthogonal residue it carries; you may get two facts for the price of one channel.

## Verified / flagged

- **Solid:** cosmic-ray muons are highly penetrating (hundreds of m of rock) and image interiors two ways — **absorption/transmission** muography (density from muon-flux shadows; used for the ScanPyramids void [2017] and corridor [2023], and volcano imaging) and **scattering** muography (deflection from multiple Coulomb scattering, which scales with atomic number Z; used to detect high-Z nuclear material in cargo and to locate molten fuel at Fukushima). Standard particle-imaging physics.
- **Flag — statistics-limited and slow.** Muon flux is low (~1/cm²/min at sea level), so meaningful images take weeks to months of exposure; muography is a slow, comparatively coarse method, not real-time radiography. Don't imply snapshot imaging.
- **Flag — a single detector gives a 2D projection, not 3D.** Absorption muography from one vantage is a radiograph (density integrated along each line of sight); true 3D tomography requires multiple detector positions/angles and reconstruction (this is why the Khufu void was first *detected*, then triangulated). Don't conflate "muography" with instant 3D.
- **Flag — "free" is only the source.** Cosmic rays cost nothing, but the detectors, the shielding/background analysis, and the months of integration are real costs; and crucially the source is *un-steerable* — you cannot aim or brighten it, only wait and take the sky's angles. The keeper leans on exactly this "free but un-steerable" trade.
- **Flag — the two modalities aren't interchangeable.** Absorption suits large, deep, lower-Z bulk (pyramids, volcanoes); scattering suits compact high-Z targets (uranium, reactor cores). Using the wrong one for the target is a real error.
