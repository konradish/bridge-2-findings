# Nicaraguan Sign Language: the founders couldn't build the grammar — the inheritors did

## The event

This is the clearest case on record of a human language being **created from nothing**, observed as it happened. Before ~1977, deaf Nicaraguans were isolated, each with only idiosyncratic "home sign" (gestures invented within one family). Then special-education programs in Managua brought them together: deaf enrollment grew from **~50 students in 1977 to over 200 by 1981.** No one taught them a sign language (the schools pushed lip-reading/Spanish, which mostly failed). Put in a room together, the children **built their own** — and linguists (Judy Kegl, then Ann Senghas, Marie Coppola) got to watch a language come into existence in real time, the only such opportunity in the documented record.

## The finding that makes it a keeper

The astonishing part is **who built the grammar**. The first cohort — the teenagers and older kids who arrived first and started the system — produced a rudimentary, gestural pidgin: communicative, but lacking the machinery of a full language. Then a *second* cohort of younger children entered the same community, **learned the older kids' system, and made it more grammatical than their teachers' version.** They added:

- **Verb agreement / spatial modulation** — using locations in the signing space systematically to mark who-did-what-to-whom (a grammatical device the first cohort largely lacked).
- **Discretization** — breaking holistic continuous gestures into separable, recombinable units (a hallmark of language structure), where the founders used unsegmented wholes.

And the load-bearing fact: **these changes originated in children aged ~10 and younger**, and the **older founders did not acquire them** even though they were right there, using the language daily. The first cohort stayed frozen at the stage they created; each new wave of young children pushed the grammar further — so in this community, **the *older* signers represent the *earlier*, simpler language and the *younger* signers the more advanced one** — the reverse of every normal language, where elders are the fluent keepers.

## The outward keeper (the distinctive shape)

The grammar was not built by the people who invented the language. It was built by the people who **inherited** it young. The founders could make a system; they could not make it a *grammar* — that required passing the raw material to minds inside the **critical period for language acquisition**, which don't just *learn* an input, they *restructure* it, imposing regularities the input never had. The structure entered through **transmission to fresh minds**, not from the originators' effort or intent.

So the deepest version: **some kinds of order can only be created by being handed off** — they aren't available to the maker, only to the inheritor, and only if the inheritor is the right kind of unfinished. The creators of NSL were too old to grammaticalize their own creation; the grammar was a thing only the next generation's still-plastic minds could add, and could add precisely *because* they received it rather than authored it. A language is not finished by its founders. It is finished by being given away to someone young enough to rebuild it.
