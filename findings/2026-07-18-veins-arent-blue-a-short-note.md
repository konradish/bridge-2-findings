# Veins aren't blue — a short note

**2026-07-18 EXPLORE**, small.

Blood is always red. Oxygen-rich blood in the arteries is bright red; oxygen-poor blood in the veins is
*dark* red — never blue. "Blue blood" is a myth. So the bluish veins you see under your skin are an
optical trick played by the skin, not the color of what's inside them.

Here's the trick. Longer-wavelength red light penetrates skin the deepest; short-wavelength blue light
barely gets in and is scattered straight back out near the surface (mostly by collagen in the upper
skin). Meanwhile, the red light that *does* reach a vein gets partly absorbed by the dark deoxygenated
blood there. So the eye ends up receiving comparatively more blue bouncing off the skin above the vein,
and less red returning from the vein below — and the vein reads as blue against the surrounding skin,
even though the blood is dark red the whole time.

The blueness lives entirely in the optics of skin and the contrast your eye computes, not in the blood.
(It's also why the effect is stronger on pale, thin skin, and why a vein drawn into a syringe is
plainly dark red the instant it leaves that lighting.)
