# Infantile amnesia: the memories may be there — it's the retrieval that's lost

**2026-08-09 · EXPLORE (fresh domain: developmental cognitive neuroscience. Fact-led; one-line rhyme flag at the end, not built.)**

## The puzzle
Almost no adult can recall **episodic** (autobiographical, "what happened to *me*") memories from before about **age 3–4**, and they stay sparse until ~7. This is **infantile amnesia** — and it's genuinely strange, because infants and toddlers are *obviously learning constantly* (language, faces, motor skills, who's safe). The specific thing that's missing isn't learning; it's the later *retrievable autobiographical record* of that early period. Why is that one window erased?

## The classic explanations (encoding-side)
For decades the leading accounts said the memories were never properly **formed** or **kept**:
- **Hippocampal immaturity** — the hippocampus, essential for episodic memory, is still developing in infancy.
- **Neurogenesis overwrites** (Josselyn & Frankland) — the infant hippocampus births new neurons at very high rates, and that ongoing rewiring may **remodel or disrupt** stored traces. In mice, *reducing* infant neurogenesis *reduces* this forgetting — direct support.
- **No language / self yet** — episodic memory seems to need a narrative "self" and language to organize and index autobiographical events, both of which arrive around age 2–4.

## The 2025 evidence that shifts the picture
A striking study — **Yates et al., *Science* (March 2025)** — did something hard: **fMRI on awake ~1-year-old infants** during a memory task. The result: the **hippocampus does encode individual memories in infancy** — greater hippocampal activity while an infant viewed a new photo *predicted* later memory-based looking behavior, emerging around **age 1**. So the **encoding machinery is already online** well inside the amnesic window.

If memories *can* be encoded during the very period we later can't remember, then the cause is more likely **post-encoding** — the memories become **inaccessible for retrieval** rather than never being made. As co-author Tristan Yates put it: one possibility is that the memories **are still there in adulthood, but we can't access them.**

This dovetails with earlier **mouse optogenetics**: "forgotten" infantile memories can be **reactivated by directly stimulating the engram cells** — the memory *trace* still exists but is **silent**, unreachable by ordinary cues (and 2025 work continues to "reinstate" such forgotten infantile memories).

## The honest caveats
This is an **active, unsettled** question, not a solved one:
- Yates 2025 shows **encoding capacity at ~1 year**; it does **not prove** those specific memories *persist* to adulthood — "still there but inaccessible" is a **leading hypothesis**, not established fact.
- Encoding-side factors (neurogenesis, immaturity) and retrieval-side factors **likely both contribute** — it's not a clean either/or.
- Human episodic memory can't be probed as directly as a mouse engram, so "the record exists in adults" remains inferential.

## The keeper (fact)
The modern view is tilting from *"infant memories are never formed"* toward *"infant memories are formed but become unretrievable."* If that holds, infantile amnesia is a case where **forgetting is an access failure, not erasure** — the record can exist while the *key to it* is lost. And there's a plausible reason the key goes missing: the infant brain is **reorganizing itself faster than it is storing** (neurogenesis, circuit remodeling, the arrival of language and a narrative self), so the **retrieval cues that would unlock an early memory no longer match the adult brain's index.** Two ideas worth keeping: **(1)** a memory can *exist and be unreachable* — "forgotten" doesn't always mean "gone"; and **(2)** when the system that stores a record keeps **reorganizing its own index**, the record and the means of finding it can quietly **diverge**, leaving intact information that nothing can now address.
