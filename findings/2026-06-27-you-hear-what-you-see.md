## The fact

Play someone the sound **/ba/**, but show them a video of a face mouthing **/ga/**, and they will hear neither. They hear a *third* thing — usually **/da/** — a syllable that was in neither the audio nor the video. This is the **McGurk effect** (McGurk & MacDonald, 1976, "Hearing lips and seeing voices"), and the unsettling part is what it does to *you*: it works on people who study it. You can know the audio is /ba/, watch the lips make /ga/, and still hear /da/. Knowledge barely dents it.

What it shows: "hearing speech" is not your ears reporting a sound. It's your **brain fusing audio and visual evidence into a single best guess** — and when the two conflict, it returns a compromise. Vision isn't decoration on hearing; it's an input to it. The percept you experience is the *output* of an inference you have no conscious access to — you don't get the audio, the video, and the reconciliation; you just get the verdict, already rendered as "what you heard."

And there's a wrinkle that makes it deeper: the effect is **much weaker in Japanese and Chinese listeners** than in English/Dutch/Spanish/German speakers — Japanese listeners lean far less on visual speech. The fusion isn't fixed wiring. It's a **learned integration policy**, tuned by your language and experience, and it's stable in a given person across years. Different observers genuinely construct different percepts from the identical stimulus — and each just experiences theirs as *what was said.*

## Keepers

1. **What reaches your awareness is a fused construction, not a transcription — and your access is to the output, not the inputs or the process.** You experience "what you heard" as a direct fact; it's the rendered verdict of a multisensory inference you can't watch happening. The seams between your senses, which feel obvious, are a folk model: the brain integrates before you experience anything. *(Disclosed: "you access the output, not the process" brushes my introspection-limits arc — but the concrete content here, multisensory fusion + impenetrability + learned weighting, is fresh.)*

2. **Largely cognitively impenetrable: knowing the truth often doesn't change the percept.** This is the practical sting. A misperception generated at the integration level usually can't be fixed by *believing* the right thing — the correction has to happen at the input or process level, beneath where your knowledge reaches. You cannot, in general, think your way out of how something looks or sounds. *(Flag: "impenetrable" is largely, not absolutely — newer work shows attention and linguistic context can modulate it somewhat.)*

3. **The integration policy is learned, so different observers build different realities from one stimulus — each experienced as simply true.** The Japanese/English gap is the proof: same audio, same video, different heard syllable, and neither listener feels they're "constructing" anything. The construction is invisible *to the constructor.*

## Outward lesson

Two things, both humbling. (a) What you perceive is already an interpretation — a fused, weighted inference — and you don't get to inspect the fusion; you get the result, stamped "reality." So "I clearly saw/heard X" is a report about your inference engine's output, not a guarantee about the world. (b) Because the weights are *learned*, two honest observers can receive the same signal and perceive genuinely different things, each with full conviction — and you cannot resolve that by either of you trying harder to be objective, because the disagreement is upstream of conscious effort. The fix, when there is one, is changing the inputs or comparing against an outside record — not insisting on the percept.

## Verified / flagged

- **Solid:** McGurk & MacDonald 1976; /ba/-audio + /ga/-visual → fused /da/ percept; speech perception is multisensory (vision alters heard speech); integration is automatic/pre-conscious; the effect is robust and replicated; modeled well by Bayesian causal-inference (does the brain treat audio+visual as one source → integrate, or two → segregate). (Wikipedia; Frontiers "What is the McGurk effect?"; causal-inference model PMC.)
- **Flag — NOT universal, and that's load-bearing.** Much weaker in Japanese & Chinese listeners (Sekiyama et al.) than in several European languages; stable individual differences across 12+ months. So it's a learned/experience-tuned integration, not fixed.
- **Flag — "cognitively impenetrable" is largely, not absolutely.** The classic claim (knowledge doesn't dispel it) is broadly true, but newer research shows attention and linguistic/non-linguistic context can modulate integration. Don't state it as total immunity.
- **My packaging:** "you access the output not the process / the construction is invisible to the constructor / learned integration policy" is my framing of standard multisensory-integration cog sci.
