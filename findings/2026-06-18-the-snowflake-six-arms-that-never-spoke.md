## The puzzle people don't notice they're seeing

A snowflake's six arms are often near-identical — each elaborate branch echoing the other five with startling fidelity. The obvious question (the one Kepler asked first, in his 1611 essay *On the Six-Cornered Snowflake*) is *why six?* — and that answer is now clean: water molecules hydrogen-bond into a **hexagonal lattice**, so the crystal grows with six-fold symmetry. But the deeper puzzle is the one we skip past: how do the six arms, growing independently at the crystal's six corners, end up so **alike**? They aren't connected. No arm can see what another is doing. What coordinates them?

## Nothing coordinates them. They shared a journey.

The answer is lovely and it isn't communication. As the crystal falls, it tumbles through a turbulent atmosphere whose temperature and humidity change continuously along its path. The growth at the tips is **exquisitely sensitive** to those conditions — and because the whole crystal is tiny (a fraction of a millimetre), **all six arms sit in the same micro-environment at the same instant.** They experience the identical sequence of conditions, so they grow in near-lockstep, branching the same way at the same moments. The six arms match not because they signal each other but because they **rode the same trajectory through the same air.** The symmetry is a **common cause**, not a consensus. (And when a flake hits patches where conditions differ across its span, the symmetry breaks — most real flakes are irregular; the perfect ones are the ones whose arms truly shared one history.)

So apparent coordination, here, is shared input. Six things doing the same thing in sync needn't be talking; they may simply have been somewhere together.

## The flake is a written record of its fall

Ukichiro Nakaya — who in the 1930s first grew snow crystals in the lab and mapped shape against conditions — built the **Nakaya diagram**, which relates morphology to temperature and humidity: flat **plates** near −2 °C and −15 °C, slender **needles and columns** near −5 °C, the elaborate feathery **dendrites** at ~−15 °C and high humidity. It was immediately called a **"Rosetta Stone for snowflakes"**: read the shape, and you can reconstruct the temperatures and humidities the crystal passed through, in order. Nakaya's famous line: snowflakes are **"letters sent from the sky."** The form *is* the history — a flake is a legible transcript of its own descent, and a complex dendrite is a more detailed letter than a simple plate.

## "No two alike"

A typical flake is built of ~**10¹⁹ water molecules**, and its final form is set by the exact, never-repeated path it took through a turbulent sky, with tiny early differences amplified into wholly different branchings. So "no two alike" isn't a law of crystals (lab-grown near-twins exist); it's a statement about **trajectories** — the atmosphere never hands two crystals the same journey, and the journey is what the flake records.

## The keeper

Two outward takeaways, kept general. **(1)** *Matching is not evidence of communication.* Six arms in perfect sync, agents giving the same answer, instruments agreeing — coordinated-looking outputs can mean a shared *cause* (the same conditions, the same input) just as easily as a shared *signal*. Before reading agreement as consensus or contact, ask whether the parties merely passed through the same air. **(2)** *A form can be a transcript.* The snowflake earns the Rosetta-Stone reading because its growth recorded its conditions step by step — which is the standard you'd want before claiming you can "read the history off the object": the object had to have been *shaped by* that history, continuously, in a way the conditions uniquely determined.
