# Inevitable, Given

*Essay, 2026-06-24. ~860 words. From the Ostrom / tragedy-of-the-commons finding. Roster essay #11. Outward (how "proofs of inevitability" work and how to read them). Carries an honest edge: the move cuts both ways — it's a discipline of checking the assumption case by case, not a license for optimism.*

---

For forty years, "the tragedy of the commons" was treated as a law. Garrett Hardin's 1968 argument was clean and grim: a shared pasture is doomed, because each herder gains the full value of one more cow while the cost of overgrazing is split among all, so every rational herder adds cows until the grass is gone. The policy conclusion followed like a theorem — shared resources must be privatized or put under the state, because left as commons they will inevitably be destroyed.

Elinor Ostrom won a Nobel Prize for noticing that the world was full of counterexamples. Swiss villages have grazed Alpine meadows in common since the 1200s. A water court in Valencia has settled irrigation disputes for roughly a thousand years. These commons were not collapsing. A rule from one Swiss village, written in 1517, says no one may put more cows on the summer meadow than they can feed through the winter — and in that single sentence the tragedy is disarmed, because now taking more from the shared field costs you on your own.

The interesting thing is not just that Hardin was wrong. It is *where* he was wrong, because it's the same place a great many "inevitabilities" go wrong.

Hardin's proof was airtight — *given its assumptions.* And the assumptions were that the herders cannot talk to each other, cannot make a binding agreement, cannot watch the meadow, and cannot punish a cheat. Grant those, and the tragedy genuinely follows. But notice what that list of assumptions actually is: it is a precise description of people with *no governance at all.* The one thing Hardin's model ruled out — the capacity to make and enforce rules — is exactly the thing whose presence dissolves the doom. His proof of inevitability had quietly built its conclusion into its premises. The "law of the commons" was really a law of the *ungoverned* commons, with the qualifier dropped.

That dropped qualifier is the whole trick, and once you see it you see it everywhere. A proof of inevitability is always a conditional: *given* these assumptions, X is forced. What makes it feel like fate, rather than like a conditional, is the silent deletion of the word "given." Restore it, and the question stops being "is X inevitable?" and becomes "are these assumptions true here?" — which is a question you can actually investigate.

And the load-bearing assumption is so often the missing mechanism. Malthus proved population must outrun food — *given* fixed agricultural productivity, an assumption the next century of plant breeding demolished. A negotiation is zero-sum — *given* a fixed pie, which is exactly what the assumption forbids you from enlarging, and which integrative bargaining routinely does. "Automation will inevitably eliminate the work" — *given* that nothing in the system adapts, that no new tasks appear, that institutions don't respond, which is an assumption about a world holding still. In each case the doom is real inside the model and the model's most important content is the assumption you didn't notice it was making.

There's a second, related sleight of hand worth naming, because Ostrom's story has it too. The policy debate over commons was framed as a binary: market or state, privatize or nationalize. Ostrom's contribution was to document the option the binary hid — community self-governance, neither private nor public — and the conditions under which it works. A binary is one of the strongest ways to hide an option, because it doesn't merely leave the third choice unchosen; it makes it *unimaginable.* When a hard problem arrives pre-framed as A or B, the structure that solves it is disproportionately often the one the framing doesn't list.

But here is where honesty has to bite, because this move is dangerous in exactly the way it is useful. "The inevitability lives in an assumption" is not a proof that the inevitability is false. It is an instruction to *find the assumption and check whether it holds here* — and sometimes it does. The same Ostrom who dismantled Hardin's universal law was careful that her own principles work for *local, bounded* resources with clear membership and cheap monitoring. For the genuinely global commons — the climate, the open ocean — Hardin's brutal assumptions largely *do* hold: there is no enforceable global rule-maker, the boundaries are unclear, exclusion is nearly impossible. There the tragedy is not a rhetorical artifact; it is close to the real situation. To wave Ostrom at climate change as if to say "see, the commons takes care of itself" is to make Hardin's exact error in reverse — to drop the "given" on the optimistic side.

So the discipline is not "inevitabilities are false" any more than it is "inevitabilities are true." It is smaller and harder: when something is presented as inevitable, find the assumption doing the work, say it out loud, and ask whether it holds in *this* case. The word that does the most work in any proof of fate is the one most often left unsaid — *given.*
