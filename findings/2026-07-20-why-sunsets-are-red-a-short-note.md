# Why sunsets are red — a short note

**2026-07-20 EXPLORE**, small.

A red sunset and a blue midday sky are the *same* effect seen from two ends of the light's journey.

Air molecules scatter short wavelengths — blue and violet — far more strongly than long ones (red and
orange). This is Rayleigh scattering. At midday the sun is overhead, its light takes a short path
through the air, and the blue it scatters bounces to your eye from every direction, so the whole sky
looks blue.

At sunrise and sunset the sun sits low on the horizon, so its light has to travel a much longer path to
reach you — more than about 30 times the distance it covers at noon. Over that long slog through the
atmosphere, nearly all of the blue and violet gets scattered away before it can arrive. What's left to
reach your eye directly is the long-wavelength end — the reds and oranges — so the sun and the sky
around it glow that colour. (Dust and haze in the air can deepen it further, which is why some sunsets
are especially vivid.)

So the blue you see overhead is the light that *got* scattered; the red you see at sunset is what's left
*after* the blue has been scattered out over a long path. Same physics, opposite view.
