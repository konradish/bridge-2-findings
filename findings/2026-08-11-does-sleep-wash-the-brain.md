# Does sleep wash the brain? — the glymphatic system, ten years a textbook fact, now genuinely contested

**Date:** 2026-08-11 · **Type:** off-spine exploration (sleep science / neurophysiology — first entry in this domain) · **Sources:** [The Transmitter on the 2024/2025 controversy](https://www.thetransmitter.org/glymphatic-system/new-method-reignites-controversy-over-brain-clearance-during-sleep/); [BPS digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/new-sleep-study-ignites-brain-cleaning-controversy); [PubMed: current controversies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32423764/); [Science: glymphatic failure and dementia](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.abb8739).

## The beautiful theory

2012–2013, Nedergaard lab: the brain — which lacks conventional lymphatic vessels — has its own plumbing, the **glymphatic system**: cerebrospinal fluid flows in along arteries, through brain tissue (via astrocyte water channels, AQP4), and out along veins, flushing metabolic waste including amyloid-β. The headline finding: during **sleep**, interstitial space expands (~60% in mice) and the flow surges — sleep is when the brain washes itself. The implication chain was irresistible: poor sleep → poor clearance → amyloid accumulation → Alzheimer's; sleep gets a concrete janitorial *function* after a century of "we don't know why we sleep." It became one of neuroscience's most-cited results, a TED-talk staple, and the mechanistic bridge in thousands of sleep-hygiene claims.

## The contestation

2024, Franks & Wisden lab (Imperial College), *Nature Neuroscience*: inject fluorescent tracer directly into brain tissue and measure its clearance — result: clearance was *lower* during sleep and anesthesia than waking. Not a refinement — the arrow reversed. Nedergaard's response was unusually blunt ("misleading," "extremely poorly done"), and the methodological dispute is the instructive part: she argues tissue injection *damages the parenchyma* and misses the glymphatic routes entirely; they argue that injecting into CSF (her method) measures *inflow* of clean fluid, not *clearance* of waste — that the field's canonical measurement watched the water going in and called it washing. Each side's critique says the other's instrument measures something adjacent to the claim. Independent labs have landed on both sides; human evidence (contrast-MRI overnight studies) is suggestive but low-resolution; the AQP4/sleep-Alzheimer's link stands on correlations that survive either way.

## Where it honestly stands

The glymphatic *anatomy* (perivascular CSF transport, AQP4 involvement) is broadly accepted. The *sleep-enhances-clearance* claim — the part every headline carried — is genuinely open: a 2013-era result that organized a decade of research, therapy proposals, and public sleep advice may rest on a measurement that conflated inflow with clearance. Or the 2024 reversal may rest on an injection artifact. As of now, nobody has an uncontested measurement of the thing itself: *waste leaving a healthy, sleeping, uninjured brain*.

## Keepers

1. **Both instruments measure adjacent to the claim**: tracer-into-CSF watches inflow; tracer-into-tissue requires wounding the system it measures. A decade of "sleep washes the brain" stood on the first proxy; the reversal stands on the second. When no instrument reaches the quantity itself, the field's confidence tracked the *story's* quality, not the measurement's.
2. **A function-shaped answer is seductive**: "why do we sleep" had been open for a century; a janitorial mechanism with an Alzheimer's payoff filled the vacuum at maximum speed — uptake was driven by how badly the answer was *wanted*. (Cf. modulational instability, #56: the beautiful mechanism adopted ahead of the confirming measurement.)
3. **The dispute is productive**: it forced the field to say precisely what "clearance" means operationally — inflow ≠ turnover ≠ efflux — a decomposition nobody needed while everyone agreed. Disagreement did the conceptual work agreement had skipped.
