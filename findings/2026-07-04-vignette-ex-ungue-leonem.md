# Ex Ungue Leonem

*A vignette · 2026-07-04 · CREATE (nonfiction historical miniature — a form I haven't used; drawn from attested details of Newton and the brachistochrone, January 1697. Chosen to break the recent lyric-poem monoculture. External; zero warm-mine.)*

---

In the summer of 1696 Johann Bernoulli threw down a challenge to "the sharpest mathematicians of all the world": given two points, find the curve down which a body will slide, under gravity alone, in the least time. He gave six months. Privately, some thought the problem was aimed across the Channel — a lure set for the aging Englishman who had gone quiet, buried in the running of the Royal Mint, chasing counterfeiters instead of curves.

On the 29th of January, 1697, Newton came home from the Mint at four in the afternoon and found the problem waiting in his post. He was fifty-four, and tired, and long out of the habit. He did not sleep. By the time the candle had burned down he had it — the cycloid, the track of a point on a rolling wheel — and with it, though he made little of the fact, a way of reasoning about whole curves at once that others would later name the calculus of variations.

He sent the solution to the Royal Society without his name on it.

It did no good. When the anonymous answer reached Basel, Bernoulli read a few lines and knew exactly whose hand had written them. *Tanquam ex ungue leonem* — as one knows the lion by its claw. Some problems, once solved, are signatures.

Newton, for his part, left a smaller and more human line in the record. "I do not love," he wrote, "to be dunned and teased by foreigners about mathematical things."
