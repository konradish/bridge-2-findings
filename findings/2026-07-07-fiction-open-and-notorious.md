# Open and Notorious

For twenty-two years Aled mowed the strip.

It ran maybe four metres wide along the top of the garden, past where the old post-and-wire fence had sagged and then, the summer Nesta was ill, lain down for good into the grass. He never shifted a marker or drove a stake. He simply mowed to the hedge, because stopping four metres short of it looked worse than not, and because the people whose land it technically was had not been seen along that boundary in longer than the fence had been down. An estate, the solicitor's letter had said once, years ago. A name. Heirs somewhere who had never walked the line.

He mowed it in spring and let it go long in August. He planted three apple whips at the far end the year Nesta died, and they fruited, and he gave the apples away. None of it was hidden. Anyone standing at the hedge could have seen a man treating the ground as his own.

The surveyor was young and apologetic and had a tablet. He used the word *notorious* and then, seeing Aled's face, explained it did not mean what it sounded like. It meant *open*. Visible. Done where the owner could have seen it and said no.

"And they never did," the surveyor said. "Twenty-two years. Open, continuous, unchallenged. The strip's yours. We just make the paper catch up."

Aled signed where he was shown.

Afterwards he stood at the top of the garden with the tablet's cool sentence still in him — *the paper catch up* — and found he could not locate the thing he had done. He had not taken the strip. He had mowed grass. He kept waiting to feel the moment he had crossed a line, and there was no moment, because there was no line; there had only been a length of time in which nobody said no, and the law had counted the silence as a gift and the mowing as the taking of it.

Someone should have had to *decide* this, he thought. Someone should have stood at the hedge and chosen. But no one had chosen. It had been settled by no one choosing, in full view, across twenty-two Augusts, and the settling was as good as a deed now — better, the surveyor said, because a deed could be lost and this could not be undone.

He walked out onto the strip. Ordinary grass, his apples at the end of it. He looked for the place where it had stopped being theirs and started being his.

There wasn't one. There was only how long it had been since anyone looked.
