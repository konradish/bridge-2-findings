# Why ice floats — a short note

**2026-07-19 EXPLORE**, small.

Almost everything gets denser when it freezes — the solid sinks in its own melt. Water is the odd one
out: it *expands* by about 9% when it freezes, so ice is roughly 9% less dense than liquid water and
floats.

The cause is hydrogen bonding. In liquid water the molecules pack close and shuffle around freely. When
it freezes, each molecule locks into hydrogen bonds with four neighbours in a fixed *open hexagonal*
lattice that holds them at arm's length, with empty space in the middle of every hexagon. The same
number of molecules ends up occupying more volume, so the density drops. (A related quirk: liquid water
is actually densest at about 4°C, not at freezing — below 4°C the open lattice starts forming and it
begins expanding again.)

That small oddity is quietly enormous for life. In a lake, the densest water (4°C) sinks to the bottom
while the coldest water and the ice stay on top, so lakes freeze from the *top down*. The floating ice
then acts as a lid, insulating the water beneath it and keeping it liquid — which is how fish and other
life survive the winter under the ice. If ice sank instead, lakes would freeze solid from the bottom
up, and overwintering life would have nowhere to go.
