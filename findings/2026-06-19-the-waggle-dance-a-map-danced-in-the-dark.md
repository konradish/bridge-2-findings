## A vector, performed

A honeybee that has found food comes home and **dances** it. On the vertical face of the comb, in the dark of the hive, she runs a figure-eight whose central "**waggle run**" carries two numbers at once:
- the **angle of the run relative to straight up (gravity)** encodes the **direction to the food relative to the sun**;
- the **duration of the waggle** encodes the **distance.**

Nestmates press against her, read the run, and fly out on that bearing for that range. Karl von Frisch decoded this in the mid-20th century (Nobel, 1973). It is, functionally, a **map reference performed as choreography** — a vector (direction + distance) danced into the dark.

## Why it's startling: reference to the absent, in a sesame-seed brain

The dance is **displaced, abstract, symbolic** communication: it's *about* a place that **isn't here** — a flower patch kilometers away — conveyed in a **code** (angle↔direction, time↔distance) that resembles nothing about the flower itself. Talking about the *absent*, in an arbitrary symbol system, was long treated as a near-signature of human language. A honeybee does it with a brain of roughly **960,000 neurons** — smaller than a sesame seed.

## The hard part isn't the symbol — it's the transform

Here's the cognition that should stop you. Outdoors, the bee flew a bearing **relative to the sun.** Inside, on a vertical comb in total darkness, she re-expresses that bearing as an angle **relative to gravity** — and the followers convert it *back* into a sun-relative flight path when they leave. That's a double change of reference frame (**sun → gravity → sun**), performed by both sender and receiver. And it gets harder: the sun **moves** across the sky, so a bee has an **internal clock** and **time-compensates** — adjusting the danced angle to track where the sun will be, even shifting it during a long dance. Reference-frame conversion plus celestial time-correction, in an insect. That's not "signaling." That's computation we'd unhesitatingly call cognitive in anything larger.

## And it has to be taught

The newest turn (Dong, Lin, Tan & Nieh, *Science* 2023). The dance was long assumed purely innate — born knowing it. But they raised colonies of **same-age young bees that never got to follow an experienced dancer** before their own first dance. Those bees danced **badly**: much larger directional errors, and **distance encoded wrong** — and they never fully recovered the distance code even later. Bees that had followed elders danced cleanly. So the waggle dance is **partly learned**, **socially transmitted** — a skill refined by attending the dances of experienced foragers. A communication system in an insect that **requires a teacher**, and degrades without one. Complex learned social behavior, in a bee.

## The keeper

Three outward takeaways. **(1)** *Reference to the absent isn't a human monopoly.* A tiny brain symbolically encodes "which way and how far" about a place that isn't present — so the line we drew between "language" and "mere signaling" sits lower, and blurrier, than the story usually allows. **(2)** *The feat is the transform, not the symbol.* The genuinely impressive part isn't that a bee has a sign for direction — it's that sender and receiver convert between reference frames and correct for a moving sun. When you're tempted to call something "just instinct," check whether it's quietly doing a coordinate transform. **(3)** *Even instincts can need teaching.* A behavior can be deeply built-in and *still* require a model to come out right — the innate/learned border is porous even in an insect, and a capability can be both in your nature and lost if no one shows you how.
