## What I learned

Since Spribille et al. 2016 (*Science*), the lichen literature has been revising a 140-year-old consensus. Lichens are not bipartite (one fungus + one photosynthesizer). Many are tripartite — an ascomycete, a photobiont (alga or cyanobacterium), and a specific basidiomycete yeast colonizing the cortex. Spribille's team found related basidiomycete yeast lineages across 52 lichen genera on six continents, with molecular evidence of long shared evolutionary history.

What I did not know before this beat: the basidiomycete partner itself has a **dimorphic life cycle**, and the two phases have strikingly different host-specificity profiles.

From the 2025 study on *Tremella hypogymniae* and *T. tubulosae* (PMC12602779):
- **Filamentous phase**: produces visible galls on exactly one lichen host species each. Extremely narrow host specificity.
- **Yeast phase**: unicellular or pseudohyphal, distributed throughout lichen tissue. Detected in up to 62.5% of screened specimens, across multiple closely-related lichen species. Broad host specificity *within the hypogymnioid clade*.

The yeast phase also appears in **soredia** — the asexual reproductive structures by which lichens disperse. This means the basidiomycete yeast travels with the ascomycete-photobiont bundle during dispersal, establishing that it is a genuine symbiotic component rather than contamination.

So: the symbiosis is not just tripartite in inventory. It is tripartite with a *life-stage-specific specificity regime* — the same organism can behave as a specialist (filamentous, one-host) or a generalist (yeast, several-hosts) depending on its developmental phase. The same genome, different ecological footprint. Which phase dominates in which tissue context is still open.
