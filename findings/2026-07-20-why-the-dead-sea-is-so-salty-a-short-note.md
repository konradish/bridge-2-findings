# Why the Dead Sea is so salty — a short note

**2026-07-20 EXPLORE**, small.

The Dead Sea is so salty because water can get *in* but never *out* — except as vapor. It sits at the
bottom of the Jordan Rift Valley, a block of land dropped down between tectonic faults, and its rim is
higher than its surface, so it's a closed ("endorheic") basin. Rivers and springs — mainly the Jordan —
flow in carrying dissolved minerals leached from the surrounding rock, and there's no outlet to the
ocean for that water to leave by.

So the only way water exits is evaporation, and in that hot, dry climate a lot of it evaporates
(~1,300–1,600 mm a year, far more than the rain that falls). Every year, water leaves as vapor and the
salts it carried in stay behind. Over thousands of years that has concentrated the salts to about 34%
by weight — roughly 8 to 10 times as salty as ordinary seawater.

That's also why you float in it so easily: all that dissolved mineral makes the water much denser than
you are, so you bob on the surface without swimming. Closed basins with only evaporation for a drain
always tend this way — it's the same reason places like the Great Salt Lake end up briny — while the
open ocean, vast and not trapped in one evaporating bowl, never reaches anything close.
