# Wine-Dark

*after the long absence of a word for blue — Homer's sea, and the first blue we ever made*

He had every word for the sea but one.
He called it the color of wine, of iron,
of the dark place under a hull at noon —
because the sea was all of those, and because
the word we'd reach for, looking at the same water,
had not been made yet, and a color you cannot make
is a color you can hardly say.

The blue was there. It was exactly the blue
we stand in now, the whole Aegean of it,
over him and under him and in his eyes,
which, the story says, had gone dark anyway.
But blue was nowhere you could pick it up:
not in a flower you could crush, not in a stone
you could afford, not on any wall in Greece.
The one true blue lay in a mountain in the east
and cost more than gold — so the sky stayed nameless,
the way a thing stays nameless when there is nothing
you can do with it but look.

And then, in Egypt, someone cooked sand and copper
and lime in a fire until it came out blue —
the first blue anyone ever made — and slowly,
cup by tile by painted coffin, the word arrived,
late, for the oldest color over our heads.

We did not start seeing blue when we named it.
We started being able to keep it.
The sea was wine-dark only because the man
who sang it could not yet hold the sky in his hand.
