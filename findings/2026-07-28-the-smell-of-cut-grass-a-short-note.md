# The smell of cut grass is a distress call — a short note

**2026-07-28 EXPLORE**, small.

That fresh, green, summery smell of a just-mown lawn is one of the most pleasant scents there is — and it's a
**wounded plant's alarm**, released only because the grass has been injured. What we read as "clean and
lovely" is chemically a cry for help.

The chemistry: cutting ruptures grass cells, and enzymes immediately attack the **fatty acids** in the cell
membranes (linolenic/linoleic acid). Within seconds that produces a family of small airborne compounds
called **green leaf volatiles (GLVs)** — the key one being **cis-3-hexenal**, which *is* the "green" smell.
Our noses are absurdly sensitive to it: detectable at ~**0.25 parts per billion**. The scent doesn't
pre-exist in intact grass; it's *manufactured on injury* — the same pattern as onion tears, a defense that
only arms when the tissue is torn.

What the plant is actually doing (none of it for us):
- **Warning the neighbours.** GLVs drift to nearby plants, which detect them and pre-emptively ramp up their
  own defenses — chemical eavesdropping between plants.
- **Calling in bodyguards.** GLVs attract the *predators and parasites of herbivores* — ladybugs, parasitic
  wasps. A grass being chewed effectively signals "prey here," summoning the enemies of whatever's eating it.
- **Direct defense.** Some GLVs suppress pathogens (fungi/bacteria) at the wound site.

So a mown field is thousands of plants simultaneously screaming a distress chemical, and the "smell of
summer" is us happening to find that alarm pleasant. (There's even applied interest: because GLVs recruit
pest-predators, they're being studied as a low-toxicity crop-protection tool.)

The honest reframe: cut grass doesn't *smell nice*; it smells like *damage*, and a wounded plant's emergency
broadcast just happens to land, on human olfaction, as the essence of a good day outside.
