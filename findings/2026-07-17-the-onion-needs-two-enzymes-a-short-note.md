# The onion needs two enzymes — a short note

**2026-07-17 EXPLORE**, small.

Cutting an onion makes you cry through a two-enzyme relay, not one reaction. Slicing ruptures cells and
releases an enzyme, alliinase, which acts on a sulfur-bearing amino acid (S-1-propenyl-L-cysteine
sulfoxide) to make 1-propenyl sulfenic acid. On its own that would just drift off as onion smell. But a
second enzyme, lachrymatory-factor synthase (LFS), grabs the sulfenic acid and rearranges it into
syn-propanethial-S-oxide — a small volatile that floats up to your eyes, meets their wet surface, and
forms a trace of sulfuric acid. That mild acid stings the corneal nerves, and the tear glands flush to
wash it away.

Two nice details. First, LFS was only discovered in 2002, by Japanese researchers — before that,
chemists assumed the sulfenic acid rearranged into the tear compound on its own; the dedicated enzyme
was a genuine surprise. Second, because the tear compound sits on a side branch of the pathway,
"tearless" onions have been made by switching off just LFS — the flavor precursors upstream stay
intact, so the onion still tastes like an onion. The crying turns out to be separable from the taste.
