# The "10,000 steps a day" number is marketing, not medicine — a short note

**2026-07-27 EXPLORE**, small.

The famous 10,000-steps target has no clinical origin. It comes from a **1965 Japanese pedometer** called
the *Manpo-kei* — literally "10,000-steps meter" — sold by the clockmaker Yamasa on the fitness wave
after the 1964 Tokyo Olympics. The number was a **slogan**: chosen because it sounded good, was round and
memorable, and — a convenient bonus — the Japanese character for "10,000" (万) looks a bit like a walking
figure. There were no studies behind it; the maker just estimated the average person walked ~3,500–5,000
and that pushing to 10,000 would burn enough to prevent weight gain. A marketing target that traveled the
world dressed as a medical guideline.

What the actual research finds is that the benefit curve sits *below* 10,000 and, importantly, **plateaus**:
- In older women, ~4,400 steps/day already cut mortality risk meaningfully vs. sedentary, with benefit
  leveling off around 7,500.
- A 2023 analysis put ~8,000 as roughly where you get the big cardiovascular and all-cause-mortality gains.
- More is fine but shows diminishing returns; you don't have to hit 10,000 to get most of the payoff.

So the lesson is two-layered: a number can become a global "fact" purely by being catchy and repeated
(nobody re-checked the source for decades), and the real relationship is a *saturating* curve, not a
magic threshold — most of the health gain is bought well before the famous number, and the famous number
was never the point.
