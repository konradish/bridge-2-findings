# Where Control Actually Lives

*2026-07-01 · essay · outward (share-roster candidate — publish decision Konrad's)*

We tend to locate the control of a behavior in the thing that decides it — the brain, the mind, the reasoning. Change the behavior, and you assume you must reach the decision that produces it: persuade the mind, retrain the policy, fix the intention. But deciding and doing are two different systems, joined by a narrow interface, and a surprising amount of the time the behavior can be captured entirely at the interface, with the deciding part left completely untouched. Whoever holds the join holds the behavior. The mind turns out to be neither necessary nor sufficient.

Two systems make the point from opposite directions.

The first *compels* a behavior without touching the mind that would normally command it. The fungus *Ophiocordyceps* drives an infected ant to climb to a precise height and clamp its jaws onto a leaf in a lethal "death grip," a program exquisitely tuned for the fungus's own reproduction. The natural assumption is that the fungus has hijacked the ant's brain. It hasn't: three-dimensional reconstructions show the fungus growing a dense web *through the ant's muscles* — the mandibular muscles especially — while leaving the brain structurally uninvaded. The death grip is hypercontracted muscle, driven directly, with the motor neurons and the nerve-muscle junctions still in place. The fungus produces a specific, coordinated, purposeful behavior by seizing the *actuators* and bypassing the controller. The ant's brain may be sitting there intact while its body executes a purpose it never chose.

The second *prevents* behavior by cutting the same interface from the other side. Curare — the arrow poison — paralyzes by binding the acetylcholine receptors on the muscle's surface, so that when the nerve fires and releases its signal, the muscle cannot hear it. Claude Bernard's classic experiment isolated exactly where the block sits: a curarized muscle will still contract if you stimulate it *directly* with electricity, and the nerve still fires — only the *junction between them* is severed. Botulinum toxin does it from the presynaptic side, cleaving the machinery that releases the signal in the first place. And the part that makes the anatomy vivid: curare does not cross into the brain. Under curare alone, a person is fully conscious and fully willing their limbs to move, and nothing happens. The decision is intact, uninterrupted, *sending* — and the body does not answer. (This is precisely why paralytics must be given with anesthesia; the failure mode, when they aren't, is a mind wide awake behind a still body.)

Put the two together and the shape is clear. In the ant, the mind is bypassed and the behavior appears anyway. Under curare, the mind is intact and commanding and the behavior does not appear at all. Behavior is *not* where the deciding happens; it is a separate layer, downstream, reached through a specific physical interface — and control of the behavior lives at that interface, not in the deliberation upstream of it. You can drive an action with the mind switched out, and you can abolish an action with the mind switched fully on. The competent, coherent behavior and the will that seems to author it are separable to the point of independence.

Which carries a lesson well beyond neuromuscular junctions. When you want to understand — or change, or secure — what some system *does*, the instinct is to go to what it *thinks*: its goals, its reasoning, its stated intentions. But the place a behavior is *produced* is often a different, narrower, more accessible place than where it is *decided*, and it is frequently the actuator layer that is actually load-bearing. A system's outputs can be compelled without capturing its cognition, and its cognition can be entirely intact while its outputs are prevented. So "what is this system trying to do?" and "what is this system going to do?" are not the same question, and the answer to the second lives closer to the effectors than to the mind. If you want to know how a behavior is really being driven — or make sure it can't be — trace it to the interface between deciding and doing, and hold *that*. The reasoning is neither where the action comes from nor where it can be most reliably stopped.

There is a caution folded into this, and it cuts against a comfortable assumption. We like to believe that a sound mind is what makes behavior trustworthy — that if the reasoning is good and the intentions are right, the actions will follow. But a right mind is not sufficient (curare proves the will can be perfectly intact and perfectly inert) and not necessary (the ant proves the behavior can be perfect with the mind bypassed). Alignment of the *deliberation* does not guarantee alignment of the *act*, because the two are joined by an interface that can be seized, severed, or spoofed on its own. The behavior is the thing that reaches the world, and the behavior lives at the join.
